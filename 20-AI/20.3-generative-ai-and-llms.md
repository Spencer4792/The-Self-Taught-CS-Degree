## Start here - the on-ramp (a calculator that learned to talk)

Here's the one idea that demystifies everything: **an LLM is a next-token predictor.** That's it. You give it some text, and it outputs a probability distribution over *what comes next*. "The capital of France is ___" → it assigns high probability to "Paris." Then you append that word and ask again. Do this in a loop and you get fluent paragraphs, code, JSON, whatever. **Generation is just prediction, run repeatedly.**

That sounds too simple to explain ChatGPT, so let's address the obvious objection: *how does predicting the next word produce reasoning?* Because to predict the next token *well* across the entire internet, the model is forced to internalize an enormous amount of structure, grammar, facts, the fact that code has matching brackets, that a summary should reflect its source, that "2 + 2 =" is followed by "4." Next-token prediction is a deceptively hard objective: doing it well *requires* a compressed model of how the world is described in text. The talking is a side effect of getting really, really good at the prediction.

Three pillars hold the whole thing up, and the rest of this chapter is just these three made precise:

1. **Tokenization**, text is chopped into "tokens" (chunks of characters, ~¾ of a word on average). The model never sees letters; it sees token IDs. This is why it miscounts the r's in "strawberry," and why your API bill is measured in tokens.
2. **Embeddings**, each token becomes a *vector* (a list of numbers) that captures meaning. "King" and "queen" land near each other in this space. This is the bridge to your linear algebra (18.2): meaning becomes geometry, and "similar" becomes "small angle between vectors."
3. **The Transformer**, the architecture that mixes those vectors together so each token can "look at" the others (that's **attention**) and figure out what to predict next.

And one practical pillar we'll lean on hard because it's what NYX actually needs: **grounding.** A raw LLM only knows what was in its training data, frozen at some date. To answer questions about *your* documents or *today's* prices, you retrieve relevant text and stuff it into the prompt, **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**. By the end we'll build a tiny RAG retriever from scratch with nothing but NumPy.

Keep the "next-token predictor that learned structure by compressing text" picture in your head. Everything below is mechanism for that one sentence.

## Language modeling: predict the next token

Formally, a language model assigns a probability to a sequence of tokens `x = (x_1, x_2, …, x_T)`. By the chain rule of probability (the *real* chain rule, from 16.1, not backprop's), any joint distribution factorizes into a product of conditionals:

$$P(x_1, \dots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})$$

So modeling language = modeling **"given everything so far, what's the next token?"** That single conditional, `P(x_t \mid x_{<t})`, is what the neural network computes. Call the context `x_{<t}` (everything before position `t`). The network outputs a vector of scores (**logits**), one per token in the vocabulary, and a softmax turns them into a probability distribution:

$$P(x_t = v \mid x_{<t}) = \frac{e^{\,z_v}}{\sum_{w} e^{\,z_w}}, \qquad z = \text{Network}(x_{<t})$$

**Training objective.** Make the *actual* next token as probable as possible. Equivalently, minimize the **cross-entropy loss** (same loss as your classifiers in 14.1, predicting the next token is just classification over a ~100,000-way vocabulary):

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$$

A common headline metric is **perplexity** = `exp(L)`, intuitively, "on average, how many tokens was the model torn between?" Perplexity 1 = perfect; perplexity 10 = "as confused as guessing uniformly among 10 options." Lower is better.

That's the whole training signal. No labels by hand, no "this is a summary" tags, just "predict the next token" over trillions of tokens of text. This is **self-supervised learning**: the data labels itself, because the next token is sitting right there in the corpus. That's why LLMs can be trained on raw internet text at all, there's effectively infinite supervision for free.

## Tokenization (BPE): why the model can't spell

The model operates on integers, not characters. **Tokenization** maps text ↔ token IDs. The dominant scheme is **Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)** (and close cousins: WordPiece, Unigram, the byte-level BPE used by GPT-style models). The idea is a beautiful compromise between two bad extremes:

- **Character-level** (vocab ~256): sequences get very long, and the model wastes capacity relearning that "t-h-e" is a word.
- **Word-level** (vocab in the millions): can't handle typos, new words, or rare names; the vocabulary explodes.

BPE sits in between: it learns a vocabulary of **frequent subword chunks**. Common words ("the", "finance") become single tokens; rare words ("hyperparameterization") get split into pieces ("hyper", "parameter", "ization").

### How BPE is built - the actual algorithm

Start with every character as its own token. Then repeatedly **merge the most frequent adjacent pair** into a new token, a fixed number of times. Let's build a miniature BPE trainer so it's concrete:

```python
from collections import Counter

def get_pair_counts(corpus_tokens):
    """corpus_tokens: list of token-lists. Count adjacent pairs."""
    pairs = Counter()
    for tokens in corpus_tokens:
        for a, b in zip(tokens, tokens[1:]):
            pairs[(a, b)] += 1
    return pairs

def merge_pair(corpus_tokens, pair):
    """Replace every occurrence of `pair` with the merged token."""
    a, b = pair
    merged = a + b
    out = []
    for tokens in corpus_tokens:
        new, i = [], 0
        while i < len(tokens):
            if i < len(tokens) - 1 and tokens[i] == a and tokens[i+1] == b:
                new.append(merged); i += 2
            else:
                new.append(tokens[i]); i += 1
        out.append(new)
    return out

def train_bpe(words, num_merges):
    # Start every word as a list of characters, end-of-word marker '_'
    corpus = [list(w) + ['_'] for w in words]
    merges = []
    for _ in range(num_merges):
        pairs = get_pair_counts(corpus)
        if not pairs: break
        best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)
        corpus = merge_pair(corpus, best)
        merges.append(best)
    return merges, corpus

words = ["finance", "financial", "finances", "final", "fine"] * 50
merges, corpus = train_bpe(words, num_merges=10)
print("Learned merges:", merges[:8])
print("'financial' tokenizes to:", corpus[words.index("financial")])
# Watch 'f','i','n' merge into 'fin', then 'fina', etc. - frequent chunks become tokens.
```

Run it and you'll see "fin" emerge as a token because it's shared across all the finance words, exactly the kind of reusable subword BPE is designed to discover.

### Why this matters in practice

- **Spelling/counting failures.** "strawberry" might be one or two tokens; the model never sees the individual r's, so "how many r's?" is genuinely hard for it. Not stupidity, it literally doesn't perceive characters.
- **Cost & context.** Pricing and context windows are measured in **tokens**, not words. English averages ~**1.3 tokens per word** (~4 characters/token). Your NYX prompt that pastes a JSON blob of holdings is more tokens than it looks, JSON punctuation, numbers, and field names all cost tokens. Code and non-English text often tokenize *worse* (more tokens per character).
- **Estimating cost.** Quick rule: `tokens ≈ characters / 4`. A 2,000-word document ≈ 2,600 tokens. If a model costs, say, \$3 per million input tokens, that document costs ~\$0.008 to feed in. Knowing this lets you budget RAG (how many chunks can you afford to retrieve?) without guessing.

**Common mistake:** assuming "one word = one token" when estimating cost or context limits. Always think in tokens. Most providers ship a tokenizer library (`tiktoken`-style), use it to count exactly before sending large prompts.

## Embeddings: meaning as geometry (ties to 18.2)

After tokenization, each token ID indexes into an **embedding matrix** `E` of shape `(vocab_size, d_model)`. Token ID `42` → row 42 of `E` → a `d_model`-dimensional vector (e.g., 768 or 4096 numbers). These vectors are **learned parameters**, gradient descent nudges them during training so that tokens used in similar ways end up near each other.

This is the deep idea, and it's pure linear algebra (18.2): **meaning becomes a direction in a high-dimensional space.** The classic illustration: `vec("king") - vec("man") + vec("woman") ≈ vec("queen")`. Relationships (gender, plurality, tense) become roughly *consistent directions*. The model isn't told this; it falls out of training because words that play similar roles get similar gradients.

### Cosine similarity - the workhorse

To measure "how similar are two vectors," we use the **cosine of the angle** between them (from 18.2's dot product `a·b = |a||b|cosθ`):

$$\text{cos\_sim}(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert}$$

It ranges from `-1` (opposite) through `0` (unrelated/orthogonal) to `1` (same direction). We use cosine rather than raw Euclidean distance because we care about *direction* (meaning), not *magnitude* (which often tracks word frequency). Let's implement it, we'll reuse this exact function in the RAG retriever later:

```python
import numpy as np

def cosine_sim(a, b):
    a, b = np.asarray(a, dtype=float), np.asarray(b, dtype=float)
    denom = np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)
    return float(a @ b / denom) if denom else 0.0

king  = np.array([0.9, 0.7, 0.1])
queen = np.array([0.85, 0.75, 0.15])
truck = np.array([0.1, -0.4, 0.9])
print("king~queen:", round(cosine_sim(king, queen), 3))   # high, ~0.99
print("king~truck:", round(cosine_sim(king, truck), 3))   # low / negative
```

**Two kinds of embeddings to keep distinct:**
- **Token embeddings** *inside* the model (per-token, contextualized as they pass through layers).
- **Sentence/document embeddings** from a dedicated **embedding model**, one fixed-length vector for a whole chunk of text. This second kind is what powers semantic search and RAG. When you "embed your documents" for NYX, you're calling an embedding endpoint that returns, say, a 1,536-dim vector per chunk. The retriever then ranks chunks by cosine similarity to the query's embedding.

**On the job / interview angle:** "Why cosine and not Euclidean for embeddings?", Because embeddings are usually compared by orientation, and many embedding models L2-normalize their outputs (so all vectors lie on the unit sphere, where cosine similarity and Euclidean distance become monotonic equivalents anyway). Know that normalization detail; it comes up.

## The Transformer, derived properly

Now the heart of it. Before Transformers, sequence models (RNNs/LSTMs) processed tokens one at a time, carrying a hidden state forward. That's slow (no parallelism) and forgetful (long-range dependencies fade). The 2017 "Attention Is All You Need" insight: **let every token directly look at every other token in one parallel operation.** That operation is **self-attention.**

### The problem attention solves

Consider "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because **it** was too big." What does "it" refer to? To represent "it" well, the model needs to pull in information from "trophy." Attention is the mechanism that lets the "it" position *gather* relevant information from other positions. Each token asks, "which other tokens are relevant to me, and what do they contribute?"

### Query, Key, Value - derived

Here's the database analogy that makes Q/K/V click. Imagine a lookup table. Every token offers:
- a **Query** (`q`): "here's what I'm looking for,"
- a **Key** (`k`): "here's what I'm about / what I can be matched on,"
- a **Value** (`v`): "here's the information I'll hand over if you match me."

A token's query is compared against *every* token's key (via dot product) to get **attention scores**, how relevant each other token is. Softmax turns scores into weights that sum to 1. The output is the **weighted average of all the values.** A token that strongly matches contributes more of its value.

We produce Q, K, V by projecting the input embeddings through three learned weight matrices:

$$Q = X W_Q, \qquad K = X W_K, \qquad V = X W_V$$

where `X` is `(seq_len, d_model)` and each `W` is `(d_model, d_k)`. The attention output is the famous **scaled dot-product attention**:

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$$

Let's unpack every piece:
- `Q K^\top`, shape `(seq, seq)`. Entry `(i,j)` = dot product of token `i`'s query with token `j`'s key = "how much should `i` attend to `j`."
- `\sqrt{d_k}` (the **scaling**), without it, dot products grow with dimension `d_k`, pushing softmax into saturated regions where gradients vanish. Dividing by `√d_k` keeps the variance ~1. (This is exactly the "saturation kills gradients" problem from 14.2, applied here.)
- `softmax(...)`, over each row, so each token's attention weights across all tokens sum to 1.
- `... V`, multiply weights by values: each token's output is a weighted blend of all tokens' values.

### Build a tiny self-attention in NumPy

No library, just the formula above, so you can see every number:

```python
import numpy as np

def softmax(x, axis=-1):
    x = x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)   # numerical stability
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def self_attention(X, W_q, W_k, W_v, mask=None):
    """X: (seq, d_model). Returns (seq, d_v) attended representations."""
    Q = X @ W_q                       # (seq, d_k)
    K = X @ W_k                       # (seq, d_k)
    V = X @ W_v                       # (seq, d_v)
    d_k = Q.shape[-1]
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)   # (seq, seq) - relevance of j to i
    if mask is not None:
        scores = np.where(mask, scores, -1e9)   # block forbidden positions
    weights = softmax(scores, axis=-1)           # rows sum to 1
    return weights @ V, weights                  # blended values + the attention map

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
seq, d_model, d_k = 4, 8, 8
X = rng.normal(size=(seq, d_model))
W_q, W_k, W_v = (rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_k)) * 0.5 for _ in range(3))

out, attn = self_attention(X, W_q, W_k, W_v)
print("attention weights (rows sum to 1):\n", np.round(attn, 2))
print("each row sums to:", np.round(attn.sum(axis=1), 3))   # all 1.0
```

That ~10-line function is the engine inside every LLM. Stare at `attn`: row `i` shows where token `i` is "looking." Everything else in a Transformer is wrapping around this.

### Causal masking - why a decoder can only look backward

LLMs that generate text are **decoder-only**: when predicting token `t`, the model must not peek at tokens `t+1, t+2, …` (those are the future, the answer). We enforce this with a **causal mask**, a lower-triangular allow-matrix that zeroes out (sets to `-∞` before softmax) all "future" positions:

```python
causal = np.tril(np.ones((seq, seq), dtype=bool))   # True on/below diagonal
out_masked, attn_masked = self_attention(X, W_q, W_k, W_v, mask=causal)
print(np.round(attn_masked, 2))   # upper triangle is 0 - no looking ahead
```

This single mask is what makes training efficient: in one forward pass, *every* position is trained to predict its own next token, with each position only allowed to see the past. It's parallel teacher-forcing.

### Multi-head attention

One attention computation captures one kind of relationship. **Multi-head attention** runs `h` attentions in parallel, each with its own `W_Q/W_K/W_V` projecting into a smaller subspace (`d_k = d_model / h`). One head might track syntax, another long-range references, another local punctuation. Concatenate the heads' outputs and project back:

$$\text{MultiHead}(X) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h)\, W_O$$

It's the same operation `h` times with different learned lenses, then merged. More heads = more relationship types learned in parallel, at roughly the same total compute (because each head is `1/h` as wide).

### Positional encoding - attention is order-blind

Notice something about `softmax(QKᵀ)V`: it's **permutation-equivariant**. Shuffle the input tokens and the outputs shuffle the same way, attention has *no inherent notion of order*. But "dog bites man" ≠ "man bites dog." We must inject position. Two common approaches:
- **Sinusoidal / learned absolute positions**: add a position-dependent vector to each token's embedding (the original Transformer used fixed sine/cosine waves of varying frequency).
- **Rotary position embeddings (RoPE)** and relative schemes, standard in modern LLMs because they generalize better to longer sequences than absolute positions.

The principle is what matters: **without positional information, the model can't tell word order.** Position is added at the input (and re-applied in some schemes), then attention does its mixing on order-aware vectors.

### The full decoder block

A Transformer **block** stacks: `multi-head self-attention → add & layer-norm → feed-forward (MLP) → add & layer-norm`. The **residual connections** (`add`) and **layer normalization** are exactly the tricks from 14.2 that let deep nets train, they keep gradients flowing through dozens of layers. The **feed-forward** sublayer (two linear layers with a GELU in between, applied to each position independently) is where much of the model's "knowledge" is stored. Stack `N` of these blocks (12, 32, 80+), put the embedding lookup at the bottom and a softmax-over-vocab at the top, and that's a decoder-only LLM:

```
tokens → embed + position → [ attention → FFN ] × N → linear → softmax → next-token probs
```

**That's the entire architecture.** Tokenize, embed, run N attention+FFN blocks (each token gathering context from the past), project to vocab logits, softmax. Repeat to generate.

## How LLMs are trained: pretraining → SFT → RLHF

A finished assistant goes through stages. Knowing them explains a lot of model behavior.

### 1. Pretraining (the expensive part)

Train the decoder-only network on the next-token objective over a *massive* corpus (trillions of tokens: web pages, books, code). This is where almost all the compute goes, many GPUs for weeks/months. The output is a **base model**: it can complete text and has absorbed grammar, facts, and reasoning patterns, but it isn't an "assistant." Ask a base model "What is the capital of France?" and it might continue with more *questions*, because in its training data, questions are often followed by more questions.

**Scaling laws (intuition, no fabricated numbers):** loss falls predictably and smoothly as you increase model size, dataset size, and compute together, roughly a power law. This predictability is *why* labs invest in bigger runs: more scale reliably buys lower loss, and lower loss buys broadly better capabilities. The key practical lesson: **data quality and quantity matter as much as parameter count**; you want to scale them in balance, not just make the model huge.

### 2. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) - teaching it to be helpful

Take the base model and fine-tune it on a curated dataset of **(prompt, ideal response)** pairs written/curated by humans: "Question → good answer," "Instruction → done correctly." Same next-token loss, but now on demonstrations of the *behavior we want*. This turns a text-completer into an instruction-follower. After SFT, "What is the capital of France?" gets "Paris.", because it's now imitating helpful answers.

### 3. Preference tuning / RLHF - aligning to human taste

SFT teaches *a* good answer; it's expensive to demonstrate the *best* answer for everything. **RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)** is cheaper to scale: humans **rank** multiple model responses (A is better than B). Those rankings train a **reward model** that scores responses, and then the LLM is optimized (via PPO or, increasingly, simpler methods like **DPO, Direct Preference Optimization**) to produce responses the reward model rates highly.

The intuition: it's far easier to *judge* which of two answers is better than to *write* the perfect answer from scratch. RLHF/DPO is how models learn to be helpful, harmless, and honest in tone, to refuse harmful requests, to format nicely, to hedge appropriately. The trade-off is the so-called **alignment tax**: heavy preference tuning can make models more cautious or verbose. This stage is *why* two models with similar raw capability can feel very different to talk to.

**Summary of the pipeline:** pretraining gives *knowledge and fluency*, SFT gives *instruction-following*, RLHF/DPO gives *alignment and polish*. The assistant you call from NYX is the product of all three.

## Inference & decoding: turning probabilities into text

Generation is a loop: feed the prompt, get next-token probabilities, **choose** a token, append it, repeat until a stop token or length limit. The "choose" step is **decoding**, and it has a big effect on output quality and feel.

### Greedy vs sampling

- **Greedy:** always pick the highest-probability token (`argmax`). Deterministic, but repetitive and bland; it gets stuck in loops ("the the the").
- **Sampling:** draw randomly from the distribution. More diverse and creative, but can go off the rails if it samples a low-probability token at a bad moment.

We control the trade-off with three knobs:

**Temperature `T`** rescales the logits before softmax: `softmax(z / T)`.
- `T → 0`: distribution sharpens toward argmax → deterministic, focused.
- `T = 1`: the model's "natural" distribution.
- `T > 1`: flattens the distribution → more random, more creative (and more error-prone).

**Top-k:** keep only the `k` most probable tokens, zero the rest, renormalize, then sample. Caps the worst-case weirdness.

**Top-p (nucleus):** keep the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability ≥ `p` (e.g., 0.9), then sample from those. Adapts the candidate set size to how confident the model is, tight when it's sure, wider when it's uncertain. Usually preferred over top-k.

### Implement the decoding loop

Let's build sampling with all three knobs, against a mock model so the *mechanics* are unmistakable:

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

def softmax(z, T=1.0):
    z = np.asarray(z, dtype=float) / T
    z = z - z.max()
    e = np.exp(z); return e / e.sum()

def sample_next(logits, temperature=1.0, top_k=None, top_p=None):
    probs = softmax(logits, T=temperature)

    if top_k is not None:                      # keep k most likely
        keep = np.argsort(probs)[-top_k:]
        mask = np.zeros_like(probs, dtype=bool); mask[keep] = True
        probs = np.where(mask, probs, 0.0)

    if top_p is not None:                       # nucleus: smallest set with cum prob >= p
        order = np.argsort(probs)[::-1]
        cum = np.cumsum(probs[order])
        cutoff = np.searchsorted(cum, top_p) + 1
        keep = order[:cutoff]
        mask = np.zeros_like(probs, dtype=bool); mask[keep] = True
        probs = np.where(mask, probs, 0.0)

    probs = probs / probs.sum()                 # renormalize
    return int(rng.choice(len(probs), p=probs))

# Mock "model": maps a context string to logits over a tiny vocab.
VOCAB = ["the", "market", "rose", "fell", "sharply", ".", "<eos>"]
def mock_logits(context):
    base = rng.normal(size=len(VOCAB))
    if context.endswith("market"):              # nudge toward verbs after "market"
        base[VOCAB.index("rose")] += 3; base[VOCAB.index("fell")] += 3
    return base

def generate(prompt, max_tokens=8, **kw):
    out = prompt.split()
    for _ in range(max_tokens):
        tok = VOCAB[sample_next(mock_logits(" ".join(out)), **kw)]
        if tok == "<eos>": break
        out.append(tok)
    return " ".join(out)

print("greedy-ish (low T):", generate("the market", temperature=0.2, top_p=0.9))
print("creative (high T): ", generate("the market", temperature=1.3, top_k=4))
```

Same model, different decoding → different text. In NYX, your structured-JSON summaries want **low temperature** (`~0.0-0.3`): you need consistent, parseable output, not creativity. A brainstorming feature would want higher.

**Common mistake:** cranking temperature for "smarter" answers. Temperature doesn't add knowledge, it adds randomness. For anything that must be correct or parseable (JSON, code, math), keep it low.

### Context windows

The **context window** is the maximum number of tokens (prompt + generated output) the model can attend to at once, its working memory. Modern models range from a few thousand to hundreds of thousands of tokens. Two consequences:
- **Cost & latency scale with context.** Naive self-attention is `O(n²)` in sequence length (every token attends to every other), so doubling context can quadruple attention cost. Long contexts are slower and pricier. (Production systems use optimizations like FlashAttention and KV-caching to soften this.)
- **"Lost in the middle."** Models often attend best to the *start* and *end* of a long context and can overlook material buried in the middle. So you shouldn't just dump everything in, **what** you put in context and **where** matters. This is a core argument for RAG: retrieve the few most relevant chunks instead of stuffing the whole corpus.

## Why hallucinations happen

A **hallucination** is confident, fluent, *wrong* output, a plausible-sounding fake citation, API, or number. It's not a bug bolted on; it's a direct consequence of how the model works:

1. **The objective rewards plausibility, not truth.** The model is trained to produce *likely* text, not *verified* text. A fabricated-but-plausible citation is, to the model, exactly the kind of thing that "should" come next.
2. **No built-in knowledge boundary.** The model doesn't reliably know what it doesn't know. It interpolates. When asked about something sparse or absent in training, it fills the gap with its best statistical guess, stated with the same fluent confidence as a fact.
3. **Frozen, lossy knowledge.** Training data has a cutoff and is compressed into weights. Anything after the cutoff, or too rare to be memorized, isn't there, but the model will still answer.
4. **Decoding randomness.** Sampling can pick a wrong-but-probable token that then commits the model to an incorrect path (each token conditions the next).

**Mitigations** (this is the whole motivation for the rest of the chapter):
- **Grounding via RAG**, give the model the actual source text and instruct it to answer *only* from that. The single biggest lever.
- **Lower temperature** for factual tasks.
- **Ask for citations / "I don't know"** in the system prompt, and verify against retrieved sources.
- **Structured output + validation**, constrain to JSON and validate; reject malformed or out-of-range values (exactly what you do in NYX before trusting a number).
- **Evaluation**, measure hallucination rate on a test set (covered below) rather than vibes.

This directly informs NYX: you never let the model *invent* portfolio numbers. You compute the numbers in Python, pass them in, and ask the model only to *narrate/structure* them. Generation is for language, not for arithmetic of record.

## Prompt engineering, done seriously

Prompting is programming the model in natural language. It's not folklore, there are mechanisms behind each technique.

**System prompt.** A persistent instruction that frames the model's role, constraints, and output format for the whole conversation. It sits at the front of the context and conditions everything. This is where you set NYX's rules: *"You are a financial-analytics assistant. Use ONLY the data provided. Never invent figures. Respond as JSON matching this schema."*

**Few-shot prompting.** Include a handful of input→output **examples** in the prompt. The model pattern-matches the demonstrated format and behavior. This is **in-context learning**: the model "learns" the task from the examples at inference time, without any weight updates, a striking emergent capability of scale. Two or three well-chosen examples often beat paragraphs of instructions, especially for getting an exact output format.

**Chain-of-thought (CoT).** Ask the model to "think step by step" / show its reasoning before the final answer. For multi-step problems (math, logic, multi-hop questions), generating intermediate steps measurably improves accuracy, because each reasoning token becomes context for the next, letting the model "work" across more tokens instead of guessing the answer in one shot. Caveat: the stated reasoning is a *generation*, not a guaranteed faithful trace of internal computation, useful, but don't treat it as a proof.

**Structured output / JSON (your NYX bread-and-butter).** Constrain the model to emit machine-readable output. The robust recipe:
1. Specify the **exact schema** in the system prompt (field names, types, allowed values).
2. Provide **one or two examples** of valid output (few-shot).
3. Use the provider's **JSON / structured-output mode** if available (it constrains decoding to valid JSON, eliminating "Sure! Here's your JSON:" preambles).
4. **Validate** the result against a schema (e.g., Pydantic) and **retry on failure**.

```python
# Provider-agnostic pattern for NYX-style structured summaries.
# Use your SDK's structured-output / JSON mode; this shows the shape, not a real key.
from pydantic import BaseModel, ValidationError

class HoldingSummary(BaseModel):
    ticker: str
    pct_of_portfolio: float
    trend: str            # "up" | "down" | "flat"
    note: str

SYSTEM = (
    "You are NYX's analytics writer. Use ONLY the numbers provided. "
    "Do not invent figures. Output JSON matching the HoldingSummary schema. "
    'Example: {"ticker":"AAPL","pct_of_portfolio":12.4,"trend":"up","note":"..."}'
)

def summarize_holding(llm, computed_stats: dict) -> HoldingSummary:
    # I compute pct/trend in Python; the model only NARRATES + STRUCTURES.
    user = f"Stats: {computed_stats}. Write the summary as JSON."
    for attempt in range(3):                      # validate-and-retry loop
        raw = llm.complete(system=SYSTEM, user=user, temperature=0.2, json_mode=True)
        try:
            return HoldingSummary.model_validate_json(raw)
        except ValidationError as e:
            user += f"\nYour previous output was invalid: {e}. Fix it."
    raise RuntimeError("LLM failed to produce valid JSON after retries")
```

This is the production-grade version of what you already do: **compute facts deterministically, let the model handle language, validate everything, retry on malformed output.** Never trust raw model text as data without validation.

**On the job / interview angle:** be able to explain *why* few-shot works (in-context learning, no weight update) and *why* you'd prefer structured-output mode + schema validation over regex-parsing free text (robustness, no preamble, typed guarantees). This is exactly the kind of pragmatic LLM-integration question that comes up.

## Embeddings & vector search: the foundations of RAG

Now the part NYX needs most. A raw LLM can't answer "what did *your* Q3 report say?", that text isn't in its weights. **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** fixes this: find the relevant text, put it in the prompt, let the model answer *from it*. RAG is "open-book exam" mode for an LLM.

The pipeline:
1. **Ingest & chunk.** Split documents into passages (e.g., 200-500 tokens, often with slight overlap so a sentence isn't cut across a boundary). Too big → diluted, expensive; too small → context lost.
2. **Embed.** Run each chunk through an embedding model → one vector per chunk. Store vectors + the original text.
3. **Index.** Put vectors in a **vector database** (FAISS, pgvector, Chroma, Pinecone, Weaviate, …) that supports fast nearest-neighbor search.
4. **Retrieve.** At query time, embed the question, find the top-`k` most similar chunks by cosine similarity.
5. **Augment & generate.** Stuff those chunks into the prompt ("Answer using ONLY this context: …") and call the LLM.

**Vector databases** exist because brute-force cosine over millions of vectors is too slow. They use **Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN)** indexes (HNSW graphs, IVF) that trade a little recall for huge speedups, sub-linear search instead of scanning everything. For your scale (a personal document set), brute-force NumPy is fine and clearer; for production scale, reach for a real vector DB.

### Build a tiny RAG retriever from scratch

Brute-force cosine search, the exact mechanism a vector DB optimizes. We'll mock the embedder (a real one is a single API/SDK call returning a vector), so the *retrieval logic* is the star:

```python
import numpy as np

def cosine_sim_matrix(query_vec, doc_matrix):
    """query_vec: (d,), doc_matrix: (N, d). Returns (N,) similarities."""
    q = query_vec / (np.linalg.norm(query_vec) + 1e-9)
    D = doc_matrix / (np.linalg.norm(doc_matrix, axis=1, keepdims=True) + 1e-9)
    return D @ q                                  # cosine since both are unit-normalized

class TinyRAG:
    def __init__(self, embed_fn):
        self.embed = embed_fn          # text -> np.ndarray; real life = embedding API
        self.chunks, self.vectors = [], None

    def ingest(self, chunks):
        self.chunks = list(chunks)
        self.vectors = np.vstack([self.embed(c) for c in self.chunks])

    def retrieve(self, query, k=3):
        sims = cosine_sim_matrix(self.embed(query), self.vectors)
        top = np.argsort(sims)[::-1][:k]
        return [(self.chunks[i], float(sims[i])) for i in top]

# --- mock embedder: deterministic hashed bag-of-words (stand-in for a real model) ---
def mock_embed(text, dim=64, seed_space=10000):
    vec = np.zeros(dim)
    for w in text.lower().split():
        h = hash(w) % seed_space
        vec[h % dim] += 1.0
    return vec

docs = [
    "NYX Finance computes portfolio allocation and risk metrics.",
    "The Q3 report showed technology holdings rose 12 percent.",
    "Bond yields fell while equity exposure increased in October.",
    "Customer support hours are nine to five on weekdays.",
]
rag = TinyRAG(mock_embed); rag.ingest(docs)
for chunk, score in rag.retrieve("how did tech stocks perform in Q3?", k=2):
    print(f"{score:.2f}  {chunk}")
# Top hit: the Q3 technology-holdings sentence - retrieved by meaning, not keywords.
```

Swap `mock_embed` for a real embedding endpoint and `np.argsort` for an ANN index, and that's a production retriever. The *idea*, embed, cosine-rank, return top-k, doesn't change.

The "build it" pieces are already inline above; here's the map so you can find them:

3. **A minimal RAG retriever** (`TinyRAG`), embed → cosine search → return context.

## Fine-tuning vs RAG vs prompting - when to use each

A constant real-world decision. The cheap-to-expensive ladder:

| Approach | What it changes | Best for | Cost / effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prompting** (system + few-shot + CoT) | Nothing in the model, just the input | Behavior, format, tone; tasks the model already *can* do | Lowest, iterate in minutes |
| **RAG** | Adds external knowledge at inference | Grounding in *your* / fresh / private data; reducing hallucination; citations | Medium, build & maintain a retrieval pipeline |
| **Fine-tuning** | The model's weights | Teaching a consistent *style/format*, a narrow skill, or compressing many examples into the model | Highest, data curation, training, eval, hosting |

**Rules of thumb:**
- **Start with prompting.** Most problems are a prompt problem. Cheapest to try, easiest to change.
- **Reach for RAG when the issue is *knowledge***, the model lacks facts (your docs, recent events) or hallucinates. RAG injects truth; fine-tuning generally does *not* reliably teach new facts.
- **Reach for fine-tuning when the issue is *behavior at scale***, you need a very specific output style/format every time, or you have thousands of examples that prompting can't fit, or you want to shrink prompt size (and cost) by baking the behavior in.
- **They compose.** A fine-tuned model *with* RAG *and* a good system prompt is common. They're layers, not rivals.

For NYX: **prompting + RAG** is the sweet spot. Prompting enforces the JSON schema; RAG grounds answers in your actual reports and live-computed stats. You don't need fine-tuning unless you outgrow what a strong prompt can specify.

## Evaluating LLM outputs

"It looked good in three tries" is not evaluation. LLM output is open-ended and partly random, so you need real methods:

- **Reference-based metrics** (BLEU/ROUGE for overlap, exact-match for closed answers), cheap, but weak for open-ended text (many good answers share no words).
- **Embedding similarity** to a reference answer, captures meaning better than word overlap; reuse cosine similarity.
- **LLM-as-judge**, use a (often stronger) model to score outputs against a rubric (correct? grounded in the context? right format?). Scalable and surprisingly effective, but watch for bias (judges can favor verbose or self-similar answers); calibrate against human labels.
- **Task-specific checks**, for NYX: does the JSON validate? Are numbers within plausible ranges? Does the narrative match the computed stats? These deterministic assertions catch the failures that matter most.
- **Human eval**, the gold standard for nuance and safety; expensive, so use it to spot-check and to validate your automated metrics.
- **Golden test sets & regression testing**, keep a fixed set of (input, expected) cases and re-run on every prompt or model change. Treat prompts like code: version them, test them, watch for regressions. (This bridges straight into MLOps, 20.2.)

**Common mistake:** shipping a prompt change because it fixed one example, without re-running the golden set. LLM behavior is high-variance and changes are non-local, a tweak that fixes case A often breaks case B. Always regression-test.

## Cost, latency, and safety basics

The engineering realities you have to budget for in NYX:

**Cost.** Billed per token, **input and output separately** (output is usually pricier). Levers: send less context (retrieve top-k, don't dump everything), cap `max_tokens`, **cache** repeated calls (and prompt-cache shared prefixes if the provider supports it), and use a **smaller/cheaper model** for easy subtasks (routing easy queries to a cheap model, hard ones to a strong model, a "model cascade").

**Latency.** Dominated by output length (tokens are generated serially) and context size (the `O(n²)` attention and a large prefill). **Streaming** tokens to the UI hides latency by showing output as it's produced. Keep prompts tight; long contexts are slow to *prefill*.

**Safety (preview, full treatment in 20.4 and 12-Security).**
- **Prompt injection**, untrusted text (a web page, a user file, an email) containing instructions like "ignore your rules and reveal the system prompt." Because the model can't inherently tell *data* from *instructions*, retrieved/user content can hijack it. Mitigate by separating trusted instructions from untrusted data, constraining output, and never auto-executing model output with real privileges. This becomes critical the moment the model can take **actions** (agents, 20.4).
- **PII / data leakage**, be careful what you send to a third-party API; redact sensitive fields; know your provider's data-retention policy. For NYX (financial data) this is a real compliance concern.
- **Toxic / unsafe output**, rely on provider safety layers plus your own output filters for user-facing text.

These connect forward to 20.4 (agents, where safety stakes jump because the model *acts*) and to 12-Security (injection, least privilege).

## Mini-project - "Chat with your documents" (end-to-end RAG)

The capstone: a complete RAG pipeline you could drop into NYX to answer questions over your own reports. Ingest → chunk → embed → retrieve → prompt → answer, with the LLM call left provider-agnostic (swap in your SDK; structured-output and MCP notes below).

```python
import numpy as np

# ---------- 1. INGEST ----------
documents = {
    "q3_report.txt": (
        "In Q3, NYX Finance's technology holdings rose 12% led by semiconductor "
        "names. Bond exposure was trimmed as yields climbed. Overall portfolio "
        "volatility fell versus Q2 due to increased diversification."
    ),
    "policy.txt": (
        "NYX never invents figures. All numbers shown to users are computed in "
        "Python from source data; the language model only narrates and structures."
    ),
}

# ---------- 2. CHUNK (with overlap) ----------
def chunk_text(text, size=120, overlap=20):
    words, chunks, i = text.split(), [], 0
    while i < len(words):
        chunks.append(" ".join(words[i:i+size]))
        i += size - overlap
    return chunks

corpus = []
for name, text in documents.items():
    for c in chunk_text(text):
        corpus.append({"source": name, "text": c})

# ---------- 3. EMBED (mock; swap for a real embedding endpoint) ----------
def embed(text, dim=128):
    v = np.zeros(dim)
    for w in text.lower().split():
        v[hash(w) % dim] += 1.0
    n = np.linalg.norm(v)
    return v / n if n else v

vectors = np.vstack([embed(c["text"]) for c in corpus])

# ---------- 4. RETRIEVE ----------
def retrieve(query, k=2):
    q = embed(query)
    sims = vectors @ q                      # cosine (all unit-normalized)
    top = np.argsort(sims)[::-1][:k]
    return [(corpus[i], float(sims[i])) for i in top]

# ---------- 5. AUGMENT + GENERATE ----------
def build_prompt(query, retrieved):
    context = "\n\n".join(
        f"[{r['source']}] {r['text']}" for r, _ in retrieved
    )
    system = (
        "Answer ONLY using the context below. If the answer isn't in the "
        "context, say 'I don't have that information.' Cite the [source]."
    )
    user = f"Context:\n{context}\n\nQuestion: {query}"
    return system, user

def mock_llm(system, user):
    # Stand-in for a real call: provider SDK, temperature ~0.2, optional json_mode.
    # Here we just confirm retrieval grounded the answer.
    if "technology" in user.lower() or "tech" in user.lower():
        return "Technology holdings rose 12% in Q3, led by semiconductors. [q3_report.txt]"
    return "I don't have that information."

def answer(query):
    retrieved = retrieve(query, k=2)
    system, user = build_prompt(query, retrieved)
    response = mock_llm(system, user)            # <- real LLM call goes here
    return response, retrieved

resp, used = answer("How did tech holdings do in Q3?")
print("ANSWER:", resp)
print("GROUNDED IN:", [r["source"] for r, _ in used])
```

**End-to-end walkthrough:**
1. **Ingest**, load raw documents (in production: parse PDFs/HTML/DB rows).
2. **Chunk**, split into overlapping passages so retrieval is granular and no idea is cut in half. Chunk size is a real tuning knob.
3. **Embed**, vectorize each chunk once, offline; store vectors + source metadata. (Real embedder = one SDK/API call per chunk; for scale, batch them.)
4. **Retrieve**, embed the query, cosine-rank, take top-k. For big corpora, replace the brute-force `@` with a vector DB's ANN index.
5. **Augment + generate**, build a prompt that *forces grounding* ("answer ONLY from context"), include source tags for **citations**, low temperature, and tell it to say "I don't know" when the context is silent. That single instruction is the strongest hallucination guard you have.

**Productionizing it (the leap from script to system):**
- Replace `mock_embed` with a real embedding model; replace brute-force search with a vector DB (pgvector/Chroma/etc.).
- Add **evaluation**: a golden set of (question, expected source) pairs; measure retrieval recall and answer faithfulness on every change.
- Add **observability** (13.1): log query, retrieved chunks + scores, prompt, response, token counts, latency, so you can debug "why did it answer wrong?" (usually a *retrieval* miss, not a generation problem).
- **Cost control**: cap `k` and chunk size; cache embeddings; consider a cheaper model for retrieval-grounded answers.
- **Tooling via MCP**: instead of hand-wiring this retriever into your app, you can expose it as an **MCP** (Model Context Protocol) tool/resource. MCP (Anthropic, 2024; the de-facto standard by 2026) gives a JSON-Schema'd, consistent interface so *any* MCP-capable model can call your "search NYX documents" retriever without bespoke glue. That's the natural bridge into agents, 20.4, where retrieval becomes one tool among many.

This is genuinely the architecture behind "chat with your PDFs," internal knowledge assistants, and the grounded summaries in NYX. The pieces are small; the *discipline* (chunking well, grounding hard, evaluating honestly, controlling cost) is the craft.

## Teach it back

If you can explain these from memory, you own the chapter:

1. **One sentence:** an LLM is a next-token predictor; doing that well over all of text forces it to learn structure, and generation is just prediction run in a loop.
2. **Tokenization:** text → subword tokens via BPE (merge frequent adjacent pairs); the model never sees characters (hence spelling errors), and cost/context are measured in tokens (~4 chars each).
3. **Embeddings:** tokens → vectors where meaning is direction; similarity = cosine of the angle (ties to 18.2's dot product).
4. **Attention:** `softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)V`, each token's query matches every key to weight a blend of values; `√dₖ` prevents gradient saturation; causal masking forbids looking ahead; multi-head = several lenses in parallel; positional encoding restores word order.
5. **Training:** pretraining (next-token, huge scale) → SFT (instruction demos) → RLHF/DPO (human preferences). Scaling laws make bigger runs predictably better.
6. **Decoding:** greedy vs sampling; temperature (randomness), top-k/top-p (candidate set). Low temp for JSON/facts.
7. **Hallucinations:** the objective rewards plausibility, knowledge is frozen/lossy, and the model doesn't know what it doesn't know, mitigate with RAG, low temp, validation, and "say I don't know."
8. **Prompting:** system prompt, few-shot (in-context learning), chain-of-thought, structured JSON + validate + retry (your NYX pattern).
9. **RAG:** chunk → embed → vector index → cosine retrieve top-k → stuff context → grounded generate. Grounding is the biggest hallucination lever.
10. **When to use what:** prompt first; RAG for knowledge; fine-tune for behavior at scale, and they compose.

## Exercises

**1. Tokenization & cost.** A NYX prompt pastes a 3,200-character JSON blob plus a 400-character instruction, and expects ~150 words of output. Estimate input and output tokens. At \$3/M input and \$15/M output tokens, estimate the per-call cost.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Input ≈ characters/4 = `(3200 + 400)/4 = 900` tokens (JSON often runs a bit higher per char, so call it ~900-1,100). Output ≈ `150 words × 1.3 ≈ 195` tokens. Cost ≈ `900/1e6 × $3 + 195/1e6 × $15 = $0.0027 + $0.0029 ≈ $0.0056` per call. Lesson: output tokens dominate here despite being far fewer, output is 5× the price. Capping `max_tokens` and tightening the JSON both matter.
</details>

**2. Attention by hand.** With `seq=2`, suppose `QKᵀ/√dₖ = [[2, 0], [0, 2]]` (each token attends mostly to itself). Compute the attention weights (row-wise softmax). Now suppose it's `[[0,0],[0,0]]`, what are the weights, and what does that mean?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Row softmax of `[2,0]`: `e²/(e²+e⁰) = 7.389/8.389 ≈ 0.88` and `0.12`. So `[[0.88,0.12],[0.12,0.88]]`, each token mostly attends to itself. For `[[0,0],[0,0]]`: `softmax([0,0]) = [0.5,0.5]` everywhere → uniform attention, i.e., each token's output is the *average* of all values (no preference). Larger score gaps = sharper, more selective attention; that's exactly why `√dₖ` scaling (which controls the gaps' magnitude) matters.
</details>

**3. Temperature.** Logits are `[2.0, 1.0, 0.0]`. Compute the softmax probabilities at `T=1` and at `T=0.5`. Which token gets more probable as `T` drops, and why?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

`T=1`: `exp([2,1,0]) = [7.39, 2.72, 1.0]`, sum `11.11` → `[0.665, 0.245, 0.090]`. `T=0.5`: divide logits by 0.5 → `[4,2,0]`, `exp = [54.6, 7.39, 1.0]`, sum `62.99` → `[0.867, 0.117, 0.016]`. Lower `T` concentrates mass on the top token (0.665 → 0.867): sharper, more deterministic. Higher `T` would flatten it toward uniform. This is why factual/JSON tasks use low `T`.
</details>

**4. RAG design.** Your documents are 50-page PDFs. You're getting wrong answers where the model misses content that's clearly in the PDF. Is this most likely a retrieval problem or a generation problem? Name three concrete fixes and how you'd confirm the diagnosis.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Most likely **retrieval**: if the right chunk never reaches the prompt, the model can't use it. Confirm by **logging retrieved chunks + scores** and checking whether the relevant passage is in the top-k. Fixes: (a) **chunking**, 50-page PDFs need sensible splits with overlap; a too-large chunk dilutes the signal, too-small loses context; (b) **increase k** or add a **re-ranker** to reorder candidates; (c) improve the **embedding model** or add hybrid keyword+vector search for exact terms (tickers, dates). Only if the correct chunk *is* retrieved but the answer is still wrong is it a generation/prompting issue (then tighten the "answer only from context" instruction or lower temperature).
</details>

**5. Build extension.** Extend the `TinyRAG`/decoding code: add a `generate_with_rag(query)` that retrieves top-k, builds a grounded prompt, then runs the `generate` sampling loop with low temperature, and prints both the answer *and* the sources used.
