## Start here - the on-ramp

Forget robots and chatbots for a second. Here's the oldest, truest definition of AI: **getting a computer to do something that, if a human did it, you'd say required intelligence.** Playing chess. Planning a delivery route. Proving a theorem. Filling in a Sudoku. Diagnosing a fault from symptoms.

Notice that none of those *necessarily* involves learning from data. A chess engine in 1997 beat the world champion with almost no learning at all. It **searched**: it looked ahead through millions of possible move sequences and picked the line that led to the best position. That's the heart of classical AI. **Most "intelligent" behavior, when you squint, is searching through a space of possibilities for one that satisfies your goal.**

Two big paradigms have fought for the soul of AI for 70 years:

- **Symbolic / "good old-fashioned AI" (GOFAI).** Intelligence = manipulating *symbols* by *rules*. You hand-code knowledge ("a king can move one square in any direction", "if fever and cough then maybe flu") and the machine reasons over it with search and logic. Transparent, explainable, brittle. Great at chess and theorem proving, terrible at recognizing a cat.
- **Learning / statistical.** Intelligence = fitting *functions* to *data*. You don't tell the machine the rules; you show it examples and it infers the rules itself. This is everything in Chapter 14. Powerful, data-hungry, opaque. Great at recognizing a cat, historically bad at multi-step reasoning.

The modern story (which we'll come back to at the end) is that **learning won the perception battle and is now eating reasoning too**, but the classical machinery in this chapter never went away. It's underneath AlphaGo (search + learned evaluation), underneath constraint solvers worth billions to logistics companies, and underneath the planners in robotics. You can't claim to understand AI with only the deep-learning half.

One more idea to pocket before we start: the **AI effect**. *"AI is whatever hasn't been done yet."* The moment a problem is solved (OCR, chess, route-finding, spam filtering), people stop calling it AI and start calling it "just an algorithm." So the field perpetually feels unsolved, because its successes keep getting renamed. Keep that in mind whenever someone says "that's not *real* AI."

And the **narrow vs. general** distinction, said plainly: everything we have today is **narrow AI**, superhuman at one task, clueless outside it. A chess engine can't book a flight. **Artificial general intelligence (AGI)**, one system that flexibly handles arbitrary tasks like a human, does not exist, and we'll be honest about that gap rather than hype it.

## Intelligent agents - the unifying frame

AI's textbook (Russell & Norvig) organizes the entire field around one abstraction: the **agent**. Lock this in, because every later topic is a special case.

An **agent** is anything that **perceives** its environment through **sensors** and **acts** on it through **actuators**. A thermostat is a (trivial) agent: senses temperature, acts by switching a heater. A self-driving car, a chess program, a trading bot, a Roomba, all agents.

```
        ┌─────────────────────────────┐
        │          AGENT              │
 percepts│  ┌──────────────────────┐  │ actions
 ───────►│  │  agent function       │  ├────────►
        │  │  f: percept* → action │  │
        │  └──────────────────────┘  │
        └─────────────────────────────┘
                    ▲   │
                    │   ▼
              ┌───────────────┐
              │  ENVIRONMENT  │
              └───────────────┘
```

The **agent function** maps the *history of percepts* to an action. The agent *program* is the concrete implementation. The whole field is really: *how do we write good agent functions?*

### Rationality - the goal, defined precisely

A **rational agent** is one that, for each possible percept sequence, **selects an action expected to maximize its performance measure**, given its built-in knowledge and what it has perceived so far.

Unpack the careful wording:

- **"Performance measure"**, an *external* yardstick we define (cleaned squares, games won, profit). Rationality is relative to it. Pick the wrong measure and a "rational" agent does something dumb-but-technically-optimal (the cleaning robot that dumps dirt so it can re-clean it).
- **"Expected to maximize"**, not *guaranteed* to. A rational poker player can play perfectly and still lose the hand. Rationality is about making the *best decision given the information*, not about being clairvoyant. **Rational ≠ omniscient, and rational ≠ successful.**
- **"Given what it has perceived"**, you can only be rational about what you could have known. Crossing on a green light and getting hit by a falling meteor wasn't irrational.

This separation, *do the best you can with what you know*, is the moral backbone of the whole subject, and it's why we can talk about "optimal" search even in an uncertain world.

### PEAS - how to specify a task

Before building any agent, specify its task environment with **PEAS**: **P**erformance, **E**nvironment, **A**ctuators, **S**ensors. It forces you to be concrete. For a self-driving taxi:

| Component | Self-driving taxi |
|---|---|
| **P**erformance | Safety, legality, trip time, fuel, passenger comfort, profit |
| **E**nvironment | Roads, traffic, pedestrians, weather, customers |
| **A**ctuators | Steering, accelerator, brake, signals, horn, display |
| **S**ensors | Cameras, LIDAR, GPS, speedometer, accelerometer |

We'll do this for NYX later: an *anomaly-alerting agent* whose Performance is "true anomalies caught minus false pages," Environment is the live metrics stream, Actuators are alerts/dashboards, Sensors are the telemetry feeds (this connects straight to the observability work in [13.1](../13-Observability/13.1-observability.md)).

### Environment properties (they decide which algorithm you need)

- **Fully vs. partially observable**, can the agent see the whole state (chess) or only part of it (poker, driving)?
- **Deterministic vs. stochastic**, does an action have one outcome (sliding-tile puzzle) or a distribution (dice, real roads)?
- **Single vs. multi-agent**, are there *other* agents, and are they adversarial (chess) or cooperative?
- **Static vs. dynamic**, does the world change while you think? (Chess is static; driving is brutally dynamic.)
- **Discrete vs. continuous**, finite moves (board games) vs. continuous control (steering angle).

This chapter lives mostly in the *fully-observable, deterministic, discrete, single-agent* corner (search/CSP) and the *adversarial, deterministic, discrete* corner (games). Those are the corners where classical AI is cleanest and most provable. The messy corners are where learning and probability take over.

### A ladder of agent designs

1. **Simple reflex**, `if percept matches condition then action`. No memory. (Thermostat.) Fails when the right action depends on history you can't currently see.
2. **Model-based reflex**, keeps an internal model of how the world evolves, so it can act on inferred state, not just the raw percept.
3. **Goal-based**, has an explicit *goal* and **searches** for an action sequence that reaches it. **This is where the rest of the chapter begins.**
4. **Utility-based**, goals aren't binary; it maximizes a *utility* function over outcomes (trades off speed vs. safety vs. cost).
5. **Learning**, improves its own components from experience. (All of Chapter 14.)

Search is the engine of the goal-based agent, so let's build it.

## Problem-solving as search

Here's the move that unlocks half of classical AI. **Frame your problem as a graph of states, and "solving it" becomes "finding a path."** A *search problem* is exactly five things:

1. **Initial state**, where you start (the scrambled puzzle, the start city).
2. **Actions(s)**, the legal moves from a state.
3. **Result(s, a)**, the **transition model**: the state you land in after doing `a` in `s`.
4. **Goal test(s)**, is this state a goal?
5. **Step cost(s, a, s')**, the price of that transition (often 1; sometimes distance, time, money).

Together, states + actions + transitions implicitly define a **state-space graph**. We almost never build it explicitly (the 8-puzzle has 181,440 states; chess has ~10⁴⁰); instead we **generate it on the fly** as we search. The thing we explore is a **search tree**: nodes are *paths* through the state space, and the same state can appear in many tree nodes via different paths.

You built the raw graph-traversal machinery in [Chapter 3.4](../03-Algorithms/03.4-graph-algorithms.md). This is that machinery aimed at *problem solving*: the graph is conceptual and infinite-ish, and we care about the **path**, not just reachability.

### A reusable problem interface

```python
from abc import ABC, abstractmethod

class SearchProblem(ABC):
    """The five-part contract every search problem implements."""
    @abstractmethod
    def initial_state(self): ...
    @abstractmethod
    def actions(self, state): ...            # iterable of legal actions
    @abstractmethod
    def result(self, state, action): ...     # transition model -> new state
    @abstractmethod
    def is_goal(self, state): ...
    def step_cost(self, state, action, next_state):
        return 1                              # default: every step costs 1
```

A search node bundles a state with the bookkeeping we need to recover the path:

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Any, Optional

@dataclass
class Node:
    state: Any
    parent: Optional["Node"] = None
    action: Any = None
    path_cost: float = 0.0          # g(n): cost from start to this node

    def path(self):
        """Walk parent pointers back to the root, return list of states."""
        node, seq = self, []
        while node:
            seq.append(node.state)
            node = node.parent
        return list(reversed(seq))

    def __lt__(self, other):        # so it can sit in a heap as a tiebreaker
        return self.path_cost < other.path_cost
```

### The four uninformed searches (recap → unified)

"Uninformed" (a.k.a. *blind*) search has **no idea which states are closer to the goal**. It only knows the graph structure. We covered BFS/DFS mechanics in [3.4](../03-Algorithms/03.4-graph-algorithms.md); here's how they specialize a *single generic algorithm*, the difference being only the **data structure of the frontier**:

| Search | Frontier | Complete? | Optimal? | Time | Space |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **BFS** | FIFO queue | Yes (finite b) | Yes *if step costs equal* | O(bᵈ) | O(bᵈ) |
| **UCS** (Dijkstra) | priority queue by `g(n)` | Yes | **Yes (any non-neg costs)** | O(b^(1+⌊C*/ε⌋)) | same |
| **DFS** | LIFO stack | No (infinite paths) | No | O(b^m) | **O(bm)** |
| **IDS** | DFS with rising depth limit | Yes | Yes (equal costs) | O(bᵈ) | **O(bd)** |

(`b` = branching factor, `d` = depth of shallowest goal, `m` = max depth, `C*` = optimal cost, `ε` = smallest step cost.)

Three lessons worth remembering:

- **BFS is optimal only when every step costs the same.** It finds the *shallowest* goal, which equals *cheapest* only if depth ∝ cost.
- **Uniform-Cost Search (UCS) = Dijkstra** repurposed for problem-solving. Frontier ordered by **path cost `g(n)`**. It's optimal for *any* non-negative step costs because it always expands the cheapest-so-far path. This is the search BFS *wishes* it were.
- **Iterative Deepening (IDS)** is the sleeper: run depth-limited DFS with limit 0, 1, 2, … until you find the goal. It gets DFS's tiny **O(bd) memory** with BFS's completeness and optimality. The repeated work is negligible because the last (deepest) layer dominates the node count. **IDS is the default uninformed search** when the state space is too big to hold the frontier in memory.

Here's UCS concretely (it's the bridge to A*):

```python
import heapq, itertools

def uniform_cost_search(problem):
    start = Node(problem.initial_state())
    if problem.is_goal(start.state):
        return start
    counter = itertools.count()                  # stable FIFO tiebreaker
    frontier = [(0.0, next(counter), start)]     # (g, tiebreak, node)
    best_g = {start.state: 0.0}                  # cheapest g seen per state

    while frontier:
        g, _, node = heapq.heappop(frontier)
        if g > best_g.get(node.state, float("inf")):
            continue                              # stale, cheaper path already found
        if problem.is_goal(node.state):
            return node                           # goal-test on POP, not push -> optimal
        for action in problem.actions(node.state):
            nxt = problem.result(node.state, action)
            new_g = g + problem.step_cost(node.state, action, nxt)
            if new_g < best_g.get(nxt, float("inf")):
                best_g[nxt] = new_g
                child = Node(nxt, node, action, new_g)
                heapq.heappush(frontier, (new_g, next(counter), child))
    return None
```

> **The single most common search bug:** goal-testing when you *generate* a node instead of when you *expand* (pop) it. For UCS/A*, you must test on pop, otherwise you might return a suboptimal goal that was generated early via an expensive path. We expanded on pop above on purpose.

## Informed search - heuristics and A*

Uninformed search wanders. **Informed (heuristic) search** uses a **heuristic function `h(n)`**, a cheap estimate of the cost from `n` to the nearest goal, to *aim* the search at the goal. A good heuristic turns "explore millions of states" into "explore thousands."

- **Greedy best-first** expands the node with the smallest `h(n)`. Fast, but **not optimal and not complete**: it chases the goal myopically and can charge down a deceptively-close-looking but expensive path.
- **A\*** expands the node with the smallest **`f(n) = g(n) + h(n)`**: *cost so far* + *estimated cost to go*. This one balance is one of the most important ideas in all of CS.

### Why A* is optimal - derived, not asserted

`f(n) = g(n) + h(n)` is "estimated total cost of the cheapest path through `n`." A* always expands the node with the lowest `f`. Everything hinges on the heuristic's properties.

**Admissibility.** `h` is *admissible* if it **never overestimates** the true cost-to-go: `0 ≤ h(n) ≤ h*(n)` for all `n`, where `h*` is the real cheapest cost from `n` to a goal. An admissible heuristic is *optimistic*.

**Theorem (A\* tree-search optimality).** With an admissible `h`, A* returns an optimal solution.

*Proof.* Suppose A* is about to return a suboptimal goal `G₂` (cost `g(G₂) > C*`, the optimal cost). Because the optimal path exists, there must be some node `n` *on the optimal path* still sitting in the frontier. For that `n`:
$$f(n) = g(n) + h(n) \le g(n) + h^*(n) = C^*$$
(the first ≤ is admissibility; the equality is because `n` is on an optimal path, so `g(n)+h*(n)` is exactly the optimal cost). Meanwhile, since `G₂` is a goal, `h(G₂)=0`, so `f(G₂) = g(G₂) > C* ≥ f(n)`. So `f(n) < f(G₂)`, which means A* would expand `n` **before** `G₂`. Contradiction: it never gets to return `G₂`. ∎

**Consistency (monotonicity)**, the stronger, usually-what-you-want property. `h` is *consistent* if for every node `n`, action `a`, successor `n'`:
$$h(n) \le \text{cost}(n, a, n') + h(n')$$
This is a triangle inequality: the estimate from `n` can't exceed one real step plus the estimate from where that step lands. **Consistency ⇒ admissibility** (proof by induction along any path to the goal), and it gives you something practical: **with a consistent heuristic, `f` is non-decreasing along every path**, so the first time A* pops a state it has already found the optimal path to it, meaning **you never have to re-expand a state**. That's why graph-search A* with a consistent `h` is clean and fast. Nearly every heuristic you'll hand-build (straight-line distance, Manhattan distance) is consistent.

A* with `h ≡ 0` *is* UCS. A* with a *perfect* `h = h*` walks straight to the goal expanding nothing extra. Real heuristics live between, and a *more informed* (larger but still admissible) heuristic **dominates**: it expands no more nodes than a weaker one.

## Build it - A* from scratch

One A* that takes any `SearchProblem` plus a heuristic. Notice how little it differs from UCS: just `+ h` in the priority.

```python
import heapq, itertools

def a_star(problem, heuristic):
    """A* graph search. heuristic(state) -> estimated cost to nearest goal."""
    start_state = problem.initial_state()
    start = Node(start_state)
    counter = itertools.count()
    h0 = heuristic(start_state)
    frontier = [(h0, next(counter), start)]        # priority = f = g + h
    best_g = {start_state: 0.0}

    while frontier:
        f, _, node = heapq.heappop(frontier)
        if problem.is_goal(node.state):
            return node, len(best_g)               # also report nodes touched
        for action in problem.actions(node.state):
            nxt = problem.result(node.state, action)
            new_g = node.path_cost + problem.step_cost(node.state, action, nxt)
            if new_g < best_g.get(nxt, float("inf")):
                best_g[nxt] = new_g
                child = Node(nxt, node, action, new_g)
                f_child = new_g + heuristic(nxt)
                heapq.heappush(frontier, (f_child, next(counter), child))
    return None, len(best_g)
```

### A* on a grid (the route-planner you'd actually ship)

```python
class GridProblem(SearchProblem):
    """4-connected grid with walls. State = (row, col)."""
    def __init__(self, grid, start, goal):
        self.grid = grid                  # list of strings, '#' = wall
        self.start, self.goal = start, goal
        self.R, self.C = len(grid), len(grid[0])

    def initial_state(self): return self.start
    def is_goal(self, s):    return s == self.goal

    def actions(self, s):
        r, c = s
        for dr, dc in ((1,0),(-1,0),(0,1),(0,-1)):
            nr, nc = r+dr, c+dc
            if 0 <= nr < self.R and 0 <= nc < self.C and self.grid[nr][nc] != '#':
                yield (dr, dc)

    def result(self, s, a): return (s[0]+a[0], s[1]+a[1])

def manhattan(goal):
    """Admissible & consistent on a 4-connected unit-cost grid."""
    return lambda s: abs(s[0]-goal[0]) + abs(s[1]-goal[1])

grid = [
    "S....#....",
    ".###.#.##.",
    ".#...#.#..",
    ".#.###.#.#",
    ".#.....#.G",
]
start = next((r,c) for r,row in enumerate(grid) for c,ch in enumerate(row) if ch=='S')
goal  = next((r,c) for r,row in enumerate(grid) for c,ch in enumerate(row) if ch=='G')

prob = GridProblem(grid, start, goal)
node, touched = a_star(prob, manhattan(goal))
print("Path length:", len(node.path()) - 1, "steps;  nodes expanded:", touched)
```

**Why Manhattan distance is admissible here:** on a 4-connected unit-cost grid you *must* take at least `|Δrow| + |Δcol|` steps to cover the gap (walls only make it *longer*), so the estimate never exceeds reality. It's consistent because one orthogonal step changes the estimate by exactly 1 = the step cost.

### A* on the 8-puzzle (and why the heuristic does all the work)

The 8-puzzle: a 3×3 grid, tiles 1-8 plus a blank, slide tiles into the blank to reach the goal. State space 181,440, too big for breath-holding BFS in your head, trivial for A* with the right `h`.

```python
GOAL = (1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,0)   # 0 = blank

class EightPuzzle(SearchProblem):
    def __init__(self, start): self.start = start
    def initial_state(self): return self.start
    def is_goal(self, s): return s == GOAL
    def actions(self, s):
        i = s.index(0); r, c = divmod(i, 3); moves = []
        if r > 0: moves.append(-3)
        if r < 2: moves.append(+3)
        if c > 0: moves.append(-1)
        if c < 2: moves.append(+1)
        return moves
    def result(self, s, a):
        i = s.index(0); j = i + a
        lst = list(s); lst[i], lst[j] = lst[j], lst[i]
        return tuple(lst)

def misplaced_tiles(s):                       # h1: count tiles out of place
    return sum(1 for i,v in enumerate(s) if v != 0 and v != GOAL[i])

def manhattan_puzzle(s):                       # h2: sum of tile distances (dominates h1)
    total = 0
    for i, v in enumerate(s):
        if v == 0: continue
        gi = GOAL.index(v)
        total += abs(i//3 - gi//3) + abs(i%3 - gi%3)
    return total

start = (7,2,4,5,0,6,8,3,1)
p = EightPuzzle(start)
n1, t1 = a_star(p, misplaced_tiles)
n2, t2 = a_star(p, manhattan_puzzle)
print(f"misplaced: {len(n1.path())-1} moves, {t1} states touched")
print(f"manhattan: {len(n2.path())-1} moves, {t2} states touched")
# Both find the SAME optimal solution length; manhattan touches far fewer states.
```

Run it: both heuristics give the same *optimal* move count (admissibility guarantees that), but **Manhattan touches dramatically fewer states** because it *dominates* misplaced-tiles (Manhattan ≥ misplaced for every state, since a misplaced tile is ≥1 Manhattan step away). **This is the core craft of informed search: a better admissible heuristic is pure free speed.** Where do good heuristics come from? Usually from **relaxing the problem**: Manhattan distance is the *exact* cost of an 8-puzzle where tiles can pass through each other. The relaxed problem's true cost is an admissible heuristic for the real one. That's a reusable trick.

## Local search - when you only care about the goal state, not the path

A* needs the whole path and a manageable frontier. But for **optimization** problems, "find me a *configuration* that scores high," where the path there is irrelevant (N-queens layout, a good schedule, tuned parameters), we use **local search**: keep a single current state, repeatedly nudge it toward better neighbors. Tiny memory, scales to enormous spaces, but can get stuck.

**Hill climbing.** Move to the best neighbor; stop when no neighbor is better. Dead simple, and it walks straight into three traps: **local maxima** (a peak that isn't the highest), **plateaus** (flat regions with no gradient to follow), and **ridges**. Random restarts patch it but it's fundamentally greedy.

**Simulated annealing** fixes the local-maximum trap with an idea borrowed from metallurgy: **sometimes accept a *worse* move, with a probability that shrinks over time.** Early on (high "temperature") it wanders freely and can climb out of bad valleys; as `T` cools it becomes greedy and settles. The acceptance rule is `P(accept worse) = exp(-Δ/T)`, straight from the Boltzmann distribution. With a slow enough cooling schedule it's guaranteed to find the global optimum (in the limit).

**Genetic algorithms** maintain a *population* of states, score them with a *fitness* function, **select** the fit ones, **crossover** (mix two parents' "DNA"), and **mutate**, survival-of-the-fittest as an optimizer. Great when the structure recombines meaningfully; finicky to tune.

## Build it - simulated annealing on N-Queens

N-queens: place `N` queens on an `N×N` board, none attacking. We'll represent a state as `queens[col] = row` (one queen per column, so we only fight over rows and diagonals), and minimize the number of attacking pairs (0 = solved).

```python
import math, random

def conflicts(state):
    """Number of attacking queen pairs. 0 == a solution."""
    n = len(state); c = 0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i+1, n):
            if state[i] == state[j]:                       # same row
                c += 1
            elif abs(state[i]-state[j]) == abs(i-j):       # same diagonal
                c += 1
    return c

def simulated_annealing_nqueens(n, T0=30.0, cooling=0.995, steps=100_000):
    state = [random.randrange(n) for _ in range(n)]        # random start
    cur = conflicts(state)
    T = T0
    for _ in range(steps):
        if cur == 0:
            return state                                    # solved!
        col = random.randrange(n)                           # tweak one queen
        new_row = random.randrange(n)
        old_row = state[col]
        state[col] = new_row
        new_cost = conflicts(state)
        delta = new_cost - cur                              # >0 means worse
        if delta < 0 or random.random() < math.exp(-delta / T):
            cur = new_cost                                  # accept (always if better)
        else:
            state[col] = old_row                            # reject, revert
        T = max(T * cooling, 1e-3)                          # cool down
    return state if cur == 0 else None

sol = simulated_annealing_nqueens(8)
print("8-queens solution (queens[col]=row):", sol, "conflicts:", conflicts(sol))
```

The whole intelligence is in **`math.exp(-delta / T)`**: when `T` is large, even a bad move (`delta>0`) is accepted with decent probability, so we explore; as `T→0` that probability →0 and we converge. This single line is the difference between getting stuck and getting unstuck. (For N-queens specifically a smarter "min-conflicts" heuristic is faster, but SA is the technique worth owning because it generalizes to *any* cost surface, including, conceptually, the loss landscapes you optimize in Chapter 14.)

## Adversarial search - games

Now add an opponent who's actively trying to beat you. The environment is **multi-agent and adversarial**, so we can't just search for *our* best path; we have to account for the other player choosing *their* best response. The framework is **minimax**.

Two players, **MAX** (us, maximizing the score) and **MIN** (the opponent, minimizing it). At each game state it's someone's turn. The **minimax value** of a state is:

- terminal state → its actual utility (e.g. +1 win, 0 draw, −1 loss),
- MAX's turn → the **max** of the children's minimax values,
- MIN's turn → the **min** of the children's minimax values.

**The derivation, in words:** you assume your opponent is perfect. So when you evaluate a move, you assume that *afterward* MIN will pick whatever is worst for you, and *before* that you pick whatever is best for you given MIN's best reply, and so on recursively down to terminal states whose value you actually know. Minimax is just "back up the known leaf values through alternating min and max layers." Play the move at the root that leads to the highest backed-up value, and you play **optimally against a perfect opponent**, which, for solved small games like tic-tac-toe, means **you literally cannot lose.**

### Alpha-beta pruning - same answer, fraction of the work

Plain minimax explores the entire tree: `O(bᵐ)`. Most of it is wasted: once you've found that a branch is *good enough to be ignored by a rational opponent*, you can stop exploring it. **Alpha-beta** carries two bounds down the recursion:

- **α** = the best (highest) value MAX can *already guarantee* on the path so far,
- **β** = the best (lowest) value MIN can already guarantee.

When `α ≥ β`, the current node can't influence the final decision (one player already has a better option elsewhere), so we **prune** the rest of its children. The result is **identical** to minimax: it's a pure efficiency win. With good move ordering, alpha-beta searches `O(b^(m/2))`, effectively **doubling the depth** you can look ahead for the same cost. That single optimization is what made deep game search practical.

## Build it - minimax + alpha-beta, and an unbeatable tic-tac-toe AI

```python
import math

class TicTacToe:
    """Board = tuple of 9 chars: 'X', 'O', or ' '. X is MAX, O is MIN."""
    LINES = [(0,1,2),(3,4,5),(6,7,8),(0,3,6),(1,4,7),(2,5,8),(0,4,8),(2,4,6)]

    def initial(self):
        return (' ',)*9

    def player(self, board):                       # whose turn (X moves first)
        return 'X' if board.count(' ') % 2 == 1 else 'O'

    def actions(self, board):
        return [i for i, c in enumerate(board) if c == ' ']

    def result(self, board, i):
        b = list(board); b[i] = self.player(board)
        return tuple(b)

    def winner(self, board):
        for a, b, c in self.LINES:
            if board[a] != ' ' and board[a] == board[b] == board[c]:
                return board[a]
        return None

    def terminal(self, board):
        return self.winner(board) is not None or ' ' not in board

    def utility(self, board):
        w = self.winner(board)
        return 1 if w == 'X' else -1 if w == 'O' else 0


def alphabeta(game, board, alpha=-math.inf, beta=math.inf):
    """Return (value, best_move) for the player to move at `board`."""
    if game.terminal(board):
        return game.utility(board), None

    maximizing = (game.player(board) == 'X')
    best_move = None

    if maximizing:
        value = -math.inf
        for a in game.actions(board):
            v, _ = alphabeta(game, game.result(board, a), alpha, beta)
            if v > value:
                value, best_move = v, a
            alpha = max(alpha, value)
            if alpha >= beta:
                break                     # β-cutoff: MIN won't allow this branch
        return value, best_move
    else:
        value = math.inf
        for a in game.actions(board):
            v, _ = alphabeta(game, game.result(board, a), alpha, beta)
            if v < value:
                value, best_move = v, a
            beta = min(beta, value)
            if alpha >= beta:
                break                     # α-cutoff: MAX won't allow this branch
        return value, best_move


def best_move(game, board):
    _, move = alphabeta(game, board)
    return move

# Prove it's unbeatable: optimal X vs. optimal O always draws.
game = TicTacToe()
board = game.initial()
while not game.terminal(board):
    board = game.result(board, best_move(game, board))
assert game.utility(board) == 0     # perfect play -> draw, every time
print("Optimal vs optimal:", "DRAW" if game.utility(board)==0 else game.utility(board))
```

You can drop a human into the loop (prompt for `O`'s move) and you'll find the AI never loses; at best, you force a draw. That's minimax doing exactly what the math promised. For bigger games (connect-4, chess) the tree is too deep to search to terminal states, so you (1) cut off at a depth limit and (2) replace `utility` at the cutoff with a **learned or hand-crafted evaluation function** that *estimates* who's winning. That's the seam where classical search meets ML: AlphaGo is literally minimax-style search (MCTS) with a deep-net evaluation, the marriage of both chapters.

> **Interview angle.** "Implement tic-tac-toe AI," "explain alpha-beta," and "what makes a heuristic admissible" are bread-and-butter AI/algorithms interview questions. Being able to *derive* why alpha-beta is exact (not approximate) and why move ordering matters separates you from people who memorized the pseudocode.

## Constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs)

A huge class of problems isn't "find a path", it's "find an **assignment of values to variables** that satisfies a set of **constraints**." Sudoku, map coloring, exam scheduling, register allocation in compilers, seating charts. A **CSP** is:

- **Variables** `X₁…Xₙ`,
- **Domains** `D₁…Dₙ` (allowed values per variable),
- **Constraints** restricting which combinations are legal.

The payoff of recognizing a CSP: instead of treating each problem as a unique puzzle, you get **general-purpose solvers**, backtracking + constraint propagation + smart heuristics, that crush all of them.

**Backtracking search** is DFS over partial assignments: pick an unassigned variable, try a value consistent with constraints so far, recurse; if you get stuck, undo and try the next value. Pure backtracking already beats brute force enormously (it prunes the moment a constraint is violated). Three accelerators make it *fast*:

1. **Constraint propagation (AC-3 / arc consistency).** Before/during search, prune values that *can't possibly* participate in a solution. An arc `X→Y` is *consistent* if for every value of `X` there's some compatible value of `Y`; AC-3 repeatedly enforces this until nothing changes, collapsing domains and often solving easy CSPs with no search at all.
2. **Variable ordering, MRV (Minimum Remaining Values).** Assign the most-constrained variable next (fewest legal values). Fail fast: if a variable is about to have *no* options, find out now, not 20 levels deep.
3. **Value ordering, LCV (Least Constraining Value).** Try the value that rules out the fewest options for *neighbors*, leaving maximum flexibility.

## Build it - a Sudoku solver (backtracking + AC-3 + MRV)

Sudoku is the perfect CSP demo: 81 variables (cells), domains 1-9, constraints "all different" within each row, column, and 3×3 box.

```python
from collections import defaultdict

def neighbors_map():
    """For each cell 0..80, the set of cells it shares a row/col/box with."""
    nb = defaultdict(set)
    for r in range(9):
        for c in range(9):
            i = r*9 + c
            for c2 in range(9):                 # row
                if c2 != c: nb[i].add(r*9 + c2)
            for r2 in range(9):                 # col
                if r2 != r: nb[i].add(r2*9 + c)
            br, bc = 3*(r//3), 3*(c//3)         # box
            for dr in range(3):
                for dc in range(3):
                    j = (br+dr)*9 + (bc+dc)
                    if j != i: nb[i].add(j)
    return nb

NB = neighbors_map()

def parse(grid):
    """grid: 81-char string, '.' or '0' for blank. Returns domains dict."""
    domains = {}
    for i, ch in enumerate(grid):
        domains[i] = {int(ch)} if ch in "123456789" else set(range(1, 10))
    return domains

def ac3(domains):
    """Enforce arc consistency. Returns False if a domain wipes out."""
    queue = [(xi, xj) for xi in range(81) for xj in NB[xi]]
    while queue:
        xi, xj = queue.pop()
        if revise(domains, xi, xj):
            if not domains[xi]:
                return False                    # contradiction
            for xk in NB[xi] - {xj}:
                queue.append((xk, xi))
    return True

def revise(domains, xi, xj):
    """Remove values of xi with no consistent partner in xj ('all-different')."""
    revised = False
    for v in set(domains[xi]):
        # if xj can ONLY be v, then xi can't be v
        if domains[xj] == {v}:
            domains[xi].discard(v)
            revised = True
    return revised

def backtrack(domains):
    if all(len(d) == 1 for d in domains.values()):
        return domains                          # solved
    # MRV: pick unassigned cell with the smallest domain (>1)
    i = min((k for k, d in domains.items() if len(d) > 1), key=lambda k: len(domains[k]))
    for v in sorted(domains[i]):
        trial = {k: set(d) for k, d in domains.items()}   # copy
        trial[i] = {v}
        if ac3(trial):                                     # propagate
            result = backtrack(trial)
            if result:
                return result
    return None

def solve_sudoku(grid):
    domains = parse(grid)
    if not ac3(domains):
        return None
    solved = backtrack(domains)
    if not solved:
        return None
    return "".join(str(next(iter(solved[i]))) for i in range(81))

puzzle = ("53..7...."
          "6..195..."
          ".98....6."
          "8...6...3"
          "4..8.3..1"
          "7...2...6"
          ".6....28."
          "...419..5"
          "....8..79")
print(solve_sudoku(puzzle))
# 534678912672195348198342567859761423426853791713924856961537284287419635345286179
```

Run it: the classic "world's hardest" Sudokus that defeat naive backtracking fall in milliseconds, because **AC-3 propagation does most of the work and MRV makes the rare guesses cheap**. That's the CSP lesson in one program: *propagate first, search second, and search the most-constrained thing first.* Map-coloring (color a map so no two neighbors share a color) is the same code with a different graph; it's the textbook intro CSP and worth coding as an exercise.

## Knowledge representation & logic

Search and CSPs are about *finding* solutions. The other GOFAI pillar is **reasoning**: representing what you know as **logic** and *deriving* new facts. This is how expert systems and theorem provers work.

**Propositional logic.** Statements are true/false symbols combined with `¬ (not)`, `∧ (and)`, `∨ (or)`, `→ (implies)`, `↔ (iff)`. A **knowledge base (KB)** is a set of sentences asserted true. **Entailment** (`KB ⊨ α`) means: in *every* world where the KB is true, `α` is also true. **Inference** is the mechanical process of deriving entailed sentences. (You built the truth-table and boolean foundations back in [1.2](../01-Foundations/01.2-boolean-logic.md), this is that, aimed at reasoning.)

A sound, complete inference procedure: **resolution**. Put the KB in **conjunctive normal form** (ANDs of ORs of literals), then repeatedly apply the resolution rule, from `(A ∨ B)` and `(¬A ∨ C)` infer `(B ∨ C)` (the `A`/`¬A` "cancel"). To prove `KB ⊨ α`, add `¬α` and derive a contradiction (the empty clause). This is **proof by refutation**, and it's the engine inside SAT solvers and Prolog.

```python
def resolve(c1, c2):
    """Resolve two clauses (sets of literals like 'A', '!A'). Yield resolvents."""
    for lit in c1:
        comp = lit[1:] if lit.startswith('!') else '!' + lit
        if comp in c2:
            yield (c1 - {lit}) | (c2 - {comp})

def entails(kb_clauses, query):
    """Refutation: KB ⊨ query  iff  KB ∧ ¬query is unsatisfiable."""
    neg = {('!' + q if not q.startswith('!') else q[1:]) for q in query}
    clauses = [set(c) for c in kb_clauses] + [neg]
    new = set()
    while True:
        pairs = [(clauses[i], clauses[j])
                 for i in range(len(clauses)) for j in range(i+1, len(clauses))]
        for ci, cj in pairs:
            for r in resolve(ci, cj):
                if not r:                       # empty clause => contradiction
                    return True                 # query is entailed
                new.add(frozenset(r))
        if new <= {frozenset(c) for c in clauses}:
            return False                        # no new info, not entailed
        for c in new:
            if set(c) not in clauses:
                clauses.append(set(c))

# KB: (rain -> wet), rain.   Query: wet?
kb = [{'!rain', 'wet'}, {'rain'}]
print("wet entailed?", entails(kb, {'wet'}))     # True
print("snow entailed?", entails(kb, {'snow'}))   # False
```

**First-order logic (FOL).** Propositional logic can't say "*all* humans are mortal" without enumerating every human. FOL adds **objects, predicates, functions, and quantifiers** (`∀ for-all`, `∃ exists`): `∀x. Human(x) → Mortal(x)`. Combined with `Human(Socrates)`, inference derives `Mortal(Socrates)`. FOL is vastly more expressive, it's the language of mathematics, at the cost of harder (semi-decidable) inference.

**Expert systems** were the 1980s commercial peak of this idea: encode a human specialist's knowledge as hundreds of `IF…THEN` rules (MYCIN for infections, XCON for configuring computers) and let an inference engine chain through them. They worked in narrow domains and then hit a wall, the **knowledge-acquisition bottleneck**: hand-encoding enough rules, keeping them consistent, and handling uncertainty was crushing. That wall is precisely what motivated the shift to learning.

## Planning (briefly)

**Planning** is search aimed at *action sequences* in a world described by logic. Instead of an opaque transition model, actions are described by **preconditions** and **effects** (STRIPS/PDDL style): `Action(Move(a,b), precond: At(a) ∧ Path(a,b), effect: At(b) ∧ ¬At(a))`. A planner searches the space of world-states for a sequence of actions whose effects achieve the goal. The win over plain search is **structure**: the planner can reason about which actions are even *relevant* to the goal and ignore the rest, and it can build *partial-order* plans that don't over-commit to an ordering.

Classical planning powers logistics, spacecraft autonomy (NASA's Remote Agent flew this), and robotics task sequencing. It's the bridge from "single decision" to "coherent multi-step behavior," and it's where symbolic AI is still state-of-the-art.

## The bridge to machine learning - why learning won

Everything above is **hand-coded intelligence**: a human writes the rules, the heuristics, the evaluation functions, the logic. It's transparent and provably correct, and for *crisp, well-defined* problems (chess, Sudoku, scheduling) it's still the best tool. But it hit three walls that learning walks straight through:

1. **The knowledge bottleneck.** Hand-encoding enough rules for a messy real domain is infeasible and unmaintainable. You can't write rules for "is this a cat": nobody can articulate the rules.
2. **Brittleness.** Symbolic systems fail off-distribution and degrade ungracefully, one missing rule, total failure. Learned systems degrade gracefully and handle noise and ambiguity natively.
3. **Perception.** Vision, speech, and language defied rule-writing for 40 years. Statistical/deep learning shattered all three by **inferring the rules from data** instead of demanding humans state them (Chapter 14.2).

So the field's center of gravity moved from "engineer the knowledge" to "engineer the *learning*." But, and this is the mature take, **it's not either/or.** The strongest modern systems are *hybrids*: AlphaGo = search (this chapter) + learned evaluation (Chapter 14). Modern LLMs do statistical learning but are increasingly bolted to search, tool-use, and symbolic verification for reasoning. Constraint solvers, planners, and SAT solvers quietly run trillion-dollar logistics, chip design, and verification workloads where you need *guarantees*, not vibes. **Knowing both halves is the point of this chapter.**

## A sober word on AI ethics, safety, and limits

We won't end on hype. Honest engineering means stating the limits plainly:

- **Today's AI is narrow.** Every system we have is superhuman in a sliver and helpless outside it. AGI does not exist, and confident timelines for it are guesses, not engineering.
- **Capable ≠ correct ≠ aligned.** A model can be fluent and wrong (hallucination), accurate on average and catastrophic on a minority slice (the fairness problem, we'll quantify this in [20.2](20.2-ml-engineering-and-mlops.md)), or optimizing *exactly* the objective you gave it in a way you didn't intend (specification gaming, the cleaning robot that hides the dirt). **The objective is a design responsibility, not a detail.**
- **Bias in, bias out.** Learned systems inherit and amplify the biases in their training data. A model trained on historical hiring decisions learns historical discrimination. This isn't a bug to patch later; it's a property to measure and mitigate from day one.
- **Opacity is a real cost.** The deep-learning systems that beat GOFAI did so partly by giving up the explainability GOFAI had. In medicine, lending, and criminal justice, "the model said so" is not an acceptable answer, which is why interpretability and the *right to an explanation* matter.
- **Safety scales with capability.** As systems take more consequential actions with less human oversight, the cost of a misspecified objective or an undetected failure mode grows. Monitoring, guardrails, human-in-the-loop, and the ability to *roll back* (which we treat as engineering, not philosophy, in [20.2](20.2-ml-engineering-and-mlops.md) and [10.3](../10-Cloud-DevOps/10.3-cicd-and-deployment.md)) are part of building AI responsibly.

None of this is a reason to not build. It's a reason to build like an engineer who'll be on call when it breaks.

## Exercises

**1. (BFS optimality.)** Give a concrete graph with non-uniform step costs where BFS returns a *valid* path that is *not* the cheapest, and UCS returns the cheapest. Explain in one sentence why BFS fails.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Start `A`. Edges: `A→G` cost 10 (one edge, depth 1), and `A→B` cost 1, `B→G` cost 1 (path cost 2, depth 2). BFS explores by *depth*: it fully exhausts depth 1 before touching depth 2, so it discovers the goal via the direct edge `A→G` (cost 10) and returns immediately, before it ever generates the depth-2 path through `B` (cost 2), no matter which order the edges out of `A` are listed in, since both depth-1 nodes are enqueued before any depth-2 node. UCS orders the frontier by accumulated cost, so it expands the cost-1 edge to `B` before the cost-10 edge to `G`, and correctly returns `A→B→G` (cost 2). **BFS optimizes *number of edges*, not *cost*.**
</details>

**2. (Admissibility check.)** For the 8-puzzle, is "number of misplaced tiles, then multiplied by 2" admissible? Prove or give a counterexample.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Not admissible.** Misplaced-tiles is admissible (each misplaced tile needs ≥1 move), but each misplaced tile might need *only* 1 move, so the true cost can equal the misplaced count. Doubling it then *overestimates*: a single tile one square from home gives `misplaced=1`, doubled `=2`, but the real cost-to-go is `1`. Overestimating `h > h*` breaks the optimality proof (the bound `f(n) ≤ C*` no longer holds), and A* can return a suboptimal solution.
</details>

**3. (Alpha-beta ordering.)** Explain why move ordering takes alpha-beta from `O(bᵐ)` toward `O(b^(m/2))`, and what the *worst* ordering does.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Alpha-beta prunes a branch as soon as `α ≥ β`. If you examine the **best move first** at each node, the bounds tighten immediately, so most sibling branches get cut after one child, you effectively only fully expand one child per node, halving the effective depth to `m/2`. With the **worst ordering** (best move last), the cutoff never fires early; you must examine every child before finding the good one, and alpha-beta degrades to full minimax `O(bᵐ)`. This is why real engines spend effort on move ordering (killer moves, history heuristics, iterative deepening to seed the order).
</details>

**4. (CSP propagation.)** In the Sudoku solver, what does AC-3 contribute that plain backtracking doesn't, and construct (in words) a puzzle that AC-3 alone solves with *zero* backtracking.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

AC-3 enforces arc consistency: it deletes any candidate that has no compatible partner under "all-different," shrinking domains *before* and *between* guesses. Plain backtracking only checks consistency *after* a tentative assignment. Any puzzle where the propagation cascade fully determines every cell, i.e., each removal forces a singleton, which forces the next, is solved by AC-3 alone with no search. "Easy"/"naked-singles" Sudokus are exactly these: every blank, once its row/col/box are considered, has exactly one possibility, and resolving it exposes the next. AC-3 chains these to completion; `backtrack` then just reads off the singletons.
</details>

**5. (Local search trap.)** Why can hill-climbing get permanently stuck while simulated annealing escapes, expressed in terms of the acceptance rule?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Hill-climbing only ever moves to a *strictly better* neighbor, so at a local maximum (no better neighbor) it halts, even though a globally better state exists across a valley. Simulated annealing accepts a worse move with probability `exp(-Δ/T)`. While `T` is non-trivial, that probability is positive, so SA can step *downhill* out of the local maximum's basin and into another where it may then climb higher. As `T→0` the probability of a downhill move →0 and SA converges; the cooling schedule trades exploration (high `T`) for exploitation (low `T`). Hill-climbing is the `T=0` special case from the start, which is exactly why it's trapped.
</details>

**6. (Entailment.)** Using the resolution code, encode "If it's a weekday and not a holiday, the market is open. Today is a weekday. Today is not a holiday." Show the KB clauses and verify the market is open.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Rule `(weekday ∧ ¬holiday) → open` in CNF is `(¬weekday ∨ holiday ∨ open)`. Facts: `weekday`, `¬holiday`.

```python
kb = [{'!weekday', 'holiday', 'open'}, {'weekday'}, {'!holiday'}]
entails(kb, {'open'})   # True
```

Refutation adds `¬open` and resolves: `(¬weekday ∨ holiday ∨ open)` with `weekday` → `(holiday ∨ open)`; with `¬holiday` → `(open)`; with `¬open` → empty clause = contradiction, so `open` is entailed.
</details>
