# Chapter 17.4 - Chip Design Flow & Hardware Description Languages

> Goal: After this chapter you'll understand how a chip goes from an idea to silicon, the full ASIC/SoC flow (spec → architecture → RTL → verification → synthesis → place-and-route → timing closure → DFT → tapeout → bring-up), and you'll be able to *write and simulate real hardware* in Verilog using free tools on your laptop. You'll build a counter and a finite-state machine, write a testbench that checks them, and understand why "describing hardware" is a fundamentally different act than "writing software." Most importantly, you'll see exactly where your software skills (scripting, testing, automation, Python/C++) plug into this industry, because the biggest entry door for a CS grad, **design verification**, is *mostly software*.

## 🌱 Start here (the gentle on-ramp)

Let's clear up the single most confusing thing first, because it trips up almost everyone for a while.

When you write Python, you're writing a list of *instructions that happen one after another*. Line 1, then line 2, then line 3. Time flows down the file. That mental model runs so deep that it's easy to forget it's a choice.

A hardware description language (Verilog, SystemVerilog, VHDL) looks like code, it has `if`, it has `=`, it has modules that look like functions, but it is **not** a list of instructions. It is a *description of a circuit*. When you write three lines of Verilog, you're not saying "do this, then this, then this." You're saying "wire up an AND gate here, a flip-flop there, and a multiplexer over there", and **they all exist and operate at the same time, forever, in parallel.** There is no "line 1 runs first." The hardware just *is*.

Here's the analogy that makes it click. Software is a recipe: a sequence of steps a single cook follows. Hardware is the *kitchen itself*, the stove, the sink, the fridge, all physically present and all "running" simultaneously. Verilog isn't the recipe; it's the blueprint of the kitchen. When the chapter on [Boolean Logic](../01-Foundations/01.2-boolean-logic.md) had you build an adder out of AND/OR/NOT gates in Python, you *simulated* a circuit with sequential code. Verilog lets you *describe* that same circuit directly, and real tools turn your description into actual transistors.

**Why this matters for your career:** designing a chip is now overwhelmingly a *software* activity. Nobody draws transistors by hand. Engineers write HDL, then a stack of programs (the EDA tools, Synopsys, Cadence, Siemens) automatically transforms that HDL into a physical layout of billions of transistors. And before any of that, an army of **verification engineers** writes enormous amounts of software, testbenches, randomized stimulus, checkers, coverage models, to prove the design is correct *before* it costs millions to manufacture. That verification world is built on the exact skills you already have: scripting, test design, automation, data analysis, debugging. This chapter is the bridge.

## 📐 The big picture: what is an ASIC, an SoC, an FPGA?

A few words you'll see constantly:

- **IC (integrated circuit):** any chip, transistors and wires fabricated together on silicon.
- **ASIC (Application-Specific Integrated Circuit):** a chip designed for one purpose and *frozen into silicon*. Once made, it can't change. A Bitcoin miner ASIC, a custom AI accelerator, the chip in your earbuds.
- **SoC (System on Chip):** an ASIC that packs a *whole system* onto one die, CPU cores, GPU, memory controllers, radios, accelerators. Your phone's chip (Apple A-series, Snapdragon) is an SoC. "SoC design" is where most modern complexity lives.
- **FPGA (Field-Programmable Gate Array):** a chip full of *reconfigurable* logic. You load your design onto it like loading software, and it *becomes* that hardware, then you can wipe it and load a different design. Slower and less efficient than an ASIC, but you can actually run your design *today* on a $100-300 board without a $30M manufacturing run. **This is how you'll actually execute the hardware you design.** More on this later.

The same Verilog can target an FPGA (to prototype and run) or an ASIC (to manufacture in volume). That's the magic: **one design language, two destinations.**

## 📐 The ASIC/SoC design flow, end to end

Here's the whole journey from idea to working chip. We'll walk each stage, then zoom in on the ones a CS grad lives in.

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │                     THE CHIP DESIGN FLOW                          │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

   1. SPEC & ARCHITECTURE   "What should it do? How fast? How much power?"
            │               microarchitecture, block diagram, budgets
            ▼
   2. RTL DESIGN            Write the behavior in Verilog/SystemVerilog
            │               (register-transfer level - the "source code")
            ▼
   3. FUNCTIONAL            Does the RTL actually do what the spec says?
      VERIFICATION  ◄────┐  Testbenches, simulation, UVM, coverage,
            │            │  assertions.  *** BIGGEST CS-GRAD DOOR ***
            │  bugs ─────┘  (loops back to RTL until clean)
            ▼
   4. SYNTHESIS            RTL  →  a netlist of actual logic gates,
            │               mapped to the foundry's standard-cell library
            ▼
   5. PLACE & ROUTE         Put every gate at an (x,y) on the die and
      (physical design)     draw the metal wires connecting them
            │
            ▼
   6. TIMING CLOSURE        Static Timing Analysis (STA): does every
      (STA)                 signal arrive in time at every clock edge?
            │               Fix violations. Repeat. (the grind)
            ▼
   7. DFT                   Add Design-For-Test structures (scan chains)
            │               so the manufactured chip can be tested
            ▼
   8. SIGNOFF & TAPEOUT     Final checks (DRC, LVS, power, IR drop) →
            │               send the layout (GDSII) to the foundry (TSMC…)
            ▼
   9. FABRICATION           The foundry makes wafers (see Chapter 17.5)
            │               weeks to months, millions of dollars
            ▼
  10. BRING-UP & TEST       First silicon arrives. Power it on. Does it
                            work? Debug. Maybe respin. Then mass production.
```

Let's walk each stage in plain English.

**1. Spec & architecture.** Someone decides *what the chip does* and the constraints: throughput, clock frequency, power budget, silicon area (which drives cost), interfaces. Architects then design the **microarchitecture**, the block diagram of how to actually achieve it (how many cores, pipeline depth, cache sizes, bus widths). Output: documents and diagrams, not yet code. This is where computer architecture knowledge (pipelining, caches, memory hierarchy) pays off, see [Systems / Memory](../05-Systems/05.1-memory.md).

**2. RTL design.** Engineers write the design in an HDL at the **register-transfer level (RTL)**, describing how data moves between registers each clock cycle and what logic transforms it. This is the "source code" of the chip. RTL is the contract: everything downstream is a transformation *of* this RTL.

**3. Functional verification.** The question: *does the RTL match the spec?* This is where **40-70% of total project effort goes**, verification engineers outnumber designers, often 2:1 or 3:1. They build **testbenches** (software that drives inputs into the design and checks outputs), run **simulations**, write **assertions** (machine-checkable claims about correctness), and track **coverage** (have we exercised every case?). A bug found here costs an engineer an afternoon. The same bug found *after tapeout* costs a multi-million-dollar respin and months of delay. That asymmetry is why verification is so heavily staffed, and why it's the **best entry point for someone with a software background.**

**4. Synthesis.** A tool (Synopsys **Design Compiler** / **Fusion Compiler**, Cadence **Genus**) reads the RTL and *compiles* it into a **netlist**, a list of real logic gates (AND, OR, flip-flops, muxes) chosen from the foundry's **standard-cell library**, wired together to implement the RTL's behavior. This is conceptually like a compiler turning C into assembly: RTL is the high-level intent, the gate netlist is the "machine code" of hardware. Synthesis also does first-pass timing and area optimization.

**5. Place & route (physical design).** Now the abstract gates need physical homes. **Placement** decides where on the 2D die each of the (often billions of) gates sits. **Routing** draws the actual metal wires, across many stacked metal layers, connecting them. Tools: Cadence **Innovus**, Synopsys **IC Compiler II / Fusion Compiler**. This is a staggering optimization problem (think VLSI Tetris with electromagnetic constraints).

**6. Timing closure (Static Timing Analysis).** A digital chip runs on a **clock**. Every clock tick, signals must travel from one flip-flop, through some logic, and arrive at the next flip-flop *before the next tick*. **STA** (Synopsys **PrimeTime**) checks this for *every* path in the design without simulating, it adds up gate and wire delays mathematically. If a path is too slow (a **setup violation**) or too fast (a **hold violation**), the chip won't work. Fixing these, "closing timing", is a famous grind. (We'll formalize setup/hold time in 17.6's interview prep; it's a top interview topic.)

**7. DFT (Design For Test).** You can't probe billions of internal transistors directly, so designers add **scan chains**, stitching the chip's flip-flops into long shift registers so test equipment can load known states in and read results out. Plus **BIST** (built-in self-test) for memories. Without DFT you can't tell a good chip from a defective one at the factory. (Tools: Siemens **Tessent**, Synopsys **TestMAX**.)

**8. Signoff & tapeout.** Final automated checks: **DRC** (Design Rule Check, does the layout obey the foundry's geometric rules?), **LVS** (Layout vs Schematic, does the physical layout match the netlist?), power and **IR-drop** analysis, electromigration. When everything passes, the design is exported as a **GDSII** (or OASIS) file, the photomask blueprint, and **taped out**: sent to the foundry. (The name is historical: designs used to ship on magnetic tape.)

**9. Fabrication.** The foundry (TSMC, Samsung, Intel) turns the GDSII into physical wafers through photolithography and the rest of the fab loop, that's all of [Chapter 17.5](17.5-semiconductor-manufacturing.md). Takes weeks to months and is extraordinarily expensive, which is *why* verification and signoff are so paranoid.

**10. Bring-up & test.** "First silicon" arrives in the lab. Engineers power it up on a test board and find out if it works. Some bugs only show up here (analog issues, rare corner cases). Fixable bugs that require changing the silicon need a **respin**, back through the flow, more months, more millions. Once it works and yields well, it goes to mass production.

> 💼 **On the job:** you don't do all ten stages. Teams are specialized. A **design verification engineer** lives in stage 3. A **physical design engineer** lives in 5-6. A **DFT engineer** owns 7. An **EDA software engineer** at Synopsys/Cadence *builds the tools* used in 4-8. Knowing where you sit, and how your stage hands off to the next, is most of what "knowing the flow" means in an interview.

## 📐 What an HDL actually is (and why it's not software)

An **HDL (Hardware Description Language)** lets you describe digital hardware in text. The two that matter:

- **Verilog / SystemVerilog**, dominant in the US and at most companies you'd target. SystemVerilog is Verilog plus a huge verification feature set (it's really two languages: a design language and a verification language). **Learn this first.**
- **VHDL**, common in Europe, defense, aerospace. More verbose, strongly typed. Worth recognizing; learn it only if a target employer uses it.

The core mental shift, stated precisely:

| Software (Python/C++) | Hardware (Verilog) |
|---|---|
| Statements run **sequentially** | Hardware blocks run **concurrently, always** |
| `x = x + 1` happens *at a moment* | A circuit *continuously* computes its outputs |
| One thread of control (mostly) | Massive inherent parallelism |
| `for` loop = repeat over time | `for` loop = **replicate hardware in space** (unrolled) |
| Variables live in memory | Signals are *physical wires* and *flip-flops* |
| Cost = time/memory | Cost = **area, power, timing** |

That last row is the deepest. In software, a slow function is annoying. In hardware, every operation you describe becomes *physical gates that take up area, burn power, and add delay.* A multiply isn't free, it's a big block of silicon. Writing good RTL means constantly thinking "what hardware does this line synthesize into?"

**Two kinds of logic** you'll describe, and they map directly onto [Boolean logic](../01-Foundations/01.2-boolean-logic.md):

- **Combinational logic:** output is a pure function of current inputs, no memory. AND/OR/NOT, adders, muxes. Like a pure function with no state. Change an input, the output changes (after a tiny propagation delay).
- **Sequential logic:** has *memory*, it remembers state and updates on a **clock edge**. The building block is the **flip-flop** (a 1-bit memory cell). Counters, FSMs, registers, pipelines are all sequential. State + clock is what makes a circuit able to *do something over time*.

```
  COMBINATIONAL                         SEQUENTIAL
  inputs ─►[ logic ]─► output           inputs ─►[ logic ]─►[ FF ]─► output
           (no memory)                                       ▲
                                                        clk ──┘
                                          output updates only on clock edge,
                                          and the FF *remembers* between edges
```

## 📐 Baseline Verilog: the syntax you actually need

Let's build up the vocabulary, then write real, simulatable modules.

**A module** is the unit of hardware, like a function/class, it has a name, inputs, and outputs, and can contain other modules.

```verilog
module and_gate (
    input  wire a,      // input ports
    input  wire b,
    output wire y       // output port
);
    assign y = a & b;   // continuous assignment: y is ALWAYS a AND b
endmodule
```

`assign` creates **combinational** logic, `y` continuously tracks `a & b`. It's a wire, permanently driven by that expression.

**`wire` vs `reg`**, the classic beginner trap:
- A **`wire`** is a physical connection driven by `assign` or by a module output. It has no memory.
- A **`reg`** is a variable assigned *inside* a procedural block (`always`/`initial`). The name is misleading: a `reg` does **not** necessarily become a hardware register! Whether it becomes a flip-flop or just combinational logic depends on *how* you write the `always` block. (SystemVerilog cleans this up with `logic`, which you can use almost everywhere, prefer it.)

**`always` blocks** describe procedural behavior. Two patterns cover 95% of designs:

```verilog
// COMBINATIONAL: sensitive to ALL inputs (@*), use blocking '='
always @(*) begin
    y = a & b;          // recomputes whenever a or b change
end

// SEQUENTIAL: triggered by a CLOCK EDGE, use non-blocking '<='
always @(posedge clk) begin
    q <= d;             // q takes d's value at the rising clock edge
end
```

**Blocking (`=`) vs non-blocking (`<=`)**, the single most important Verilog rule, and a guaranteed interview question:

- **Blocking `=`:** executes immediately and in order, like normal software assignment. **Use in combinational `always @(*)` blocks.**
- **Non-blocking `<=`:** all right-hand sides are evaluated first, *then* all left-hand sides update simultaneously at the clock edge. This models how real flip-flops update *together* on one edge. **Use in sequential `always @(posedge clk)` blocks.**

The golden rule, memorize it:

```
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Combinational (always @*)   →  use  =   (blocking)      │
  │  Sequential   (always @posedge)→ use  <=  (non-blocking) │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Why it matters: in sequential logic, suppose `a <= b; b <= a;`. With non-blocking, both RHS are read first, so this **swaps** a and b, exactly like two flip-flops swapping on a clock edge. With blocking, `a = b` then `b = a` would just make both equal to b, wrong hardware. Mixing them up produces designs that *simulate fine but synthesize to broken silicon*, or vice versa. This bug class is why the rule is drilled so hard.

> ⚠️ **Common mistakes**
> - Using `<=` in combinational blocks or `=` in clocked blocks, produces simulation/synthesis mismatches.
> - Incomplete sensitivity lists (`always @(a)` when you meant `@(a or b)`), use `always @(*)` and let the tool figure it out.
> - Forgetting that a `for` loop in synthesizable RTL **unrolls into parallel hardware**, a loop to 1,000,000 makes a million copies of logic, not a fast loop.
> - Treating `reg` as "this is a register." It's just "assigned in a procedural block." Whether it's a flip-flop depends on the block.
> - Latches you didn't ask for: an `always @(*)` that doesn't assign an output on *every* path infers a **latch** (memory you didn't want). Always assign a default first.

## 💻 Build it - simulate real Verilog with FREE tools

Here's the part to love: you can run actual hardware simulation on your laptop, no expensive licenses. The free open-source toolchain:

- **Icarus Verilog** (`iverilog`), compiles and simulates Verilog.
- **GTKWave**, views the resulting waveforms (signals over time).
- **Verilator**, a faster simulator that compiles Verilog to C++ (great for big designs and when you want to drive tests from C++/Python). Start with Icarus; graduate to Verilator.

**Install (macOS):**
```bash
brew install icarus-verilog gtkwave verilator
# verify
iverilog -V
```

**Install (Ubuntu/Debian or WSL):**
```bash
sudo apt update
sudo apt install -y iverilog gtkwave verilator
```

### Module 1 - a 4-bit counter

`counter.v`:
```verilog
// A 4-bit up-counter with synchronous reset and enable.
module counter #(parameter WIDTH = 4) (
    input  wire             clk,    // clock
    input  wire             rst,    // synchronous active-high reset
    input  wire             en,     // count enable
    output reg  [WIDTH-1:0] count   // current count value
);
    always @(posedge clk) begin     // sequential: updates on rising edge
        if (rst)
            count <= 0;             // non-blocking: this is a register
        else if (en)
            count <= count + 1;     // wraps automatically at 2^WIDTH
    end
endmodule
```

Now the **testbench**, this is *software that tests hardware*, and it's exactly the kind of code a software background makes you good at. `counter_tb.v`:
```verilog
`timescale 1ns/1ps          // time unit / precision for the simulator
module counter_tb;
    reg        clk = 0, rst = 1, en = 0;
    wire [3:0] count;

    // Instantiate the Device Under Test (DUT)
    counter dut (.clk(clk), .rst(rst), .en(en), .count(count));

    // Clock generator: toggle every 5ns -> 10ns period -> 100 MHz
    always #5 clk = ~clk;

    // Stimulus + checking
    initial begin
        $dumpfile("counter.vcd");   // waveform output for GTKWave
        $dumpvars(0, counter_tb);

        #12 rst = 0; en = 1;        // release reset, start counting
        // let it run and self-check
        repeat (20) @(posedge clk);
        // simple check: after 17 enabled edges from 0, value should be 17 % 16 = 1
        if (count !== 4'd1)
            $display("FAIL: expected 1, got %0d", count);
        else
            $display("PASS: counter wrapped correctly, count=%0d", count);

        en = 0; @(posedge clk);     // pause counting
        rst = 1; @(posedge clk);    // reset
        if (count !== 0) $display("FAIL: reset, got %0d", count);
        else             $display("PASS: reset works");

        $finish;
    end

    // log every cycle (a poor man's trace; verification scales this up)
    always @(posedge clk) $display("t=%0t  rst=%b en=%b  count=%0d", $time, rst, en, count);
endmodule
```

**Run it:**
```bash
iverilog -o counter.vvp counter.v counter_tb.v   # compile design + testbench
vvp counter.vvp                                  # simulate (prints PASS/FAIL)
gtkwave counter.vcd                              # open the waveform viewer
```

In GTKWave you drag `clk`, `rst`, `en`, `count` into the wave window and *see* the count stepping up on each rising clock edge, wrapping from 15 to 0. Seeing the waveform is the moment hardware stops being abstract.

> 💼 Notice what the testbench *is*: setup, drive stimulus, observe outputs, compare against expected, report PASS/FAIL, log a trace. That's unit testing. Your instinct to write `assert`s, fuzz inputs, and build a harness is *exactly* the verification mindset, just in a different language. This is why the door is open for you.

### Module 2 - a finite-state machine (traffic light)

FSMs are the heart of control logic. Let's build a traffic-light controller, a clean three-state FSM. The standard, readable structure is **three blocks**: state register, next-state logic, output logic.

`traffic.v`:
```verilog
module traffic (
    input  wire clk,
    input  wire rst,
    output reg  [1:0] light   // 00=RED, 01=GREEN, 10=YELLOW
);
    // State encoding
    localparam RED = 2'd0, GREEN = 2'd1, YELLOW = 2'd2;
    reg [1:0] state, next;
    reg [3:0] timer;          // cycles spent in current state

    // 1) STATE REGISTER (sequential): hold state, advance timer
    always @(posedge clk) begin
        if (rst) begin
            state <= RED;
            timer <= 0;
        end else if (timer == 0) begin
            state <= next;    // change state when timer expires
            timer <= dwell(next);
        end else begin
            timer <= timer - 1;
        end
    end

    // 2) NEXT-STATE LOGIC (combinational): RED -> GREEN -> YELLOW -> RED
    always @(*) begin
        case (state)
            RED:    next = GREEN;
            GREEN:  next = YELLOW;
            YELLOW: next = RED;
            default:next = RED;
        endcase
    end

    // 3) OUTPUT LOGIC (combinational): output depends only on state (Moore machine)
    always @(*) begin
        case (state)
            RED:    light = 2'b00;
            GREEN:  light = 2'b01;
            YELLOW: light = 2'b10;
            default:light = 2'b00;
        endcase
    end

    // helper: how long to dwell in each state (in cycles)
    function [3:0] dwell(input [1:0] s);
        case (s)
            RED:    dwell = 4'd5;
            GREEN:  dwell = 4'd4;
            YELLOW: dwell = 4'd1;
            default:dwell = 4'd5;
        endcase
    endfunction
endmodule
```

That three-block pattern, **(1) clocked state register, (2) combinational next-state, (3) combinational output**, is the canonical FSM template. Memorize it; it's both good practice and an interview expectation. A **Moore** machine's outputs depend only on the current state (like this one); a **Mealy** machine's outputs also depend on inputs (fewer states, but outputs can glitch). Knowing the difference is a classic question.

A quick testbench `traffic_tb.v`:
```verilog
`timescale 1ns/1ps
module traffic_tb;
    reg clk = 0, rst = 1;
    wire [1:0] light;
    traffic dut (.clk(clk), .rst(rst), .light(light));
    always #5 clk = ~clk;
    initial begin
        $dumpfile("traffic.vcd"); $dumpvars(0, traffic_tb);
        #12 rst = 0;
        repeat (60) @(posedge clk);   // watch several full cycles
        $finish;
    end
    always @(posedge clk)
        $display("t=%0t light=%s", $time,
                 light==2'b00 ? "RED" : light==2'b01 ? "GREEN" : "YELLOW");
endmodule
```
```bash
iverilog -o traffic.vvp traffic.v traffic_tb.v && vvp traffic.vvp
gtkwave traffic.vcd
```
You should see RED for 5 cycles, GREEN for 4, YELLOW for 1, repeating. If it does, you've built a working sequential controller in real HDL.

## 📐 The verification world (where you'd actually get hired)

Functional verification deserves its own section because it's both the **biggest stage** and the **best fit for a software person.** The job: *prove the RTL is correct before it's manufactured.* The toolkit:

- **Testbenches**, software that surrounds the design under test (DUT), drives inputs, and observes outputs. Simple ones are directed (hand-written cases like above). Serious ones are *constrained-random*.
- **Simulation**, running the DUT + testbench in an event simulator (Icarus/Verilator free; Synopsys **VCS**, Cadence **Xcelium**, Siemens **Questa** in industry).
- **SystemVerilog & UVM**, modern verification uses **UVM (Universal Verification Methodology)**, a SystemVerilog class library (think: a standardized OOP framework for building reusable testbenches). UVM testbenches have **drivers** (turn abstract transactions into pin wiggles), **monitors** (observe the bus), **scoreboards** (a reference model comparing expected vs actual), **sequences** (generate randomized stimulus), and **agents** that bundle these. If you know OOP design patterns from software, **UVM will feel familiar**, it's factories, callbacks, and inheritance applied to test infrastructure.
- **Constrained-random verification**, instead of hand-writing every case, you describe *legal* random inputs (constraints) and let the tool generate thousands of scenarios you'd never think of. This is fuzzing for hardware.
- **Functional coverage**, since random tests are blind, you define *coverage points* ("did we ever test a full FIFO? an overflow? back-to-back transactions?") and measure what fraction got hit. Verification is "done" when coverage closes, not when tests pass.
- **Assertions (SVA, SystemVerilog Assertions)**, inline, machine-checkable claims like "a request is always followed by an acknowledge within 5 cycles." They catch bugs at the moment of violation, deep inside the design, instead of as a mysterious wrong output later.
- **Formal verification**, instead of simulating, a tool *mathematically proves* properties hold for *all* inputs (or finds a counterexample). Adjacent to a discrete-math/logic background.

```
        A UVM-style testbench (the verification mental model)
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  SEQUENCE  ─► DRIVER ─►┌─────────┐                            │
   │  (random   transactions│   DUT   │── outputs ─► MONITOR ──┐   │
   │   stimulus)            │  (RTL)  │              (observe)  │  │
   │                        └─────────┘                         ▼  │
   │   REFERENCE MODEL ────────────────────────────►  SCOREBOARD   │
   │   (golden expected)                              (compare!)   │
   │                          COVERAGE  ◄── did we test enough? ───┘
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

> 💼 **Why this is the door for you:** verification is ~50-70% of chip-design headcount, it's chronically understaffed, and it's *fundamentally a software job*, you write SystemVerilog/UVM (OOP), Python/Perl/Tcl automation, build regression flows, parse logs, analyze coverage data, debug. A data/ML and telemetry background (instrumenting systems, analyzing signals, finding the one bad record) maps *directly* onto coverage analysis and debug. We'll come back to concrete prep in [17.6](17.6-semiconductor-careers.md).

## 📐 FPGA vs ASIC - and why FPGA is your on-ramp

| | **FPGA** | **ASIC** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Reconfigurable chip you "program" | Fixed custom silicon |
| Cost to start | $100-300 dev board | $1M-$100M+ (masks, fab) |
| Time to running design | Minutes (synthesize + load) | Months (full flow + fab) |
| Per-unit cost | Higher | Much lower at volume |
| Speed / power efficiency | Lower | Much higher |
| Changeable? | Yes, reload anytime | No, frozen forever |
| Best for | Prototyping, low volume, learning | Mass production, max performance |

The punchline for *learning and portfolio-building*: **an FPGA lets you actually run the hardware you design, today, on cheap hardware.** You write the same Verilog, run it through the FPGA vendor's free tools (AMD/Xilinx **Vivado**, Intel **Quartus**, or the open-source **Yosys + nextpnr** flow), and load it onto a board where it becomes real, running logic driving real LEDs and pins.

**Beginner FPGA board path** (pick one, cheapest-first):
- **Tang Nano 9K / 20K** (~$15-40, Gowin, fully open-source toolchain), superb cheap entry.
- **iCEBreaker / TinyFPGA** (Lattice iCE40, 100% open-source Yosys flow), great for the FOSS purist.
- **Digilent Arty A7 / Basys 3** (AMD/Xilinx, Vivado), industry-standard tools, what employers use; the most "resume-real" choice.

A great learning arc: blink an LED → debounce a button → drive a 7-segment display → build a UART → implement your traffic-light FSM on real hardware → a simple CPU. Each is a GitHub-able portfolio piece (see the mini-project and [17.6](17.6-semiconductor-careers.md)).

## 📐 The EDA tool ecosystem (who makes what)

**EDA (Electronic Design Automation)** is the software that runs every stage above. It's a ~$20.8B (2026) industry and a tight oligopoly: **Synopsys (~31%)**, **Cadence (~30%)**, **Siemens EDA (~13%)**, together over 70% of the market. (Synopsys completed its ~$35B acquisition of Ansys in July 2025, folding in multiphysics simulation.) High barrier to entry, deeply technical, and *full of software/R&D jobs*, these companies are essentially specialized software houses. Mapping tools to flow stages:


> 💼 **EDA software engineering** is a distinct, excellent career for a strong programmer: you build the C++/Python/Tcl tools that the whole industry runs on, graph algorithms, optimization, compilers (RTL is compiled!), simulation engines, and increasingly **ML-for-EDA** (using machine learning to speed up placement, routing, and verification, a perfect intersection if you have an ML background). ## 🧪 Mini-project - design + verify a 4-function ALU

Let's build something an interviewer would respect: a small **ALU** (Arithmetic Logic Unit), combinational logic that, given two operands and an opcode, produces a result and status flags. This ties straight back to the ALU you built in Python in [Boolean Logic](../01-Foundations/01.2-boolean-logic.md), now in real HDL.

**Spec:** 8-bit operands `a`, `b`; 2-bit `op`; outputs `result` (8-bit), `zero` flag (result==0), `carry` flag.
```
  op  operation
  00  ADD   (a + b)         carry = bit 8 of the sum
  01  SUB   (a - b)         carry = borrow
  10  AND   (a & b)
  11  OR    (a | b)
```

`alu.v`:
```verilog
module alu (
    input  wire [7:0] a, b,
    input  wire [1:0] op,
    output reg  [7:0] result,
    output wire       zero,
    output reg        carry
);
    reg [8:0] tmp;                 // 9 bits to capture carry-out
    always @(*) begin
        carry = 1'b0;
        case (op)
            2'b00: begin tmp = a + b;        result = tmp[7:0]; carry = tmp[8]; end
            2'b01: begin tmp = {1'b0,a} - {1'b0,b}; result = tmp[7:0]; carry = tmp[8]; end // borrow
            2'b10: begin result = a & b;     end
            2'b11: begin result = a | b;     end
            default: result = 8'b0;
        endcase
    end
    assign zero = (result == 8'b0);
endmodule
```

**Self-checking testbench** `alu_tb.v`, and here's where your software testing instincts shine: you'll check against a *reference* computed in the testbench, the hardware analog of comparing against Python's own arithmetic.
```verilog
`timescale 1ns/1ps
module alu_tb;
    reg  [7:0] a, b;
    reg  [1:0] op;
    wire [7:0] result;
    wire       zero, carry;
    integer i, errors = 0;
    reg  [7:0] expect;

    alu dut (.a(a), .b(b), .op(op), .result(result), .zero(zero), .carry(carry));

    task check;  // compare DUT against a golden reference
        begin
            case (op)
                2'b00: expect = a + b;
                2'b01: expect = a - b;
                2'b10: expect = a & b;
                2'b11: expect = a | b;
            endcase
            #1; // let combinational logic settle
            if (result !== expect) begin
                $display("FAIL op=%b a=%0d b=%0d : got %0d expected %0d", op,a,b,result,expect);
                errors = errors + 1;
            end
        end
    endtask

    initial begin
        $dumpfile("alu.vcd"); $dumpvars(0, alu_tb);
        // constrained-random style: throw many random cases at it
        for (i = 0; i < 1000; i = i + 1) begin
            a  = $random; b = $random; op = $random;
            check;
        end
        if (errors == 0) $display("ALL %0d RANDOM TESTS PASSED", i);
        else             $display("%0d FAILURES", errors);
        $finish;
    end
endmodule
```
```bash
iverilog -o alu.vvp alu.v alu_tb.v && vvp alu.vvp
```

**Extensions** (each makes the portfolio piece stronger):
1. Add operations: XOR, shift-left, shift-right, comparison (set-less-than).
2. Add an **overflow** flag for signed addition (a real interview topic).
3. Add a **SystemVerilog assertion**: `assert property (@(*) op==2'b10 |-> result == (a & b));`
4. Write a **functional coverage** model: did you test op=ADD with carry-out? SUB with borrow? a==b? Track coverage like you'd track test coverage in software.
5. Synthesize it for an FPGA board and drive the result onto LEDs with switches as inputs, now it's *running hardware* you can demo.

A clean, well-tested ALU with random verification, assertions, and coverage, pushed to GitHub with a README explaining the flow, is a genuinely strong entry-level verification portfolio piece.

## ✏️ Exercises

**1.** You write `always @(posedge clk) begin a <= b; b <= a; end`. What are `a` and `b` after one clock edge if they started as 3 and 7? What if you'd used blocking `=` instead?

**2.** Explain why a `for (i=0;i<8;i=i+1)` loop in *synthesizable* RTL is fundamentally different from the same loop in Python. What physically happens?

**3.** Your combinational block is `always @(*) begin if (sel) y = a; end`. What unintended hardware did you just create, and how do you fix it?

**4.** Classify each as combinational or sequential: (a) a 32-bit adder, (b) a 4-bit counter, (c) a multiplexer, (d) a UART receiver, (e) a NAND gate, (f) a shift register.

**5.** In one sentence each, what happens at: synthesis, place-and-route, static timing analysis, tapeout?

**6.** Why are there typically *more* verification engineers than design engineers on a chip project? Give the economic argument.

**7.** You want to *run* your traffic-light FSM on physical hardware this weekend for under $50. What do you buy and what toolchain do you use?

### Solutions

**1.** With non-blocking `<=`, both RHS are sampled first (a's old 3, b's old 7), then both update: **a=7, b=3, they swap.** This models two flip-flops swapping on the edge. With blocking `=`: `a = b` makes a=7, then `b = a` makes b=7, **both become 7.** This is precisely why sequential logic uses non-blocking; the swap is the canonical demonstration.

**2.** In Python the loop *executes 8 times over time* on one piece of hardware (the CPU). In synthesizable RTL the loop is **unrolled at compile time into 8 parallel copies of the logic**, it replicates hardware in *space*, all operating simultaneously in one clock cycle. A loop bound that's large makes a large *area* of silicon, not a slow program.

**3.** Because `y` is only assigned when `sel` is true, the tool infers a **latch** to "remember" `y` when `sel` is false, unintended memory in combinational logic, a classic bug. Fix: assign a default first, `always @(*) begin y = 1'b0; if (sel) y = a; end`, so `y` has a value on every path.

**4.** Combinational: (a) adder, (c) mux, (e) NAND gate. Sequential (has memory/clock): (b) counter, (d) UART receiver (it has state, tracking bits over time), (f) shift register.

**5.** *Synthesis:* compile RTL into a gate-level netlist mapped to a standard-cell library. *Place-and-route:* assign each gate a physical (x,y) on the die and draw the metal wires connecting them. *STA:* mathematically verify every signal arrives within the clock period at every flip-flop (no setup/hold violations). *Tapeout:* export the final layout (GDSII) and send it to the foundry to manufacture.

**6.** A design bug caught in *verification* costs an engineer-afternoon to fix. The *same* bug escaping to silicon costs a respin: new masks, another fab run, and *months* of schedule slip, millions of dollars and possibly a missed market window. Verification is cheap insurance against a catastrophically expensive failure, so it's worth staffing heavily (often 2-3 verification engineers per designer).

**7.** Buy a **Tang Nano 9K (~$15-25)** or an **iCEBreaker (~$70, a bit over, but fully open)**. Use the **open-source toolchain (Yosys for synthesis, nextpnr for place-and-route, and the vendor's loader)**, or the Tang's Gowin tools, to synthesize the `traffic.v` Verilog and load it onto the board, wiring three LEDs to the `light` output. Now the FSM runs in real silicon you can hold.
