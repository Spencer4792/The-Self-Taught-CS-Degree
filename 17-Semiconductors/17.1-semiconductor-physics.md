## 🌱 Start here (the gentle on-ramp)

Forget physics for a second. Imagine a crowded theater.

A **conductor** (a metal like copper) is a theater where there are tons of empty seats *everywhere* and the aisles are wide open. People (electrons) can stroll around freely. Push them gently from one side, apply a voltage, and they flow easily. That flow is electric current. Metals conduct because their electrons are basically free to roam.

An **insulator** (glass, rubber, the plastic on a wire) is a theater that is *completely full*, every single seat taken, no aisles, nobody can move. Push as hard as you like; nobody budges. No current flows. That's why you can hold the plastic part of a cable safely.

A **semiconductor** (silicon) is the strange and wonderful middle case: it's a theater that's *almost completely full*, with just a *few* empty seats. At first glance it barely conducts at all. But here's the magic, because it's right on the knife's edge, we can *control* it. We can add a tiny pinch of impurity to create lots of empty seats (or lots of extra wandering people), and suddenly it conducts. We can apply a voltage to a nearby plate and *summon* a conducting path into existence, then make it vanish. **A material we can switch between "conducts" and "doesn't conduct" on command is a switch. And a computer is nothing but a colossal pile of switches.** That controllability is the entire ballgame.

The realization that reframed everything for me: copper is *too* generous (always conducts, can't turn off) and glass is *too* stingy (never conducts, can't turn on). Silicon is the Goldilocks material precisely because it's mediocre, and mediocre-but-controllable beats excellent-but-fixed every time you're trying to build logic.

Everything below is just making that theater picture precise: what the "seats" really are (energy levels), why silicon has *just* the right gap, and how we engineer the empty seats on purpose.

## 📐 Why silicon? The periodic table actually matters

Let me start with the atom, because the whole story is about electrons and where they're allowed to sit.

An atom is a positive nucleus surrounded by electrons. Electrons can't sit just anywhere, they occupy discrete **shells**, and what matters for chemistry and conduction is the *outermost* shell, the **valence** electrons. Atoms are "happiest" (lowest energy, most stable) when their outer shell is full. That drive to fill the outer shell is what makes atoms bond.

Now look at the periodic table, group by group (a group is a column):

```
        Group III   Group IV    Group V
        (3 valence) (4 valence) (5 valence)
        ─────────────────────────────────────
         Boron  B    Carbon  C    Nitrogen N
         Alumin Al   Silicon Si   Phosph.  P    <- the stars
         Gallium Ga  German. Ge   Arsenic  As
```

**Silicon sits in Group IV: it has exactly 4 valence electrons.** The outer shell wants 8. So each silicon atom reaches out and shares one electron with each of *four* neighbors, and each neighbor shares one back. Every atom ends up surrounded by 8 shared electrons, a full shell, maximum stability. These shared-electron bonds are **covalent bonds**, and they lock the atoms into a rigid, repeating 3D grid called a **crystal lattice** (specifically the diamond cubic structure, the same geometry as diamond, which is Group-IV carbon).

```
A 2D cartoon of the silicon lattice (each Si shares 4 bonds):

      Si = Si = Si = Si
      ‖    ‖    ‖    ‖
      Si = Si = Si = Si      each "=" or "‖" is a covalent bond,
      ‖    ‖    ‖    ‖        a PAIR of shared electrons holding
      Si = Si = Si = Si      two atoms together
```

In a *perfect, cold* silicon crystal, every valence electron is tied up in a bond. There are no free wanderers. So pure silicon is a poor conductor, almost an insulator. (Hold that thought; it's the "almost full theater.")

**Why Group IV specifically?** Group IV is the sweet spot for the *band gap* (next section). Group III/V elements next door are exactly what we'll use to dope it. And combining a Group III with a Group V (like gallium + arsenic → GaAs) gives "III-V" compound semiconductors used in LEDs, lasers, and high-frequency chips. The periodic table isn't trivia here, it's the parts catalog.

**Why not carbon (also Group IV)?** Carbon's band gap is huge (diamond is a great insulator) and it's hard to process. Why not germanium (also Group IV)? It works, the very first transistors were germanium, but its band gap is small, so it leaks current when warm. Silicon's gap is *just right*, and, crucially, silicon grows a beautiful, stable, insulating oxide (SiO₂, literally glass) when you heat it in oxygen. That homegrown insulator turns out to be the unsung hero of the whole industry (you'll see why in 17.2, the MOSFET gate). Plus silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust. It's *sand*. Cheap, abundant, well-behaved, perfect gap, free insulator. That's why silicon won.

```
💼 On the job: when people say "silicon" as shorthand for "the chip industry"
(Silicon Valley, "going to silicon"), this is the literal reason. The entire
$600B+ semiconductor industry rests on a Group-IV element's 4 valence electrons
and its conveniently grown oxide.
```

## 📐 Energy bands - the real reason for conductors, insulators, semiconductors

Now the central idea of the whole chapter. Get this and the rest follows.

In a *single* atom, electrons occupy sharp, discrete energy levels, like rungs on a ladder. Now bring ~10²³ silicon atoms together into a crystal. Quantum mechanics says no two electrons in the system can occupy the exact same state (the Pauli exclusion principle). So each sharp atomic level *splits* into an astronomically dense ladder of slightly-different levels, so dense that it smears into a continuous **band** of allowed energies. Between the bands sit **band gaps**, ranges of energy where there are simply *no allowed states*. An electron cannot have an energy that lands in the gap, the same way a radio can't tune to a frequency between two stations.

Two bands matter:

- The **valence band**: the band of energies occupied by the bonded valence electrons. Think "the seats in the orchestra section, the bonds."
- The **conduction band**: the next band up, the energies an electron needs to be *free to roam* the crystal. Think "the open balcony where you can walk around."

Between them: the **band gap**, of width `E_g` (in electron-volts, eV).

```
ENERGY
  ▲
  │   ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░  CONDUCTION BAND  (empty = free to move)
  │   ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░
  │   ─────────────────  ─┐
  │                       │  BAND GAP  E_g  (no allowed states)
  │   ─────────────────  ─┘
  │   ████████████████  VALENCE BAND  (full = electrons locked in bonds)
  │   ████████████████
  └───────────────────────────────────────
```

**Here's the rule that explains everything: current flows only when electrons can move into nearby empty states.** A completely full band carries no current (full theater, nobody can shift). A completely empty band carries no current (nobody there to move). You need a band that is *partly* full, electrons *and* empty states next to them. Now the three material classes are just three pictures of the gap:

```
   CONDUCTOR (metal)        INSULATOR              SEMICONDUCTOR (Si)
   ────────────────         ─────────────          ──────────────────
   ░░░░░░░░░░░░               ░░░░░░░░               ░░░░░░░░ (a few e⁻ here)
   ▓▓▓▓░░░░░░░░  ← partly     ────────               ──────── 
   ████████████    full        HUGE                    small  E_g≈1.1 eV
   ████████████  conduction    GAP                      gap
   (bands overlap;  & valence  ────────               ────────
    always conducts) touch     ████████               ███████░ (a few empty here)
                               ████████  full         ████████
```

- **Metal:** the valence and conduction bands *overlap*, so there are always free electrons in a partly-filled band. Always conducts. Can't be turned off. (Copper.)
- **Insulator:** a *huge* gap (`E_g ≈ 5-9 eV` for glass/diamond). At room temperature, essentially zero electrons have enough energy to jump it. The valence band stays full, conduction band stays empty. Never conducts. (Glass.)
- **Semiconductor:** a *small* gap. Silicon's is `E_g ≈ 1.1 eV`. Big enough that pure cold silicon barely conducts, but *small enough that we can mess with it*: heat, light, or (the killer app) added impurities can populate the conduction band. **The small gap is what makes silicon controllable.**

There's no hard line between "insulator" and "semiconductor", it's a continuum of gap size. We just call gaps small enough to manipulate at room temperature "semiconductors."

```
⚠️ Common misconception: "Silicon conducts well." No - pure silicon is a
*bad* conductor, only ~10⁻¹² as conductive as copper. Its superpower isn't
conducting; it's being controllable. We're about to spend the rest of the
chapter making it conduct on purpose, exactly where and when we want.
```

### Where does the energy to jump the gap come from? (thermal generation)

At absolute zero, every electron sits in the valence band and silicon is a perfect insulator. But at room temperature (~300 K), atoms jiggle with thermal energy. The *average* thermal energy is small, about `kT ≈ 0.026 eV`, where `k` is Boltzmann's constant, much less than the 1.1 eV gap. But energy is randomly distributed (the Boltzmann distribution), so a *tiny fraction* of electrons get a lucky kick big enough to leap the gap into the conduction band.

The probability scales like `exp(−E_g / 2kT)`. Plug in numbers: `E_g/(2kT) ≈ 1.1 / 0.052 ≈ 21`, and `e⁻²¹ ≈ 10⁻⁹`. So roughly one in a billion bonds is broken at any instant. That sounds like nothing, but silicon has ~10²² atoms per cm³, so you still get ~10¹⁰ free electrons per cm³. This temperature dependence is profound and practical:

- **Hotter → exponentially more carriers → more conduction.** Unlike a metal (which conducts *worse* when hot), an intrinsic semiconductor conducts *better* when hot. This is why chips need cooling and why "thermal runaway" is a real danger.
- It also tells us the gap energy `E_g` is the master parameter. Everything traces back to it.

## 📐 Electrons and holes - the two kinds of carriers

This is the idea that makes semiconductors weird and beautiful, so let me build it carefully.

When an electron absorbs enough energy and jumps from the valence band to the conduction band, two things happen, not one:

1. A free **electron** now roams the conduction band, a negative charge carrier.
2. It left behind an **empty spot** in the valence band, a missing electron in a bond.

That empty spot is a **hole**. And here's the magic: a neighboring valence electron can slide over to fill the hole, but in doing so it leaves a hole *where it just was*. The hole effectively *moved* in the opposite direction. Apply a voltage and electrons shuffle one way while the empty spot migrates the other way. **We track the moving empty spot as if it were a real, positive particle.** It isn't a particle, it's the *absence* of an electron, but it behaves exactly like a mobile positive charge, so we treat it as one.

```
Holes move like a bubble in water, or like an empty parking space in a packed lot:

  cars:  [C][C][ ][C][C][C]      the EMPTY space is at position 3
  step:  car from pos 2 rolls right →
  cars:  [C][ ][C][C][C][C]      now the empty space is at position 2

  The cars (electrons) shuffled RIGHT; the empty space (hole) moved LEFT.
  Watching the hole is way easier than tracking 5 cars. So we do.
```

So in a semiconductor there are **two** carriers:
- **Electrons** (charge `−q`) drifting in the conduction band.
- **Holes** (charge `+q`) drifting in the valence band.

In pure (intrinsic) silicon they're created in pairs, so electron count = hole count = `nᵢ` (the *intrinsic carrier concentration*, ≈ 10¹⁰ /cm³ at room temp). They also recombine: a wandering electron meets a hole, drops back into the bond, both vanish, releasing energy (sometimes as light, hold that thought for the LED).

**Why holes matter so much:** the entire transistor story is about engineering materials that have *mostly electrons* or *mostly holes*, and then putting them next to each other. Two carriers, two flavors of material, one junction between them, that's the diode, the LED, the solar cell, and the foundation of the transistor. Holes aren't a bookkeeping trick you can ignore; they're half the device physics.

## 📐 Doping - the master trick (intrinsic → extrinsic)

Pure silicon barely conducts and we can't steer it. The genius move, the thing that turned a curiosity into an industry, is **doping**: deliberately adding a *minuscule* amount of a different element to flood the crystal with one type of carrier. We're talking ratios like *one impurity atom per ten million silicon atoms*, a pinch of salt in an Olympic pool, yet it changes conductivity by a *million times*. Let me show why it works, using the valence-electron counting from before.

### n-type: add a Group V donor (extra electrons)

Take silicon (4 valence electrons) and replace an occasional atom with **phosphorus** (Group V, **5** valence electrons). Phosphorus slots into the lattice and uses 4 of its electrons to bond with the 4 silicon neighbors, just like a silicon atom would. But it has a **5th electron left over** with no bond to join. That extra electron is only weakly held; the tiniest thermal nudge frees it into the conduction band.

```
   Si = Si = Si
   ‖    ‖    ‖
   Si = P  = Si      P bonds 4 ways like Si, but its 5th electron
   ‖   (•)  ‖        (•) is loose - it pops free into the conduction band.
   Si = Si = Si      Result: a free ELECTRON, and a fixed +1 P⁺ ion.
```

Phosphorus *donates* an electron, so it's a **donor**. The material now has *way* more free electrons than holes. Electrons are the **majority carrier**; holes are the **minority carrier**. Because the mobile charge is **n**egative, we call it **n-type** silicon. Crucially, the material is still electrically *neutral* overall, each donated electron leaves behind a fixed, positively-charged phosphorus ion locked in the lattice. The charges balance; it's just that one of them can move and one can't.

### p-type: add a Group III acceptor (extra holes)

Now instead use **boron** (Group III, **3** valence electrons). Boron can only form 3 bonds with its silicon neighbors, there's a **missing bond**, a ready-made **hole**. A neighboring electron easily slides over to complete boron's bonds, creating a hole somewhere else. Boron *accepts* an electron, so it's an **acceptor**.

```
   Si = Si = Si
   ‖    ‖    ‖
   Si = B  - Si      B has only 3 bonds; one bond is INCOMPLETE (a hole, ○).
   ‖   (○)  ‖        An electron slides in to fill it, leaving a hole elsewhere.
   Si = Si = Si      Result: a free HOLE, and a fixed −1 B⁻ ion.
```

Now holes are the majority carrier, electrons the minority. Because the mobile charge acts **p**ositive, this is **p-type** silicon. Again neutral overall: each hole is balanced by a fixed negative boron ion.

### The law of mass action (the one formula to keep)

Doping doesn't just add majority carriers, it *suppresses* the minority. There's a beautiful equilibrium relation:

```
   n · p = nᵢ²              (always true at equilibrium)

   n = electron concentration,  p = hole concentration
   nᵢ = intrinsic concentration (~10¹⁰ /cm³ in Si at 300 K)
```

So if I dope n-type to `n ≈ 10¹⁷` electrons/cm³, then `p = nᵢ²/n ≈ (10¹⁰)²/10¹⁷ = 10³` holes/cm³. Ten million billion electrons, a thousand holes. *Overwhelmingly* electron-flavored. This product rule is why doping is such a clean knob: pick the majority concentration and the minority follows.

```
SUMMARY TABLE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
            dopant     group   majority   minority   fixed ion
n-type      P, As       V      electrons   holes      + (donor)
p-type      B, Ga      III     holes       electrons  − (acceptor)
```

This is the entire toolkit. Two materials, n-type and p-type, built by sprinkling Group-V or Group-III atoms into Group-IV silicon. Now we put them together, and a device is born.

```
💼 On the job: chip fabrication is largely the art of putting precise amounts
of dopant in precise places (via ion implantation and diffusion) at the
nanometer scale, billions of times, with near-zero defects. "Process nodes"
(7nm, 3nm, etc.) describe how small those doped features are.
```

## 📐 The P-N junction - the heart of everything

Take a piece of p-type silicon and a piece of n-type silicon and join them into one continuous crystal. The boundary is a **P-N junction**, and what happens there, purely from physics, no battery yet, is the foundation of every semiconductor device. Let me derive it slowly.

**Step 1, the setup.** On the p-side: lots of mobile holes (+), plus fixed negative acceptor ions. On the n-side: lots of mobile electrons (−), plus fixed positive donor ions.

```
     p-type                    n-type
  ⊕ ⊕ ⊕ ⊕ ⊕   |   ⊖ ⊖ ⊖ ⊖ ⊖      ⊕ = mobile hole   ⊖ = mobile electron
  (− fixed ions)|   (+ fixed ions)
```

**Step 2, diffusion.** There's a huge concentration difference across the boundary: tons of holes on the left, almost none on the right (and vice versa for electrons). Just like a drop of dye spreading in water, carriers **diffuse** from where they're crowded to where they're sparse. Holes wander right into the n-side; electrons wander left into the p-side. When an electron meets a hole, they **recombine** and both disappear.

**Step 3, the depletion region forms.** As carriers cross and recombine near the boundary, they leave behind the *fixed ions*, which can't move. The p-side near the junction is now stripped of holes, exposing **negative** acceptor ions. The n-side near the junction is stripped of electrons, exposing **positive** donor ions. This carrier-free zone of bare fixed charge is the **depletion region**.

```
      p-type             DEPLETION            n-type
  ⊕ ⊕ ⊕ ⊕  |  ─ ─ ─ ─ │ + + + + |  ⊖ ⊖ ⊖ ⊖
            |  (negative│(positive|
            |   fixed   │  fixed  |
            |   ions)   │  ions)  |
            └───────────┴─────────┘
              ◄── built-in E-field points this way ──
```

**Step 4, the built-in field and potential (the self-limiting part).** Now there's exposed negative charge on the p-side and positive charge on the n-side. That's a separation of charge → an **electric field** across the depletion region, pointing from n (+) to p (−). This field *pushes back* on diffusion: it shoves holes back toward p and electrons back toward n. Diffusion stops when the field's push exactly balances the diffusion drive. Equilibrium. The junction builds its own barrier.

The voltage across this barrier is the **built-in potential** `V_bi`:

```
   V_bi = (kT/q) · ln(Nₐ · N_d / nᵢ²)
```

where `Nₐ`, `N_d` are the doping concentrations. For typical silicon doping, `V_bi ≈ 0.6-0.7 V`. **Remember that number, 0.7 V, it's the famous "diode drop" and now you know it's just the built-in barrier of the junction.** Nothing magic; it's the height of the hill carriers must climb.

This is the key picture: **a P-N junction at rest has a built-in voltage barrier and a carrier-free depletion zone.** Now let's apply our own voltage and watch it become a one-way valve.

### Forward bias - opening the valve

Connect the battery's **+** terminal to the p-side and **−** to the n-side. This external voltage *opposes* the built-in field, it pushes holes from p and electrons from n *toward* the junction, **shrinking** the depletion region and **lowering** the barrier.

Once the applied voltage exceeds ~0.7 V (enough to mostly cancel `V_bi`), the barrier nearly vanishes and carriers **flood across** the junction. Current flows freely and *grows exponentially* with voltage. The valve is open.

```
  FORWARD BIAS (conducts):   p [+]──battery──[−] n
   barrier lowered, depletion thin, carriers pour across → big current
   ⊕⊕⊕→ ─│+ ←⊖⊖⊖     current flows easily once V > ~0.7 V
```

### Reverse bias - slamming the valve shut

Now flip it: **+** to the n-side, **−** to the p-side. This external voltage *aids* the built-in field, it pulls holes and electrons *away* from the junction, **widening** the depletion region and **raising** the barrier. Almost no majority carriers can cross. Only a tiny **leakage current** flows (from the few thermally-generated minority carriers). Essentially: off.

```
  REVERSE BIAS (blocks):   p [−]──battery──[+] n
   barrier raised, depletion wide, carriers pulled apart → ~zero current
   ⊕⊕⊕←   ──────│   →⊖⊖⊖    only tiny leakage
```

**That asymmetry, conducts one way, blocks the other, is a one-way valve for current. We call it a DIODE.** And it falls straight out of the junction physics. (Push too hard in reverse and the field rips electrons out of bonds in an avalanche, **breakdown**, but that's a separate regime; some diodes, Zeners, use it deliberately.)

## 📐 The diode equation (honest math, grounded)

The exponential flooding in forward bias and the trickle in reverse are captured by the **Shockley diode equation**, the single most important formula in this chapter:

```
   I = I_S · ( e^(V / (n·Vₜ)) − 1 )

   I    = current through the diode
   V    = voltage across it (positive = forward bias)
   I_S  = reverse saturation current (tiny leakage, ~1e-12 A for a small Si diode)
   Vₜ   = kT/q = "thermal voltage" ≈ 0.0258 V at room temperature (300 K)
   n    = ideality factor (≈1 ideal, ~1-2 real). NOTE: collides with electron
          count n from before - here it's a fudge factor. Context disambiguates.
```

Let me sanity-check it against the physics, because a formula you can't read is a black box:

- **Reverse bias** (`V` very negative): `e^(negative)` → ~0, so `I ≈ I_S·(0 − 1) = −I_S`. A tiny constant leakage in the reverse direction. ✓ matches "valve shut."
- **Zero bias** (`V = 0`): `e⁰ − 1 = 0`, so `I = 0`. No battery, no net current. ✓
- **Forward bias** (`V` positive, more than a few `Vₜ`): the `−1` becomes negligible and `I ≈ I_S·e^(V/Vₜ)`, **exponential explosion.** Every extra ~60 mV multiplies current by ~10. ✓ matches "valve open." This is why the diode "turns on" so sharply around 0.7 V: the exponential is nearly flat, then nearly vertical.

The thermal voltage `Vₜ = kT/q` ties current back to temperature, the same `kT` that controlled carrier generation. The physics is consistent end to end.

```
⚠️ Common misconception: "A diode turns on at exactly 0.7 V like a switch."
No - it's a smooth exponential. There's no hard threshold; current is flowing
(tiny) below 0.7 V and rising continuously. "0.7 V" is just where the curve
gets so steep that, on a normal current scale, it *looks* like a wall.
```

## 💻 Build it - simulate the diode I-V curve in Python

We can't fab a chip, but the diode equation is a model we *can* run. Let me plot the famous I-V curve and feel the exponential. (Honest disclaimer: this is the ideal Shockley model, real diodes add series resistance, high-injection effects, and breakdown. But the *shape* is real and it's what matters.)

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- physical constants & device params ---
q   = 1.602e-19      # electron charge (C)
k   = 1.381e-23      # Boltzmann constant (J/K)
T   = 300.0          # temperature (K)
Vt  = k * T / q      # thermal voltage ≈ 0.0258 V
I_S = 1e-12          # reverse saturation current (A) - a small Si diode
n   = 1.0            # ideality factor

def diode_current(V, I_S=I_S, n=n, Vt=Vt):
    """Shockley ideal-diode equation. A MODEL, not reality, but a faithful one."""
    return I_S * (np.exp(V / (n * Vt)) - 1.0)

# sweep voltage from a bit reverse to mild forward
V = np.linspace(-0.5, 0.8, 600)
I = diode_current(V)

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 5))
plt.plot(V, I * 1000)                      # current in milliamps
plt.axhline(0, color="gray", lw=0.5)
plt.axvline(0, color="gray", lw=0.5)
plt.title("Silicon diode I-V curve (ideal Shockley model)")
plt.xlabel("Voltage across diode  V (volts)")
plt.ylabel("Current  I (mA)")
plt.ylim(-1, 20)
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.show()

# the "knee": print current at a few voltages to SEE the exponential
for v in [0.0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.6, 0.65, 0.7, 0.75]:
    print(f"V = {v:.2f} V  ->  I = {diode_current(v)*1000:10.4f} mA")
```

Expected output (notice each +0.05 V multiplies current ~7x, that's the exponential):

```
V = 0.00 V  ->  I =     0.0000 mA
V = 0.30 V  ->  I =     0.0001 mA
V = 0.50 V  ->  I =     0.0241 mA
V = 0.60 V  ->  I =     1.1503 mA
V = 0.65 V  ->  I =     7.9432 mA
V = 0.70 V  ->  I =    54.857  mA      <- "turns on" right around here
V = 0.75 V  ->  I =   378.86   mA
```

See the "knee"? Below ~0.6 V the current is microscopic; above ~0.7 V it rockets. That smooth-but-steep wall *is* the 0.7 V diode drop, emergent from `e^(V/Vt)`, exactly as the physics predicted. **No magic threshold; just an exponential viewed on a linear axis.**

A quick second experiment, watch temperature shift the curve (warmer → conducts at lower voltage, because more carriers):

```python
for T_test in [250, 300, 350]:
    Vt_T = k * T_test / q
    # I_S itself rises steeply with T in reality; here we show just the Vt effect
    i = I_S * (np.exp(0.6 / (1.0 * Vt_T)) - 1.0)
    print(f"T = {T_test} K:  I(0.6V) = {i*1000:.4f} mA   (Vt = {Vt_T*1000:.2f} mV)")
```

## 💻 Build it - a half-wave rectifier (reasoning + simulation)

Here's the diode's first job in the real world: turning AC into DC. Wall power is **alternating current**, a sine wave swinging + and −, 50-60 times a second. Your laptop wants steady **direct current**. A diode, being a one-way valve, is the first step.

**Reason it through:** feed an AC sine into a diode. When the input is positive (and above ~0.7 V), the diode is forward-biased → it conducts → output follows the input. When the input is negative, the diode is reverse-biased → it blocks → output is ~0. So the diode **chops off the negative halves**, leaving only the positive humps. That's a **half-wave rectifier**, DC-ish (always ≥ 0), though lumpy.

```
  INPUT (AC):    /\      /\      /\          OUTPUT (half-wave rectified):
              __/  \____/  \____/  \__          /\      /\      /\
                 \  /    \  /    \  /          /  \    /  \    /  \
                  \/      \/      \/      __  /    \__/    \__/    \__
              (swings + and −)              (negative humps REMOVED, ≥0 only)
```

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

t   = np.linspace(0, 0.04, 2000)        # 40 ms = ~2 cycles of 50 Hz
Vin = 5.0 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 50 * t)   # 5 V peak AC, 50 Hz

# Ideal-ish diode model: conduct when forward-biased past ~0.7 V, else block.
Vdrop = 0.7
Vout  = np.where(Vin > Vdrop, Vin - Vdrop, 0.0)   # half-wave rectified

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.plot(t * 1000, Vin,  label="AC input",  alpha=0.6)
plt.plot(t * 1000, Vout, label="rectified output", lw=2)
plt.axhline(0, color="gray", lw=0.5)
plt.xlabel("time (ms)"); plt.ylabel("Volts")
plt.title("Half-wave rectifier: the diode removes the negative half")
plt.legend(); plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3); plt.show()
```

Notice the model bakes in the 0.7 V drop (`Vin − 0.7`), real diodes "cost" that built-in potential to push current through. A real power supply then adds a **smoothing capacitor** (fills the gaps between humps) and a regulator to get clean flat DC, but the one-way valve is the heart of it, and it's pure junction physics.

## 📐 A glimpse of cousins - LED, solar cell, BJT

The P-N junction is a *platform*. Tweak it and you get a whole family. Quick tours so you see the unity:

**LED (Light-Emitting Diode).** Recall that when an electron in the conduction band recombines with a hole, it drops in energy and *releases that energy*. In silicon that energy mostly becomes heat. But in certain **direct-bandgap** materials (the III-V compounds like gallium arsenide / gallium nitride), the released energy comes out as a **photon**, light! Forward-bias such a junction, pour electrons and holes into the junction so they recombine like crazy, and it *glows*. The photon's energy equals the band gap, so the gap sets the color: bigger gap → bluer light. An LED is a forward-biased junction engineered to emit. (The 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics went to the blue LED, the last color needed for white light.)

**Solar cell (photovoltaic).** Run the LED backwards conceptually. Shine light on a P-N junction: a photon with energy above the gap kicks an electron up to the conduction band, *creating an electron-hole pair*. The built-in field of the depletion region immediately sweeps the electron one way and the hole the other, separating them before they can recombine. That separated charge is a voltage you can drive a current with. **Light in → electricity out.** A solar panel is a vast array of reverse-operated junctions harvesting photon-made carrier pairs.

```
   LED:        electricity IN  → recombination → light OUT
   Solar cell: light IN → carrier pairs → field separates them → electricity OUT
   (Same junction, opposite direction. Beautiful symmetry.)
```

**BJT (Bipolar Junction Transistor).** Put *two* junctions back-to-back, three doped layers, like N-P-N, and you get the first transistor (1947, Bell Labs, the device that launched the information age). A tiny current into the thin middle layer (the **base**) controls a large current flowing from one end (**collector**) to the other (**emitter**). It's a current-controlled amplifier and switch. The BJT ruled early electronics, but for *digital logic* it was eventually dethroned by a different transistor, the **MOSFET**, which is voltage-controlled, sips far less power, and packs more densely. That's the star of the next chapter, and the reason your phone has billions of them.

The throughline: **everything is junctions.** Master the P-N junction and the whole zoo, diode, LED, solar cell, transistor, is variations on one theme.

## 🧪 Mini-project - full diode-circuit explorer

Put it together. Build a small tool that (1) plots the I-V curve, (2) finds the operating point of a diode in series with a resistor (a real circuit!), and (3) rectifies a signal. The interesting physics is part 2: in a real circuit the diode *and* a resistor share the supply voltage, and you solve for where they agree (the **load line** meets the diode curve).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq   # root-finder; pip install scipy

q, k, T = 1.602e-19, 1.381e-23, 300.0
Vt, I_S, n = k*T/q, 1e-12, 1.0

def diode_I(V):
    return I_S * (np.exp(V / (n*Vt)) - 1.0)

def operating_point(Vsupply, R):
    """
    Circuit:  Vsupply ── R ── diode ── ground.
    Same current flows through both. Resistor's voltage is Vsupply - Vd.
    Ohm's law on R:  I = (Vsupply - Vd) / R.
    Diode:           I = diode_I(Vd).
    Solve for Vd where the two currents are EQUAL (load line ∩ diode curve).
    """
    f = lambda Vd: diode_I(Vd) - (Vsupply - Vd) / R
    Vd = brentq(f, 0.0, Vsupply)          # find the voltage where they balance
    I  = diode_I(Vd)
    return Vd, I

for Vs, R in [(5.0, 1000), (5.0, 220), (3.3, 1000), (12.0, 470)]:
    Vd, I = operating_point(Vs, R)
    print(f"Supply={Vs:>4} V, R={R:>5} Ω  ->  Vdiode={Vd:.3f} V, "
          f"I={I*1000:6.2f} mA, V_R={Vs-Vd:.2f} V")
```

Expected (notice the diode "pins" itself near ~0.7 V across a *wide* range of supply/resistor, that voltage-clamping is *why* diodes are used as references):

```
Supply= 5.0 V, R= 1000 Ω  ->  Vdiode=0.700 V, I=  4.30 mA, V_R=4.30 V
Supply= 5.0 V, R=  220 Ω  ->  Vdiode=0.741 V, I= 19.36 mA, V_R=4.26 V
Supply= 3.3 V, R= 1000 Ω  ->  Vdiode=0.685 V, I=  2.62 mA, V_R=2.62 V
Supply=12.0 V, R=  470 Ω  ->  Vdiode=0.766 V, I= 24.0  mA, V_R=11.2 V
```

**The lesson:** crank the supply from 3.3 V to 12 V and the diode voltage barely budges (0.69 → 0.77 V) while the resistor soaks up the rest. The exponential I-V is so steep that the diode acts like a fixed ~0.7 V drop, exactly the intuition, now *derived* from solving the actual circuit. You've connected physics → equation → real circuit behavior, end to end.

**Extensions to try:** (a) add a smoothing capacitor to your rectifier and watch the ripple shrink; (b) sweep temperature and watch the I-V curve slide; (c) model a Zener diode by adding reverse breakdown at, say, −5.1 V.

## ✅ Teach it back

If I can explain these out loud without notes, the chapter stuck:

1. **Why silicon?** Group IV → 4 valence electrons → 4 covalent bonds → rigid crystal with a *small* band gap (~1.1 eV) we can control; plus it grows its own insulating oxide and it's literally sand. Not too conductive (copper), not too insulating (glass), controllable.
2. **Energy bands.** Valence band (full, bonded electrons) and conduction band (empty, free electrons) separated by a band gap. Metal: bands overlap (always conducts). Insulator: huge gap (never). Semiconductor: small gap (controllable). Current needs a *partly*-filled band, electrons *and* empty states.
3. **Electrons and holes.** Excite an electron across the gap → a free electron *and* a hole (the empty bond it left). Holes act like mobile positive charges. Two carriers, not one.
4. **Doping.** Add Group V (donor, e.g. P) → extra electrons → n-type. Add Group III (acceptor, e.g. B) → extra holes → p-type. A pinch changes conductivity a million-fold. `n·p = nᵢ²` always.
5. **P-N junction.** Carriers diffuse across, recombine, leave fixed ions → depletion region → built-in field → built-in potential (~0.7 V). Forward bias lowers the barrier (conducts, exponentially); reverse bias raises it (blocks). One-way valve = **diode**.
6. **Diode equation.** `I = I_S(e^(V/nVt) − 1)`. Reverse → −I_S (tiny leak); zero → 0; forward → exponential explosion. The ~0.7 V "turn-on" is just the steep part of the exponential, not a real wall.
7. **The family.** LED (recombination → light), solar cell (light → carrier pairs → current), BJT (two junctions, current-controlled). All junctions. Next chapter: the MOSFET, our voltage-controlled switch, the brick of every chip.

## ✏️ Exercises

**1.** Pure silicon has `nᵢ ≈ 10¹⁰ /cm³`. You dope it n-type with `N_d = 10¹⁶` donors/cm³. Estimate the electron and hole concentrations.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Nearly every donor gives up its electron, so `n ≈ N_d = 10¹⁶ /cm³`. By the law of mass action `n·p = nᵢ²`, the hole concentration is `p = nᵢ²/n = (10¹⁰)² / 10¹⁶ = 10²⁰/10¹⁶ = 10⁴ /cm³`. So 10¹⁶ electrons vs. 10⁴ holes, electrons outnumber holes by a *trillion* to one. Overwhelmingly n-type, and doping crushed the minority carriers. ✓
</details>

**2.** Why does an *intrinsic* semiconductor conduct *better* when heated, while a copper wire conducts *worse* when heated?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

In intrinsic silicon, conduction is limited by how many carriers exist, and that count grows *exponentially* with temperature (`∝ e^(−E_g/2kT)`), heat liberates more electron-hole pairs, so conductivity rises. In copper, the carriers are already abundant and fixed in number (it's a metal, partly-filled band); heating just makes the lattice atoms vibrate more, which *scatters* the electrons more, *raising* resistance. Different bottlenecks: carrier *count* (semiconductor) vs. carrier *scattering* (metal). ✓
</details>

**3.** A diode has `I_S = 1e-14 A`, `n = 1`, `Vt = 0.0258 V`. What forward voltage gives 1 mA? Then 10 mA? How much extra voltage per decade of current?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Solve `I = I_S e^(V/Vt)` for V: `V = Vt · ln(I/I_S)`.
For 1 mA: `V = 0.0258 · ln(1e-3 / 1e-14) = 0.0258 · ln(1e11) = 0.0258 · 25.33 = 0.654 V`.
For 10 mA: `V = 0.0258 · ln(1e-2 / 1e-14) = 0.0258 · ln(1e12) = 0.0258 · 27.63 = 0.713 V`.
Extra voltage per 10× current = `Vt · ln(10) = 0.0258 · 2.303 ≈ 0.0594 V ≈ 60 mV/decade`. That ~60 mV/decade is a famous number, it's why the curve looks like a wall, and (foreshadowing) the same `kT/q · ln(10)` limit shows up as the "subthreshold slope" floor in transistors. ✓
</details>

**4.** A reverse-biased diode passes ~`I_S` ≈ 1 pA of leakage. In a chip with a billion transistors each leaking a little, why does this matter, and what's it got to do with the band gap?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Leakage scales with thermally-generated minority carriers, which scale as `e^(−E_g/2kT)`. A billion tiny leaks add up to real standing power that's burned even when nothing is computing (static power). Smaller band gaps and higher temperatures both increase leakage exponentially, a core reason chips can't just be made of low-gap material or run arbitrarily hot, and a major headache as transistors shrink (we'll meet static power again in 17.2). The band gap you met in this chapter directly bounds how power-efficient a chip can be. ✓
</details>

**5.** Silicon's band gap is ~1.1 eV. A photon's energy is `E = hc/λ`. Roughly what wavelength of light can a silicon solar cell absorb, and is that visible?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

A photon must have energy ≥ `E_g` to kick an electron across. The cutoff: `λ = hc/E_g`. Using the handy shortcut `λ(nm) ≈ 1240 / E(eV)`, we get `λ ≈ 1240 / 1.1 ≈ 1127 nm`. So silicon absorbs everything with wavelength *shorter* than ~1127 nm, all of visible light (400-700 nm) plus some near-infrared. That's why silicon makes a good solar cell: its gap sits just below visible light, so it harvests the whole visible spectrum. The gap quite literally tunes what light a device sees. ✓
</details>
