# Chapter 30.5 - The Field Service and Startup Playbook: Installing a Fab Tool

> _Goal: Learn the job you will actually be hired to do first: getting a multi-ton CMP tool from a crate in a loading dock to a qualified, wafer-producing machine inside a customer's fab. Building on the tool anatomy and facilities picture of [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md), the electrical discipline of [30.1](30.1-electrical-engineering-for-the-fab.md), the chemical systems of [30.2](30.2-chemical-engineering-for-cmp.md), the mechanics of [30.3](30.3-mechanical-engineering-for-fab-tools.md), and the process metrics of [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md), you will learn the install lifecycle phase by phase, how to read the drawing set (hookup drawings, P&IDs, one-lines, interlock matrices), how to behave inside someone else's fab, and a troubleshooting method you can reuse for the rest of your career. You will build an install-campaign tracker with critical-path scheduling in Python, and a qualification-marathon analyzer that prints the report a customer signs._

The four chapters before this one taught you the disciplines: electricity, chemistry, mechanics, and process. This chapter is where they meet a shipping crate. A **field service / applications engineer (FSA)** at a vendor like Ebara or Applied Materials spends the first years of the job doing exactly what this chapter describes: standing in a half-built fab with a drawing set, a torque wrench, and a schedule, turning a delivered machine into a qualified one. It is the most hands-on chapter in the book, and deliberately the most reference-flavored: parts of it read like a playbook because on the job it is one. It is also, quietly, the best apprenticeship in the industry, because nobody ever knows a tool the way the person who assembled it does.

## Start here: the launch campaign

Think of a tool install as a spacecraft launch campaign.

A spacecraft does not get launched; it gets *campaigned*. Months before anything flies, engineers survey the pad, run power and fuel lines, and verify the ground systems. The vehicle arrives in pieces, in climate-controlled containers with shock recorders riding along. It is unpacked in a clean high bay, stacked, aligned, and bolted down. Then comes the long middle: umbilicals connected one at a time, each with its own signoff; power applied bus by bus; every valve cycled and every sensor checked against a procedure written by someone who has seen what happens when you skip a line. Only then is there a launch, one loud minute that outsiders think is the event. And even after launch the campaign is not over: the spacecraft flies a shakedown period, instruments are calibrated, anomalies are worked, and only when it has demonstrated stable performance does the operations team formally accept it and the launch crew go home.

A CMP tool install maps onto this almost line for line:

```text
  Launch campaign                      Tool install
  ---------------------------------   -----------------------------------
  pad survey, ground systems          pre-facilitation, hookup drawings
  vehicle ships in containers         crated tool, shock/tilt indicators
  stacking in the high bay            rigging, placement, leveling
  umbilicals + signoffs               facilities hookup (power, PCW, slurry)
  power applied bus by bus            power-on, subsystem bring-up order
  wet dress rehearsal                 dry cycling, robot teaching
  launch                              first wafer polished
  shakedown cruise                    qualification marathon
  handover to mission operations      customer signoff, training, punch list
```

Two properties of launch campaigns carry over exactly, and they organize everything in this chapter. First, the campaign is a sequence of **phases with gates**: each phase has entry criteria (what must be true before you start) and exit criteria (what must be demonstrated before you move on), and skipping a gate does not save time, it moves the failure later, where it costs more. Second, the campaign runs on a **critical path**: at any moment, one chain of tasks determines the end date, and everything else has slack. A good install engineer always knows which chain is critical this week, because that is where a lost day is a lost day forever. We will make that idea executable in the Build it section.

Keep the campaign picture through the whole chapter. First wafer is the launch. The marathon is the shakedown cruise. And the fab you are working in is someone else's launch site: you are a guest crew on their pad, working to their range safety rules.

## What an FSA actually does

An FSA is the vendor's person inside the customer's fab. [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md) explained the economics that create the job: a leading-edge fab is tens of billions of dollars of installed equipment running around the clock, a down tool burns money by the hour, and so vendors station engineers on site, sometimes permanently, because a four-hour response beats a four-day one by a margin the customer can compute in dollars. You are that response.

The work splits into two overlapping halves, and the FSA title deliberately straddles them:

- **Field service (FSE work)** owns hardware health: installs, upgrades, preventive maintenance, repairs, and the tool's uptime numbers. The output is a machine that runs.
- **Applications (apps work)** owns process results on that machine: baseline recipes, removal rate and uniformity, defect performance, helping the customer's process engineers get what [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md) calls the transfer function under control. The output is a machine that makes good wafers.

A pure FSE can hand off a tool that cycles perfectly and still polishes badly; a pure apps engineer can specify a recipe for a tool they could not repair. The FSA sits in the seam, and for someone aiming at process engineering that seam is the ladder. During an install you will personally torque the fittings (30.3), land the 480V feed with the electricians (30.1), flush the slurry loop (30.2), and then run the first monitor wafers and compute the first WIWNU (30.4). Six months of installs teaches you the machine at a depth no classroom reaches, and every senior process engineer you meet will be able to tell within five minutes whether you have that depth.

One more framing before the phases. On an install you are simultaneously working three jobs: a technical job (build the machine), a project-management job (track hundreds of tasks against a date the customer's factory planning team is counting on), and a diplomatic job (you represent your company inside a customer site where every hour of your access is negotiated). The playbook below braids all three.

## The install lifecycle, phase by phase

Here is the campaign as a pipeline. Durations are typical order-of-magnitude for a CMP tool, not promises; a clean install of a mature tool model runs weeks, a new model at a new site runs months.

```text
  1. pre-facilitation --> 2. delivery/rigging --> 3. placement/leveling
        --> 4. hookup --> 5. power-on/checkout --> 6. process startup
        --> 7. qualification --> 8. handoff/support
```

For each phase, learn three things: what the work is, what the entry and exit gates are, and what the FSA specifically owns versus what the fab or its contractors own.

### Phase 1: Pre-facilitation

Long before the tool ships, the vendor sends the customer a **facilities requirements document**: a specification of everything the building must supply to this tool. For a CMP tool the list runs roughly: three-phase power at a specified voltage and breaker size (30.1), process cooling water (PCW) at a flow and temperature window, clean dry air (CDA) and nitrogen at specified pressures, ultrapure water (30.2), slurry supply from the fab's central distribution or a local day tank, segregated drains (slurry waste does not share plumbing with acid waste, as 30.2 explained), exhaust at a specified static pressure and flow, and a floor that can carry the weight, plus clearances for doors, maintenance access, and the crate's route from the dock to the bay.

The **site survey** is the FSA's first field task: walk the route and the bay with the drawings and verify reality matches paper. Is the bay's raised floor rated for the point loads? Do the pre-installed facilities stubs land where the hookup drawing says? Will the crate actually make the turn by the freight elevator? Every mismatch found now is an email; every mismatch found on delivery day is a crane standing idle at four figures an hour.

Floor loading is a good example of the arithmetic you do in this phase, and of why "the tool weighs 4.5 tons" is not the number that matters:

```python
# Floor loading: the number that matters is pressure under a foot,
# not total weight, and rolling loads during the move are worse
# than the parked load.
KG_PER_TON = 1000.0
g = 9.81

tool_mass = 4500.0                      # kg, crated CMP polisher module
n_feet = 8
foot = 0.10 * 0.10                      # each leveling foot pad: 100 mm square, m^2

parked_per_foot = tool_mass * g / n_feet          # N per foot
parked_kpa = parked_per_foot / foot / 1000.0
print(f"parked: {parked_per_foot/1000:.1f} kN/foot -> {parked_kpa:.0f} kPa under each pad")

# During rigging the same mass rides on 4 machine-skate rollers,
# each with a contact patch of roughly 50 mm x 30 mm.
n_rollers = 4
patch = 0.050 * 0.030
rolling_kpa = tool_mass * g / n_rollers / patch / 1000.0
print(f"rolling: {rolling_kpa:.0f} kPa under each skate roller "
      f"({rolling_kpa/parked_kpa:.1f}x the parked pressure)")

# A typical cleanroom raised-floor panel is rated for a concentrated
# load of order 10-12 kN on a 25 mm square. Check the worst roller:
roller_load_kn = tool_mass * g / n_rollers / 1000.0
print(f"each roller carries {roller_load_kn:.1f} kN; "
      f"panel concentrated-load ratings are of order 10-12 kN")
print("=> parked is fine on rated panels; the MOVE needs steel plate")
```

```text
parked: 5.5 kN/foot -> 552 kPa under each pad
rolling: 7358 kPa under each skate roller (13.3x the parked pressure)
each roller carries 11.0 kN; panel concentrated-load ratings are of order 10-12 kN
=> parked is fine on rated panels; the MOVE needs steel plate
```

This is why rigging plans specify **floor protection**: steel plates or engineered overlays along the whole route, spreading the rolling load over many panels. The survey is where you confirm the route, the plate, and the panel ratings all agree.

- **Entry gate:** facilities requirements document issued at the current revision; hookup drawings exchanged.
- **Exit gate:** signed site-readiness checklist: every utility stubbed to the bay at spec, route surveyed, floor protection planned, delivery date agreed with the fab's construction schedule (you are one line item in their much larger plan, and their schedule wins ties).

### Phase 2: Delivery and rigging

The tool ships in sections, in crates, on **air-ride trucks** (suspension that isolates the cargo from road shock). Riding on each crate are **shock and tilt indicators**: single-use devices that trip and change state if the crate ever saw an impact above a threshold g or leaned past a threshold angle. Reading them is the first act of delivery day, before anything is opened, because a tripped indicator changes everything: photos, a report to the factory, possibly an inspection before acceptance. Never let schedule pressure talk anyone into "it's probably fine." The indicator exists precisely because damage from a drop can be invisible until the spindle bearing that took the hit fails during your marathon, three phases from now.

Unpacking is a **layered protocol**, and the layers map to cleanliness zones. The outer crate comes off at the loading dock: wood and cardboard never go past the dock, because cardboard sheds particles by the billion and can carry mold spores that a cleanroom's filters exist to exclude. Under the crate is a barrier bag; it gets wiped down and opened in a staging area. The tool itself, in its inner wrap, is wiped again with cleanroom wipes and approved solvent at the airshower, and only then crosses into the fab proper. Think of it as the spacecraft moving from the transport container to the clean high bay: each door it passes through has a cleanliness price of admission.

The move itself is **rigging**, and it is a licensed trade, not an FSA task: professional riggers with gantries, machine skates, and air bearings move the sections while you walk ahead with the route survey, checking clearances you already measured once. Your job is to know the tool's lift points, its center of gravity (marked on the crate and in the install manual), and what must never take load. Watch the first time; ask questions between lifts, not during them.

- **Entry gate:** site readiness signed; rigging plan and route survey approved; floor protection installed.
- **Exit gate:** all sections in the bay, indicators read and recorded, no transport damage claims open (or claims documented and dispositioned).

### Phase 3: Placement and leveling

The sections get set on their footprint per the layout drawing, then **leveled**: precision machine levels on reference surfaces, leveling feet adjusted in a pattern (like torquing a wheel, opposite corners) until the frame sits within the manual's spec, often a few hundredths of a degree. [30.3](30.3-mechanical-engineering-for-fab-tools.md) explained why a polisher cares: platen flatness and head alignment budgets are measured in microns, and a frame twisted by an unlevel floor spends that budget before the first wafer arrives.

Then the tool is fixed to the building: **anchoring and seismic bracing** per the drawing, through the raised floor into the structural slab or onto a pedestal stand. In seismic regions this is engineered restraint, calculated for the tool's mass and center of gravity, and it is inspected. Finally the sections are **docked**: mated to each other with alignment pins and gaskets so the wafer-handling robot in one module can reach stations in the next. Docking tolerances are tight for the same reason robot teaching exists at all: a handoff repeatable to fractions of a millimeter starts with frames located to fractions of a millimeter.

- **Entry gate:** sections in the bay, floor points verified.
- **Exit gate:** level within spec (recorded numbers, not "looks good"), anchors torqued and inspected, modules docked with alignment verified.

### Phase 4: Hookup

Hookup is the umbilical phase, and it is where the drawing set earns its keep. Three parallel workstreams:

- **Mechanical:** PCW supply and return, drains, exhaust duct connection. Every fluid joint gets a **pressure or leak test** before the system it feeds is ever energized: caps and gauges, pressurize, hold, watch. A PCW fitting that seeps under an energized 480V cabinet is not a plumbing problem, it is an electrical event with plumbing origins.
- **Electrical:** the 480V feed from the fab's panel to the tool's main disconnect, sized per the one-line drawing, plus grounding per spec. [30.1](30.1-electrical-engineering-for-the-fab.md) covered the physics and the safety framework; here the practical point is division of labor: the fab's licensed electricians own the building side and the landing of the feed, the vendor owns everything downstream of the tool's disconnect, and the boundary is drawn on the hookup drawing, not negotiated in the aisle.
- **Gases and chemicals:** CDA, N2, UPW, slurry supply and return. [30.2](30.2-chemical-engineering-for-cmp.md) is the chapter for what flows in these lines; this phase is where its safety sections stop being reading. Chemical lines are flushed and leak-checked with inert media before any chemical is introduced, and first-introduction of slurry or chemistry is its own gated procedure with the fab's chemical team present.

Who does what deserves one plain paragraph, because new engineers get it wrong in both directions. The fab and its contractors typically build everything from the building to the tool's connection points; the vendor's team (you) connects, verifies, and accepts. You will feel pressure to "just help" with contractor-side work and pressure to let contractors "just finish" vendor-side work. Both are boundary violations with warranty, liability, and union-agreement consequences. Know the boundary, be friendly across it, do not cross it.

- **Entry gate:** tool placed, leveled, anchored; utilities live to the bay isolation points; permits in place.
- **Exit gate:** every connection on the hookup drawing checked off with its test record: pressure/leak tests passed, electrical connections torqued and inspected, grounds measured, all signoffs collected.

### Phase 5: Power-on and functional checkout

Now the bus-by-bus part of the campaign. Power-on is a **sequence**, not a switch: verify the feed dead, land it, verify phase rotation and voltage at the disconnect, then energize the tool's distribution one level at a time per the manual, checking at each level before enabling the next. Then comes days of **point-to-point checkout**: for every sensor and actuator, verify that the physical thing and the software name agree. Open valve V-104 from the controller; watch V-104 move, not V-105. Warm a thermocouple with your gloved hand; watch the right channel rise. This is tedious exactly the way spacecraft umbilical checkout is tedious, and it is where wiring errors die cheaply instead of expensively.

**Interlock verification** gets its own pass: trip every interlock deliberately and confirm the response matches the cause-and-effect matrix (a drawing we will read shortly). Pull the flow sensor's plug and confirm the polish aborts. Press each EMO and confirm what drops. And learn now the rule that has no exceptions: **never jumper an interlock to save time.** An interlock that is in your way is telling you either that a condition really is unsafe or that a sensor is lying, and both deserve diagnosis, not a wire across the contacts. Fabs have fired people, and vendors have lost contracts, over a jumpered interlock found in an audit; the physical injuries that motivated the rule are worse. If a checkout procedure requires a defeated interlock, there is a documented, authorized, temporary way to do it, with a tag, in the procedure. That is the only way it happens.

The phase ends with motion: **robot teaching** (guiding the wafer handler to each station and storing positions, then verifying handoffs with a teaching jig or sensor wafer) and **dry cycling**, hours of running mechanical-only wafer moves and polish motions with no slurry and no product, shaking out the faults that only appear at cycle 400.

- **Entry gate:** hookup exit gate complete; LOTO points identified; checkout procedures at current revision on hand.
- **Exit gate:** point-to-point checks 100% complete, all interlocks verified against the matrix, robot teach verified, a specified dry-cycle count completed without fault.

### Phase 6: Process startup

The launch. Slurry is introduced to the loop for the first time (a flush-and-fill procedure, verifying flows and filter differential pressures as the loop transitions from UPW to slurry). The first pad is mounted and **broken in**: conditioned and run against dummy wafers until its removal rate stabilizes, because as [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md) noted, a fresh pad polishes differently than a broken-in one. Then wafers, in a deliberate cheapness order: **mechanical-grade wafers** first (cheap, reclaimable silicon; if the tool scratches or breaks one, you lose almost nothing), then **monitor wafers** with real films for the first measured polishes.

The **baseline recipes** come from the vendor's process-applications team: known-good pressures, speeds, flows, and times for this tool model and consumable set, the starting point that the customer's process engineers will tune from ([30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md)'s whole toolkit begins here). Your job in this phase is to run those recipes, measure honestly, and converge: rate near the baseline expectation, WIWNU inside the startup limit, defects sane. The first wafer through the tool is a milestone worth the photograph everyone will take. It is also, like a launch, the *start* of the proving period, not the end.

- **Entry gate:** checkout exit gate complete; chemicals introduced per procedure; consumables installed and broken in; metrology available for the monitor wafers.
- **Exit gate:** baseline recipes executing end to end, dry-in dry-out, with rate and uniformity in the startup window on monitor wafers.

### Phase 7: Qualification

The shakedown cruise, and the phase that separates "the tool runs" from "the customer accepts the tool." Qualification is contractual: the purchase agreement includes acceptance criteria, and this phase demonstrates them with data. A typical package, in the vocabulary [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md) gave you:

- A **marathon**: a long continuous run, commonly hundreds of wafers, demonstrating **availability**, **MTBF** (mean productive time between failures), and **MTTR** (mean time to repair) against contract targets. Short demos catch offsets; only marathons catch drift, wear, and the failure that only happens on wafer 214.
- **Process quals**: removal rate and WIWNU inside spec limits with a demonstrated **Cpk** (commonly 1.33 or better) on the key output, plus defect and **particle quals**, counting adders on monitor wafers against an adder limit.
- Everything at the agreed recipe, consumable set, and metrology plan, so nobody is qualifying one tool and running another.

The 30.4 metrics are literally the contract language here: a dispute during qual is settled by the definitions of availability, WIWNU, and Cpk that both sides agreed to, which is why you learn them precisely rather than approximately. The Mini-project builds the analyzer that turns a marathon log into the report both sides sign.

Qual rarely passes clean on the first attempt, and the professional response to a miss is the **punch list**: a written, owned, dated list of every open item (the assist on wafer 178, the marginal edge uniformity, the door sensor that needed reseating), each with a corrective action. You own the punch list. A customer forgives a list that shrinks on schedule; they do not forgive discovering an item you knew about and did not write down.

- **Entry gate:** process startup exit gate; qual plan and acceptance criteria agreed in writing; metrology correlated (30.4's golden-wafer discipline).
- **Exit gate:** acceptance criteria demonstrated, report delivered, customer signature. The tool changes state in the fab's MES from install to production, and from this moment its downtime is production downtime.

### Phase 8: Handoff and support

The campaign team goes home; mission operations begins. Handoff is mostly a knowledge transfer: training the fab's own maintenance techs on PMs and first-line repairs, walking the customer's engineers through the tool's quirks, agreeing the **spares** package (which parts live on site, which ship on demand), and writing down the **escalation path**: who the fab calls at 3 a.m., what response times the service contract promises, when a problem escalates from the on-site FSA to the regional specialist to the factory.

And you write the **install report**: what was configured, what deviated from the plan and why, every punch-list item and its closure, the as-left settings and drawing revisions. Write it as if the reader is you, two years from now, called back to this tool at 3 a.m. Because it will be, and the report is the institutional memory that makes the callback an hour instead of a week. The best FSAs keep a personal install journal too, and we will return to why in the career section.

## Reading the drawings

A tool ships with a drawing set, and the drawing set is the tool's source code: the authoritative description of what the machine *is*, from which the physical object was compiled. Everything you learned about reading code before writing it transfers. You would not patch a codebase you had never read; do not put a wrench on a system whose drawing you have not traced. Four families matter daily.

### Facilities and hookup drawings

These show the tool's footprint on the fab floor and every connection point: each utility's location, size, and specification, keyed to the facilities requirements document. During phases 1 and 4 you live in these. The skill is cross-referencing: the hookup drawing says connection CW-1 is PCW supply, 3/4 inch, at a given flow; the survey confirms the stub exists; the test record proves it was pressure-tested. One connection, three documents, one checkbox.

### P&IDs: the plumbing's schematic

A **P&ID** (piping and instrumentation diagram) is to fluids what a circuit schematic is to electronics: symbols and lines that show what connects to what, with every valve, pump, and instrument labeled. The labels follow a convention: a letter code for the device type plus a loop number. Common codes you will meet on a CMP tool:

```text
  V-xxx    valve (manual unless noted)     P-xxx    pump
  AV-xxx   air-operated (pneumatic) valve  FLT-xxx  filter
  CV-xxx   check valve (one-way)           T-xxx    tank
  PT-xxx   pressure transducer             FT-xxx   flow transducer
  PI-xxx   pressure indicator (a gauge)    PRV-xxx  pressure regulator
  LS-xxx   level switch                    RV-xxx   relief valve
```

Here is a simplified slurry supply line to one platen, drawn the way a P&ID thinks, and then traced the way you should learn to trace:

```text
   T-100          V-101      P-101        FLT-101         FT-102
  ┌──────┐      ┌──┴──┐    ┌───────┐    ┌─────────┐     ┌───────┐
  │ day  │──►───┤ man.├──►─┤ diaph.├─┬►─┤ filter  ├──►──┤ flow  ├──►─┐
  │ tank │      │ iso │    │ pump  │ │  │PI-1a PI-1b    │ xducer│    │
  └──┬───┘      └─────┘    └───────┘ │  └─────────┘     └───────┘    │
     │                               │                               ▼
     │                            PT-103                         ┌───┴───┐
     ▲                        (pump discharge                    │AV-104 │
     │                            pressure)                      │dispense│
     │                                                           └───┬───┘
     │              V-105 (return isolation)                         │
     └───────◄──────┤◄├───────────◄──────────────────◄───────────────┤
                                 return leg                     slurry arm,
                                                                 platen 1
```

Trace it line by line, out loud, the first time:

1. Slurry leaves the day tank **T-100** through manual isolation valve **V-101**. Manual isolation valves exist so a human can make a section safe to open; this one is the line-break point for everything downstream.
2. **P-101**, a diaphragm pump (gentle on the colloid, per [30.2](30.2-chemical-engineering-for-cmp.md)), drives the loop. **PT-103** on its discharge is pump health: a dying diaphragm shows up here first.
3. **FLT-101** catches agglomerates. The two gauges **PI-1a/1b** across it give **filter differential pressure (dP)**: a loaded filter reads high dP, a torn one reads suspiciously low. You will use this in the troubleshooting section.
4. **FT-102** measures loop flow; it feeds the low-flow interlock.
5. **AV-104** is the air-operated dispense valve: the controller opens it during polish, slurry goes to the arm; otherwise flow continues around the return leg through **V-105** back to the tank, keeping the colloid moving (a stagnant slurry line is a settling experiment, as 30.2 warned).

Two questions turn a P&ID from a picture into a tool. For any valve: *what is downstream of this if I open it, and what am I trapping if I close it?* For any instrument: *what decision does the controller make with this reading?* Ask both about every symbol on a line before you work on that line.

### Electrical one-lines and schematics

[30.1](30.1-electrical-engineering-for-the-fab.md) taught the content; here is where the paper lives. The **one-line diagram** shows power distribution: feed, main disconnect, breakers, transformers, and which subsystem each breaker feeds, drawn as single lines even for three-phase runs. It answers "what do I lock out to make this pump safe." The **schematics** show control wiring: every relay, interlock contact, and sensor loop. They answer "why did this contactor drop out." During checkout you will mark up a printed set with a highlighter, circuit by circuit; keep that set, it becomes the tool's medical chart.

### The cause-and-effect matrix

Interlocks are documented as a **cause-and-effect matrix**: rows are causes (sensor trips, E-stops), columns are effects (aborts, valve closures, power drops), and a mark at an intersection means that cause produces that effect. It is the safety logic in table form, and phase 5's interlock verification is literally executing this table against the machine. The table is small structured data, which means your checkout procedure can be code:

```python
# The cause-and-effect matrix as executable truth. During interlock
# verification you trip each cause on the real tool and record what
# happened; this checks the record against the matrix.
CE = {
    "slurry flow low, platen 1": {"abort polish P1", "close AV-104", "alarm"},
    "PCW flow low":              {"abort polish P1", "abort polish P2",
                                  "inhibit platen motors", "alarm"},
    "EMO pressed":               {"drop motion power", "close AV-104",
                                  "close facility valves", "alarm"},
    "exhaust static low":        {"inhibit chemical dispense", "alarm"},
}

# What the tool actually did when we tripped each cause during checkout:
observed = {
    "slurry flow low, platen 1": {"abort polish P1", "close AV-104", "alarm"},
    "PCW flow low":              {"abort polish P1", "abort polish P2", "alarm"},
    "EMO pressed":               {"drop motion power", "close AV-104",
                                  "close facility valves", "alarm"},
    "exhaust static low":        {"inhibit chemical dispense", "alarm",
                                  "abort polish P1"},
}

failures = 0
for cause, expected in CE.items():
    missing = expected - observed[cause]
    extra   = observed[cause] - expected
    status = "PASS" if not missing and not extra else "FAIL"
    if status == "FAIL":
        failures += 1
    print(f"{status}  {cause}")
    for m in sorted(missing):
        print(f"      missing effect: {m}  <- interlock NOT working, stop here")
    for e in sorted(extra):
        print(f"      extra effect:   {e}  <- wiring or config error, diagnose")
print(f"\n{len(CE)-failures}/{len(CE)} causes verified clean")
```

```text
PASS  slurry flow low, platen 1
FAIL  PCW flow low
      missing effect: inhibit platen motors  <- interlock NOT working, stop here
PASS  EMO pressed
FAIL  exhaust static low
      extra effect:   abort polish P1  <- wiring or config error, diagnose
3/4 causes verified clean
```

Notice the asymmetry in the two failures. A **missing** effect means a safety response did not happen: that is a stop-work finding, because the interlock the matrix promises does not exist on this machine. An **extra** effect is usually a crossed wire or misconfigured channel: less dangerous, still a defect, because a tool that aborts platen 1 on an exhaust alarm will generate mystery scrap for years if you ship it that way.

### Mechanical drawings and torque tables

Assembly drawings show exploded views, part numbers, and, on the pages people skip, **torque tables**: the specified tightening torque for each fastener class, and the pattern and stages for anything that clamps a sealing surface or a precision fit. [30.3](30.3-mechanical-engineering-for-fab-tools.md) explained why torque is really controlled clamping force. On an install the practical rules are: torque wrench, not feel; the pattern in the drawing (crossing, in stages), not around the circle; and mark torqued fasteners with a paint pen so "did we do that flange?" has a visible answer.

## Fab protocol and cleanroom citizenship

You will do all of the above inside a working cleanroom, surrounded by product. The technical skills get you invited; the citizenship gets you invited back.

**Gowning is a ritual with a physics reason.** People are the dirtiest thing in a cleanroom by orders of magnitude: skin flakes, hair fragments, fibers. The bunny suit, hood, mask, gloves, and boots are a containment system for you, donned in a fixed order in the gowning room so that the outside of the suit never touches the dirty side of anything. Follow the site's order exactly even when it differs from the last site's; it is their room.

**Materials have a cleanroom passport.** Cleanroom paper, cleanroom wipes, approved pens, tools that have been wiped in. No cardboard past the airshower, ever; no regular paper, no pencils (graphite dust), no wooden anything. Your install kit gets unpacked and wiped at the boundary just like the tool did.

**An open tool is an open wound.** Normal cleanroom air is clean because filtered air continuously sweeps particles away from the wafers. The moment you open a tool's panels, you create a region where your work (abrasion, fasteners, connectors, your own gloves) generates particles next to surfaces that will later face product. Work like a surgical team: lay out tools on a wiped tray rather than on the tool; bag removed parts; wipe before closing; and treat the post-maintenance particle check not as bureaucracy but as the wound check it is.

**Tool time is negotiated, and the WIP outranks you.** The lots in the FOUPs around you can each be worth more than a house; the fab schedules every hour of a production tool's downtime. During install this is gentler (your tool is not in production yet) but the bay around you is, so noisy, dusty, or vibration-producing work gets scheduled with the fab, sometimes at night. Once the tool is in production, you request maintenance windows, and you return the tool on time or communicate early.

**Tool state discipline is how you talk to the factory.** [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md) described the MES that tracks every tool's state. Those states are how the fab's scheduling software and human planners see your work: a tool left in "engineering" when it is actually ready is invisible capacity; a tool marked "production" while you still have panels off is a lie that dispatches product to a machine you have opened. Update the state when the state changes, and give a **pass-down** at every shift boundary: what you did, what state everything is in, what the next person must know. Fabs run on pass-downs the way hospitals run on shift-change handoffs, and for the same reason.

## Safety as a system

Every discipline chapter in Part 30 carried its own safety section: electrical energy and LOTO in [30.1](30.1-electrical-engineering-for-the-fab.md), chemical hazards and line breaks in [30.2](30.2-chemical-engineering-for-cmp.md), stored mechanical energy in [30.3](30.3-mechanical-engineering-for-fab-tools.md). The install site is where they compose into a system, because an install is the rare time when all the energies are being connected for the first time, by multiple crews, under schedule pressure. The system has three layers.

**Permits.** A **permit-to-work** system means certain jobs (energized electrical work, chemical line breaks, hot work, work at height, lifts over a certain weight) require a signed permit before they start: a short document forcing the question "what could this job release, and what controls are in place?" to be answered by someone accountable, in writing, today, for this job. Rigging plans and lift permits are the mechanical version; line-break permits are the chemical version; energized-work permits the electrical one. As the vendor's engineer you will both request permits and be listed on others'.

**Lockout/tagout**, covered step by step in 30.1, is the personal layer: your lock, your key, your verified-zero measurement before your hands go in. On an install, LOTO has a wrinkle worth naming: early in the campaign, energy sources are being *added* week by week. The line that was safe to open in phase 3 has slurry and pressure behind it in phase 6. The energy-control procedure is a living document during install, and the FSA who assumes last month's isolation list is current is the one the near-miss reports are about.

**Design-level certification.** You will hear that a tool is **SEMI S2 certified**. SEMI S2 (with S8 for ergonomics) is the semiconductor industry's environmental, health, and safety design standard for equipment: an S2 report means a third party evaluated the tool's design (interlocks, guarding, chemical containment, alarms, emissions, seismic provisions) against the standard, and fabs generally require it before a tool comes in the door. It is the reason the interlock matrix exists in the form it does. Knowing what S2 covers also tells you what it does not: it certifies the design, not your work practices on the day.

All three layers compress into two questions you ask before every physical action on a tool, and they are the habit this chapter most wants to install in you:

1. **What energy is present here?** Electrical, pressure, chemical, thermal, gravity, spring. If you cannot enumerate it, you are not ready to act.
2. **What happens downstream of this valve, breaker, or command?** The P&ID and the one-line exist to answer this. If you have not traced it, you do not know it.

## The field loop: a troubleshooting method you can reuse

Sooner or later, always sooner, something on the tool misbehaves, and how you respond is most of your reputation. Here is the method, as a named loop so you can run it under pressure. Call it the **field loop**: *Reproduce, Gather, Localize, Hypothesize, Test cheap, Change one, Write it down.*

1. **Reproduce.** Can you make it happen on demand, and what are the minimal conditions? "Slurry flow alarm" is a symptom; "slurry flow alarm on platen 2, only during dispense, every time, since Tuesday" is a problem statement. Intermittent faults get a data logger, not a shrug.
2. **Gather before touching.** Pull the error code and its manual definition, the alarm history, and the sensor trends around the event from the tool's data log ([17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md)'s software stack exists partly so you can do this). Ask the operators and read the pass-downs: *what changed?* Most faults follow a change: a PM, a consumable swap, a recipe edit, a facilities event.
3. **Localize by halving.** Treat the system as a chain and cut it in half with a measurement, like binary search on hardware. Flow is low at the platen: is pressure correct at the pump discharge (PT-103)? If yes, the problem is downstream of the pump; you just eliminated half the loop with one gauge reading. Repeat.
4. **Hypothesize against the drawings.** With the fault localized, list what lives in that segment (the P&ID tells you) and how each item could produce the symptom. A hypothesis names a component and a mechanism: "AV-104 is only partially opening because its pilot air line is restricted," not "something's wrong with the valve."
5. **Test the cheapest reversible hypothesis first.** Rank tests by cost times risk, not by how confident you feel. Reading a gauge is free; swapping a transducer is minutes; replacing a pump is hours and a line break. Cheap reversible tests first also protects you from the classic trap of destroying the evidence.
6. **Change one thing, then verify.** One variable per iteration, then re-run the reproduction case. If you changed two things and it works, you now own a tool with an unknown fix and, likely, one unnecessary new part in it forever.
7. **Write it down.** Symptom, cause, fix, and how you proved it: in the tool's maintenance log, in the pass-down, and in your install journal. A fix that is not written down will be re-derived from scratch by the next engineer, possibly you.

### Worked example: slurry flow alarm on platen 2

Day 3 of dry-cycling-plus-slurry on a fresh install. The tool posts *slurry flow low, platen 2* and aborts, every polish, starting this morning. Run the loop:

- **Reproduce:** every dispense on platen 2; platen 1 is fine. Minimal case: command AV-204 open in manual mode, watch FT-202. Flow climbs to about 60% of setpoint and sits there. Repeatable.
- **Gather:** alarm history shows the first occurrence at 07:41. The pass-down says night shift replaced FLT-201, the platen 2 slurry filter, at the end of their shift. There is the change.
- **Localize:** PT-203 (pump discharge) reads normal, so the pump and everything upstream are eliminated. Filter dP across the new FLT-201 reads *low*, lower than platen 1's, so the filter is not clogged (a brand-new clogged filter was the obvious suspect, and the gauge just killed it). The restriction is downstream of the filter.
- **Hypothesize** against the P&ID for the segment filter-to-arm: (a) AV-204 not opening fully (pilot air problem), (b) FT-202 reading low (a lying sensor, so flow is actually fine), (c) a physical restriction in the tubing between filter and valve.
- **Test cheap first:** (b) is the cheapest check: time the return-leg flow into a graduated container in manual mode. The bucket agrees with FT-202; the sensor is honest. For (a), cycle AV-204 and check pilot pressure at the valve: crisp, full pressure, valve stroke looks full. That leaves (c).
- **Find it:** open the access panel behind FLT-201. The new filter is a slightly taller model than the old one, and to make it fit, the night tech curved the outlet tube into a tight bend against the panel. Under dispense pressure the tube flattens at the bend: a **kinked line**, invisible from the front, perfectly repeatable, downstream of the filter, exactly where the halving pointed.
- **Change one, verify:** re-route the tube with the proper bend radius (30.3's minimum-bend-radius rule was not decorative), re-run the minimal case: flow at setpoint. Run ten polish cycles: clean.
- **Write it down:** log entry, pass-down, and a punch-list item: the spares list offers two filter form factors and the install kit should specify which, so the next 3 a.m. filter change does not recreate the fault.

Notice what the method bought. The obvious suspect (new filter must be clogged) was wrong, and one free gauge reading disproved it. The real cause was a side effect of the change, not the changed part itself, which is the most common shape of post-maintenance faults. And the fix included a systemic action, not just a repair.

### Escalation etiquette

Escalating is not failure; escalating *late* is. A working rule: if you have run the loop twice and your best remaining hypotheses are all expensive, or you are two hours in with no localization, call the senior FSE. When you call, have ready: the exact alarm text and code, when it started and what changed, what you have measured (numbers, not adjectives), what you have ruled out and how, and the drawing revision you are working from. That package turns a call from "help" into a fifteen-minute consult, and it is also, not coincidentally, exactly what you would want handed to you. Senior engineers remember who calls prepared.

## Working the relationship

The technical campaign runs inside a human one, and for an FSA the central relationship is with the **customer's process engineer**: the person from [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md) who owns what comes off your tool. Three habits determine whether that relationship compounds in your favor.

**Give data, not guesses.** When the customer asks why rate dropped, "I think it's probably the pad" costs you credibility twice: once if you are wrong, and once more when they learn you guessed. "Rate is down 4% over 60 wafers, filter dP is flat, motor current trend matches the rate trend, so I suspect pad wear; I want to run two monitors after the next conditioning sweep to confirm" is an answer they can act on and a method they can trust. If you do not know, say what you would measure to find out.

**Never surprise the customer in a meeting.** If qual data looks bad, the process engineer hears it from you in the aisle before their manager hears it in the review. Vendors win multi-year relationships less on having zero problems (nobody believes that) and more on being the party whose information arrives early, complete, and un-spun.

**Mine every install for the next job.** The FSA-to-process-engineer path is walked one install at a time, and the walking is deliberate: on each campaign, pick the discipline you are weakest in and own that workstream's checkout. Keep the **install journal**: every fault you see, every number that surprised you, every question you could not answer, and close the open questions within the week. After a few installs the journal is a private textbook no one else has, and in a process-engineering interview, "let me tell you about a fault I chased on an install" beats any amount of coursework, because it is evidence you have already done the job's core loop.

## Build it: an install-campaign tracker

The campaign is a project-management pipeline, so let us build the tool a startup lead actually needs: a task graph with durations and dependencies, the **critical path** through it, a text Gantt chart, and the ability to ask "what happens to the end date if the slurry line slips?"

The math is the longest-path computation on a directed acyclic graph. For each task, the **earliest start (ES)** is the latest earliest-finish among its dependencies; a forward pass in topological order computes all of them, and the project finish is the largest earliest-finish. A backward pass computes each task's **latest finish (LF)**, the latest it can end without moving the project. The difference, **slack = LF - (ES + duration)**, is how many days that task can slip for free. Tasks with zero slack form the critical path.

```python
# install_tracker.py -- critical path and Gantt for a tool install campaign
from collections import deque

def make_plan():
    # task: (duration_days, [dependencies])
    return {
        "site survey":             ( 5, []),
        "480V feed install":       (15, ["site survey"]),
        "PCW headers":             (12, ["site survey"]),
        "CDA/N2 drops":            ( 8, ["site survey"]),
        "UPW drop":                (10, ["site survey"]),
        "slurry supply line":      (12, ["site survey"]),
        "exhaust duct":            (10, ["site survey"]),
        "drain work":              ( 9, ["site survey"]),
        "tool delivery":           ( 2, ["site survey"]),
        "rigging to bay":          ( 2, ["tool delivery"]),
        "placement + leveling":    ( 3, ["rigging to bay"]),
        "seismic anchoring":       ( 2, ["placement + leveling"]),
        "mechanical hookup":       ( 6, ["seismic anchoring", "PCW headers",
                                         "exhaust duct", "drain work"]),
        "electrical hookup":       ( 4, ["seismic anchoring", "480V feed install"]),
        "chemical hookup":         ( 5, ["seismic anchoring", "UPW drop",
                                         "slurry supply line", "CDA/N2 drops"]),
        "leak + pressure tests":   ( 3, ["mechanical hookup", "chemical hookup"]),
        "power-on + checkout":     ( 8, ["electrical hookup", "leak + pressure tests"]),
        "robot teach + dry cycle": ( 5, ["power-on + checkout"]),
        "process startup":         (10, ["robot teach + dry cycle"]),
        "qual marathon":           (15, ["process startup"]),
        "handoff + training":      ( 4, ["qual marathon"]),
    }

def schedule(plan):
    """Topological sort (Kahn), then forward and backward passes."""
    indeg = {t: len(deps) for t, (_, deps) in plan.items()}
    children = {t: [] for t in plan}
    for t, (_, deps) in plan.items():
        for dep in deps:
            children[dep].append(t)
    order, q = [], deque(t for t in plan if indeg[t] == 0)
    while q:
        t = q.popleft()
        order.append(t)
        for c in children[t]:
            indeg[c] -= 1
            if indeg[c] == 0:
                q.append(c)
    assert len(order) == len(plan), "dependency cycle in plan"

    es = {}
    for t in order:                                   # forward pass
        _, deps = plan[t]
        es[t] = max((es[d] + plan[d][0] for d in deps), default=0)
    finish = max(es[t] + plan[t][0] for t in plan)
    lf = {}
    for t in reversed(order):                         # backward pass
        kids = children[t]
        lf[t] = min((lf[c] - plan[c][0] for c in kids), default=finish)
    slack = {t: lf[t] - (es[t] + plan[t][0]) for t in plan}
    return order, es, slack, finish

def report(plan, title, scale=2):
    order, es, slack, finish = schedule(plan)
    print(f"\n=== {title}: finish day {finish} ===")
    print(f"{'task':<26}{'ES':>4}{'dur':>5}{'slack':>7}   1 char = {scale} days")
    for t in order:
        d, _ = plan[t]
        bar = " " * (es[t] // scale) + ("#" if slack[t] == 0 else "=") * max(1, d // scale)
        print(f"{t:<26}{es[t]:>4}{d:>5}{slack[t]:>7}   {bar}")
    crit = [t for t in order if slack[t] == 0]
    print("critical path: " + " -> ".join(crit))
    return finish

baseline = report(make_plan(), "baseline plan")

# Scenario: the slurry supply line hits a permit problem, 12 -> 30 days.
delayed = make_plan()
dur, deps = delayed["slurry supply line"]
delayed["slurry supply line"] = (30, deps)
new_finish = report(delayed, "slurry line slips to 30 days")
print(f"\nslip: +{30 - dur} days of delay -> +{new_finish - baseline} days of project "
      f"(the task had {30 - dur - (new_finish - baseline)} day(s) of slack to absorb)")
```

## Mini-project: a qual-marathon analyzer

Phase 7 ends with a report, and this project builds the analyzer that produces it. Input: a wafer-by-wafer log of a 300-wafer marathon, including events (assists and failures with their durations) and per-wafer metrology (removal rate, WIWNU, particle adders). Output: availability, MTBF, MTTR, Cpk against the rate spec, uniformity and particle statistics, and a line-by-line verdict against the qual contract. We generate the log synthetically with seeded randomness, which doubles as a model of what real marathons look like: slow drift, occasional assists, rare failures, and dirty first wafers after each repair.

The definitions, which are contract language and worth stating precisely:

- **Availability** = productive time / (productive time + downtime). Assists that pause the run count against it; scheduled breaks do not (we model none).
- **MTBF** = productive time / number of failures. **MTTR** = total repair downtime / number of failures.
- **Cpk** = min(USL - mean, mean - LSL) / (3 sigma), computed on removal rate against the spec limits, exactly as in [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md).

```python
# qual_analyzer.py -- simulate a 300-wafer qual marathon and print the report
import random
import statistics as stats

random.seed(20260803)

# ---------------- the qual contract (agreed before the marathon) ----------
SPEC = {
    "wafers":            300,
    "availability_min":  0.92,
    "mtbf_hours_min":    6.0,
    "mttr_hours_max":    1.0,
    "rate_target":       2800.0,   # A/min
    "rate_tol":          250.0,    # spec limits = target +/- tol
    "cpk_min":           1.33,
    "wiwnu_max_pct":     5.0,      # every wafer must be under this
    "adders_max":        30,       # particles at the qual size
    "adder_pass_frac":   0.95,     # fraction of wafers that must meet it
}

# ---------------- simulate the marathon log --------------------------------
POLISH_MIN = 2.6          # minutes of polish + handling per wafer
log = []                  # per-wafer records
events = []               # (type, duration_min)
productive_min = 0.0
downtime_min = 0.0
wafers_since_repair = 999  # large: tool starts clean and broken-in

for w in range(1, SPEC["wafers"] + 1):
    # Failures are rare; assists are merely uncommon.
    r = random.random()
    if r < 0.010:                                  # hard failure -> repair
        rep = random.uniform(25.0, 80.0)
        events.append(("failure", rep))
        downtime_min += rep
        wafers_since_repair = 0
    elif r < 0.045:                                # assist: brief interrupt
        a = random.uniform(3.0, 10.0)
        events.append(("assist", a))
        downtime_min += a

    productive_min += POLISH_MIN
    wafers_since_repair += 1

    rate = 2800.0 - 0.12 * w + random.gauss(0, 52)     # slow pad-life drift
    wiwnu = abs(random.gauss(3.1, 0.55))
    adders = max(0, round(random.gauss(11, 5)))
    if wafers_since_repair <= 2:                       # dirty after a repair
        adders += random.randint(8, 25)
    log.append({"w": w, "rate": rate, "wiwnu": wiwnu, "adders": adders})

# ---------------- reduce the log to the contract metrics -------------------
fails   = [d for t, d in events if t == "failure"]
assists = [d for t, d in events if t == "assist"]
up_h, down_h = productive_min / 60.0, downtime_min / 60.0
availability = up_h / (up_h + down_h)
mtbf = up_h / len(fails) if fails else float("inf")
mttr = (sum(fails) / 60.0) / len(fails) if fails else 0.0

rates  = [d["rate"] for d in log]
mu, sd = stats.mean(rates), stats.stdev(rates)
usl = SPEC["rate_target"] + SPEC["rate_tol"]
lsl = SPEC["rate_target"] - SPEC["rate_tol"]
cpk = min(usl - mu, mu - lsl) / (3 * sd)

wiwnu_worst = max(d["wiwnu"] for d in log)
wiwnu_ok    = all(d["wiwnu"] <= SPEC["wiwnu_max_pct"] for d in log)
adder_frac  = sum(d["adders"] <= SPEC["adders_max"] for d in log) / len(log)

# ---------------- the report the customer signs ----------------------------
def line(name, value, req, ok):
    print(f"  {name:<28}{value:>12}   req {req:<14} {'PASS' if ok else 'FAIL'}")

checks = []
print("=" * 66)
print(f" QUALIFICATION MARATHON REPORT   ({SPEC['wafers']} wafers, "
      f"{len(assists)} assists, {len(fails)} failures)")
print("=" * 66)
ok = availability >= SPEC["availability_min"]; checks.append(ok)
line("availability", f"{availability:.3f}", f">= {SPEC['availability_min']}", ok)
ok = mtbf >= SPEC["mtbf_hours_min"]; checks.append(ok)
line("MTBF (h)", f"{mtbf:.2f}", f">= {SPEC['mtbf_hours_min']}", ok)
ok = mttr <= SPEC["mttr_hours_max"]; checks.append(ok)
line("MTTR (h)", f"{mttr:.2f}", f"<= {SPEC['mttr_hours_max']}", ok)
ok = cpk >= SPEC["cpk_min"]; checks.append(ok)
line("rate mean (A/min)", f"{mu:.0f}", f"{lsl:.0f}-{usl:.0f}", lsl <= mu <= usl)
line("rate Cpk", f"{cpk:.2f}", f">= {SPEC['cpk_min']}", ok)
checks.append(lsl <= mu <= usl)
ok = wiwnu_ok; checks.append(ok)
line("WIWNU worst wafer (%)", f"{wiwnu_worst:.2f}", f"<= {SPEC['wiwnu_max_pct']}", ok)
ok = adder_frac >= SPEC["adder_pass_frac"]; checks.append(ok)
line("wafers under adder limit", f"{adder_frac:.3f}", f">= {SPEC['adder_pass_frac']}", ok)
print("-" * 66)
verdict = "TOOL ACCEPTED" if all(checks) else "QUAL NOT MET - punch list required"
print(f" verdict: {verdict}")

# Punch-list intelligence: where do the dirty wafers cluster?
dirty = [d["w"] for d in log if d["adders"] > SPEC["adders_max"]]
print(f" note: {len(dirty)} wafer(s) over the adder limit at positions {dirty}")
print("       (clustered right after repairs -> post-maintenance recovery item)")
```

  MTBF (h)                            4.33   req >= 6.0        FAIL
  MTTR (h)                            0.74   req <= 1.0        PASS

This synthetic marathon fails, and it fails the realistic way: everything process-side passes while a reliability metric misses, because three failures in 13 productive hours is an MTBF of about 4.3 hours against a 6-hour requirement. Sit with the asymmetry for a second: availability *passed* (repairs were quick) while MTBF *failed* (they were too frequent), which is exactly why contracts specify both. A tool that fails often but recovers fast is a different disease from a tool that fails rarely but takes all night to fix, and the pair of numbers distinguishes them. The report's job is to make the miss undeniable and specific, so the punch list writes itself: three failure events to root-cause with the field loop, and a post-repair particle recovery procedure (the dirty-wafer clustering the note calls out) even though the particle metric technically passed.

For your portfolio version: read a real-format CSV instead of simulating, add per-wafer trend plots of rate and adders, compute Cpk on WIWNU as well, and add a "marathon restart" rule (many contracts void the run if a failure exceeds some duration). Then run it against the 100-wafer marathon simulator you built in [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md): two of your own projects meeting is the best integration test there is.

## Common mistakes

1. **Jumping the checklist because you have done it before.** The checkout procedure is boring by design; boring is what caught the crossed valve wiring on somebody's install years ago, which is why the line exists. Gates skipped in phase 4 come back as mysteries in phase 7, where they cost ten times more to find. Fix: run the list, initial the lines, and when a step really is obsolete, get the procedure changed rather than privately skipping it.
2. **Jumpering an interlock "just for this test."** The single fastest way to end an install career, and occasionally a life. Fix: if the procedure requires a defeated interlock, there is an authorized, tagged, documented way; if it does not, the interlock is telling you something, so diagnose it.
3. **Working from the wrong drawing revision.** The tool shipped at rev C; the field-change order made it rev D; you are tracing rev B from the site trailer. Every conclusion downstream of the wrong sheet is suspect. Fix: check the revision block before you trace anything, and mark superseded prints so nobody else works from them either.
4. **Not owning the punch list.** Letting open items live in emails and memories, then being surprised in the acceptance meeting. Fix: one list, one owner (you), every item with an action and a date, reviewed with the customer weekly. A shrinking written list builds more trust than a verbal "almost done."
5. **Treating the fab's techs as juniors.** The customer's maintenance techs will run this tool for years and have seen a hundred tools installed; some of the best diagnostic hints on any install come from them. Condescend once and the hints stop. Fix: teach openly during handoff, ask what they have seen, and credit them in the pass-down when their hint cracks a problem.
6. **The silent fix.** You find your own mistake, fix it quietly, and log nothing. The fix works, and the tool's history now contains a lie that will misdirect some future 3 a.m. debug. Fix: log it anyway. Engineers who document their own errors get trusted with bigger systems, not smaller ones.
7. **Losing track of the critical path.** Grinding on a task with two weeks of slack while the pacing item waits for a decision only you can make. Fix: rerun the schedule (you built the tool for this) whenever a duration changes, and start every week knowing which chain owns the end date.
8. **Guessing in front of the customer.** Covered in the relationship section, repeated here because under pressure it is the mistake everyone makes first. Fix: "here is what I know, here is what I will measure next, here is when you will hear from me."

## On the job / interview angle

**What the job posting says** is some variant of: install, maintain, and support CMP systems at customer sites; troubleshoot electrical, mechanical, and chemical subsystems; support process qualification; travel expected. You now know what every clause means in practice, which already puts you ahead of most applicants.

**What the interview probes.** FSA interviews at vendors like Ebara or Applied Materials are less about trivia and more about method and temperament: walk me through troubleshooting a flow alarm (give them the field loop, literally by its steps); what would you check before powering on a newly connected tool (phase 5's sequence, and say "verify zero energy first"); a customer says your tool is scrapping wafers, what do you do in the first hour (gather data, secure the tool state, communicate a plan, no guessing); tell me about a time you followed a procedure you thought was unnecessary (they are testing whether you jumper interlocks). If you have built the two projects in this chapter, bring them up when scheduling or qual metrics appear: a candidate who can define MTBF versus availability precisely, and has code that computes both from a log, reads as someone who has done the reading a year of the job would otherwise teach.

**The first 90 days** on the job look like: safety and cleanroom certifications, shadowing a senior FSE on PMs and an install if the timing is lucky, owning small tasks (point-to-point checks, torque maps, log pulls) with your work double-checked, and building the reputation that gets you sent places: shows up prepared, writes things down, asks before improvising.

**Turning it into process engineering.** The path this book has been assuming, from [17.6](../17-Semiconductors/17.6-semiconductor-careers.md)'s career map through this chapter, runs: FSA on installs, then apps-heavy work (baselines, quals, customer process support), then a process role at the vendor or the fab. What converts the first job into the third is the evidence you accumulate: the install journal, the faults you root-caused, the quals you ran, and the analysis skills from [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md) applied to real tool data. Your software background is a real differentiator here, and [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md) and [29.4](../29-Cpp/29.4-industrial-embedded-cpp.md) tell you where it plugs in: the FSA who can pull and analyze tool logs with a script, or read the controller's error handling in source, closes problems that others escalate.

## Exercises

1. **Critical path by hand.** A mini-plan: A(3 days, no deps), B(6, after A), C(4, after A), D(2, after B and C), E(5, after D). Compute ES, EF, LF, and slack for every task, the project finish, and the critical path. Then: C's contractor offers to finish 2 days early for a fee. Is it worth anything?
2. **Marathon arithmetic.** A 400-wafer marathon logs 20.0 hours of productive time, 4 failures with repair times of 30, 45, 20, and 55 minutes, and 9 assists totaling 50 minutes. Compute availability, MTBF, and MTTR. The contract requires availability >= 0.93, MTBF >= 5.0 h, MTTR <= 0.75 h. Which clauses pass?
3. **Floor loading.** A 6,000 kg module will be moved on 6 skate rollers, each with a 40 mm x 30 mm contact patch. Compute the load per roller in kN and the contact pressure in kPa. If the route's floor panels are rated for 12 kN concentrated load, is steel plate optional?
4. **P&ID line break.** Using the slurry P&ID in this chapter, you must replace FLT-101. List the valves you close, what remains pressurized after closing them, what drains out when you crack the filter housing, and the two questions from the safety section as they apply to V-101 specifically.
5. **Slack hunting.** Modify the install tracker to print all tasks with slack of 2 days or less ("near-critical"). In the baseline plan, which tasks appear besides the critical path itself, and why should the campaign lead watch them?
6. **Cpk under drift.** A qual's removal-rate spec is 2550 to 3050 A/min. The marathon's first 150 wafers have mean 2815, sigma 55; the last 150 have mean 2760, sigma 55. Compute Cpk for each half and for the pooled 300 wafers (treat the pooled distribution as the equal mixture of the two halves: pooled mean is the average, and pooled variance = average within-half variance + variance of the two means around it). Why is the pooled Cpk worse than both halves suggest?

### Solutions

**1.** Forward pass: ES(A)=0, EF(A)=3. ES(B)=3, EF(B)=9. ES(C)=3, EF(C)=7. D waits for both: ES(D)=max(9,7)=9, EF(D)=11. ES(E)=11, EF(E)=16. Finish = 16. Backward: LF(E)=16, LS(E)=11; LF(D)=11; LF(B)=9, LF(C)=9. Slack: A=0, B=0, C=9-7=2, D=0, E=0. Critical path A-B-D-E. C finishing 2 days early is worth nothing: C already has 2 days of slack, so the fee buys days on a chain that does not set the finish date. (This is the exercise version of the slurry-line lesson: money spent off the critical path buys schedule margin at best, and no schedule at worst.)

**2.** Downtime = (30+45+20+55) + 50 = 200 min = 3.333 h. Availability = 20.0/(20.0+3.333) = 0.857, FAIL against 0.93. MTBF = 20.0/4 = 5.0 h, PASS (exactly at the limit; contracts specify whether "at" passes, which is why they are written carefully). MTTR = (150/60)/4 = 0.625 h, PASS. Note the assists hurt availability but by convention do not enter MTBF/MTTR, which count failures only; a tool can pass MTBF while assists quietly wreck availability, which is why both are in the contract.

**3.** Per roller: 6000 x 9.81 / 6 = 9,810 N = 9.81 kN. Patch = 0.040 x 0.030 = 0.0012 m^2. Pressure = 9810/0.0012 = 8.175 MPa = 8,175 kPa. Each roller is under the 12 kN panel rating with about 22% margin, but that margin assumes the roller sits centered on a panel's strong point and the load stays evenly shared among 6 rollers; a threshold, a panel seam, or a momentary 4-roller condition during a turn erases it. Steel plate is not optional; the calculation is why the rigging plan says so.

**4.** Close V-101 (upstream isolation, the designated line-break point) and V-105 or AV-104's segment depending on site procedure; on this simplified P&ID, closing V-101 and V-105 isolates the working section from the tank in both directions. Still present after closing: the trapped volume between V-101 and V-105/AV-104 remains full of slurry at whatever pressure was captured, so crack the housing slowly with drip containment; the filter housing itself drains its volume plus the line segment above it. The two questions for V-101: *what is downstream if I open it* (the pump, filter, and platen line: opening it during the break floods the open housing) and *what am I trapping if I close it* (the pressurized slurry-filled segment you are about to open, which is exactly why the crack-slowly step exists).

**5.** Add after the Gantt loop: `near = [t for t in order if 0 < slack[t] <= 2]` and print it. In the baseline plan this flags UPW drop (slack 2), exhaust duct (slack 2), and slurry supply line (slack 1). The campaign lead watches them because slack that small is one delivery problem from zero, and when it hits zero the critical path silently moves, as the chapter's slip scenario demonstrated with the slurry line.

**6.** First half: min(3050-2815, 2815-2550)/(3 x 55) = min(235,265)/165 = 235/165 = 1.42. Second half: min(290, 210)/165 = 210/165 = 1.27. Pooled per the stated model: mean = (2815+2760)/2 = 2787.5; variance = 55^2 + ((2815-2787.5)^2 + (2760-2787.5)^2)/2 = 3025 + 756.25 = 3781.25, sigma = 61.5. Cpk = min(262.5, 237.5)/(3 x 61.5) = 237.5/184.5 = 1.29. The pooled sigma is inflated by the drift between halves: Cpk assumes one stable distribution, and a drifting process is two (or a continuum of) distributions wearing one histogram. This is why [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md) insists on control *before* capability, and why marathons exist at all: a short qual would have seen only the 1.42.

## Teach it back

1. Walk someone through the launch-campaign analogy end to end: which install phase is the pad survey, which is the umbilical work, what is the launch, and why the campaign is not over at launch. Where does the analogy earn its keep (gates, critical path) and where does it leak?
2. Explain entry and exit gates using two concrete examples from this chapter: what must be true before power-on begins, and what must be demonstrated before qualification ends. Why does skipping a gate move cost later instead of removing it?
3. Trace the slurry P&ID from tank to platen aloud, naming each symbol and its job, then answer the two questions (downstream-of-open, trapped-by-close) for V-101 and AV-104.
4. Recite the field loop from memory and re-tell the platen 2 flow alarm story with it: what was the obvious suspect, which free measurement eliminated it, and why the real cause was a side effect of the filter change rather than the filter.
5. Define availability, MTBF, MTTR, and Cpk precisely, then explain how the Mini-project's marathon passed availability while failing MTBF, and what different disease each metric detects.
6. Explain to a new engineer why interlocks are never jumpered, what a cause-and-effect matrix is, and the difference in severity between a missing effect and an extra effect during interlock verification.
