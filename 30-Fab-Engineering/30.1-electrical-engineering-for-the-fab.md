## Start here: the tool's circulatory and nervous systems

Think of a process tool as a body. It has a **circulatory system**: the power distribution, thick copper conductors carrying hundreds of amps from the facility's electrical room down through the subfab and into the tool's main breaker, then branching into smaller and smaller circuits until a few milliamps reach a sensor. Power is blood. It carries energy, not information, and the plumbing that moves it is sized by how much must flow: big arteries (the 480V feed), medium vessels (the 208V and 120V branch circuits), capillaries (the 24V DC that powers sensors and logic).

It also has a **nervous system**: the signal wiring. A thermocouple junction in a slurry line, an encoder on a spindle, a pressure transducer on a membrane zone, each produces a tiny electrical signal, millivolts or milliamps, that must travel meters of cable through an environment full of electrical noise and arrive intact at a controller. Signals are nerves. They carry information, not energy, and everything about how they are wired (shielding, twisted pairs, current loops) exists to protect the information.

Between the two sits the **reflex layer**: relays, contactors, interlocks, and the PLC. Like your spinal cord, it reacts to danger without waiting for the brain. The tool controller software from 17.7 is the brain, and it is deliberately not trusted with the fastest, most safety-critical reactions. When a door opens on a spinning platen, a hardwired chain kills motor power before any software has scheduled a thread.

The whole chapter hangs on this one picture. Power distribution is circulation, and its diseases are overloads, phase loss, and voltage sag. Signals are nerves, and their diseases are noise, ground loops, and broken wires. The reflex layer is the interlock chain, and its disease is someone bypassing it. And electrical safety, the last section before the projects, is the rule that you never operate on a patient whose heart is still pumping: you isolate, you lock, and you verify zero energy before you touch anything.

One framing note before we start. You, the reader of this book, are a software person entering this world, likely as a field service or applications engineer (FSA) at an equipment vendor. During a tool install you will stand next to open 480V panels while electricians land cables, and during troubleshooting you will probe live 24V control circuits. The goal here is the literacy to do that work safely and to talk to electricians and controls engineers as a peer, not to replace an EE degree or a site's safety training. Where safety is concerned, your site's written procedures always win over anything in a book.

## DC circuits in practice

You met Ohm's law in [19.1](../19-Physics/19.1-physics-for-cs.md). Here we use it the way a technician does: to predict what a meter should read before you measure, so that the measurement can surprise you.

### The four relations you use daily

- **Ohm's law**: `V = I * R`. Voltage across a resistance equals current through it times the resistance.
- **Series resistances add**: `R_total = R1 + R2 + ...`, and the same current flows through all of them.
- **Parallel resistances combine reciprocally**: `1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...`, and all of them see the same voltage.
- **Power**: `P = V * I = I^2 * R = V^2 / R`. Power is where circuits meet the physical world: it is the heat in a wire, the torque in a motor, the reason a resistor smells burnt.

**Kirchhoff's laws** are the bookkeeping that makes any circuit solvable. The **current law (KCL)** says current into a node equals current out: charge does not pile up.
The **voltage law (KVL)** says the voltages around any closed loop sum to zero: the energy a charge gains from sources it must give back to drops. Every circuit solver, from your multimeter intuition to SPICE, is these two laws plus algebra.

### The voltage divider, the most common circuit in the building

Two resistors in series across a supply, output taken from the middle:

```text
   Vin ──[ R1 ]──┬──[ R2 ]── GND
                 │
               Vout = Vin * R2 / (R1 + R2)
```

The derivation takes one line. The same current `I = Vin / (R1 + R2)` flows through both resistors (series), so the voltage across R2 is `I * R2`, which gives the formula. Memorize it, because half the sensors on a tool are voltage dividers in disguise:

- A **potentiometer** position sensor is a divider whose tap moves with the mechanism.
- An **RTD** or **thermistor** temperature reading is usually a fixed resistor in series with the sensing element: temperature changes R2, which changes Vout.
- A **pull-up resistor** on a switch input is a divider where R2 is either the switch (closed, roughly 0 ohms, output low) or an open circuit (output pulled high).

The classic trap is **loading**. The divider formula assumes nothing draws current from the midpoint. Real measuring devices have finite input resistance, which sits in parallel with R2 and pulls the output down. Let's quantify it with a solver instead of hand algebra.

### A worked nodal-analysis solver

**Nodal analysis** is KCL turned into linear algebra: pick a ground node, write "sum of currents leaving each node = 0" for every unknown node, and solve. For a resistor between nodes a and b, the current leaving a is `(Va - Vb) / R`. Collect terms and you get a matrix equation `G v = i`, where G is built from conductances (`1/R`). This is exactly what SPICE does at its core, and it is fifteen lines of numpy.

```python
import numpy as np

def solve_network(n_nodes, resistors, fixed):
    """Nodal analysis. Node 0 is ground.
    resistors: list of (node_a, node_b, ohms)
    fixed: dict {node: volts} for nodes tied to ideal sources
    Returns array of node voltages."""
    G = np.zeros((n_nodes, n_nodes))
    rhs = np.zeros(n_nodes)
    for a, b, r in resistors:
        g = 1.0 / r
        G[a, a] += g; G[b, b] += g
        G[a, b] -= g; G[b, a] -= g
    fixed = dict(fixed); fixed.setdefault(0, 0.0)   # ground
    for node, volts in fixed.items():               # overwrite KCL row
        G[node, :] = 0.0
        G[node, node] = 1.0
        rhs[node] = volts
    return np.linalg.solve(G, rhs)

# A 24 V supply feeding a 10k/10k divider (a position sensor at mid-travel).
# Node 1 = supply, node 2 = divider tap, node 0 = ground.
resistors = [(1, 2, 10_000.0), (2, 0, 10_000.0)]
v = solve_network(3, resistors, {1: 24.0})
print(f"unloaded tap voltage : {v[2]:7.3f} V   (theory: 12.000 V)")

# Now connect a receiver with 100k input resistance from tap to ground.
loaded = resistors + [(2, 0, 100_000.0)]
v100k = solve_network(3, loaded, {1: 24.0})
print(f"loaded, Rin = 100k   : {v100k[2]:7.3f} V   "
      f"error {12.0 - v100k[2]:.3f} V")

# A cheap meter or an analog input with only 10k input resistance:
loaded10 = resistors + [(2, 0, 10_000.0)]
v10k = solve_network(3, loaded10, {1: 24.0})
print(f"loaded, Rin = 10k    : {v10k[2]:7.3f} V   "
      f"error {12.0 - v10k[2]:.3f} V")

# Power dissipated in each divider resistor (unloaded case):
i_div = 24.0 / 20_000.0
print(f"divider current      : {i_div*1000:.2f} mA, "
      f"power per resistor {i_div**2 * 10_000 * 1000:.1f} mW")
```

```text
unloaded tap voltage :  12.000 V   (theory: 12.000 V)
loaded, Rin = 100k   :  11.429 V   error 0.571 V
loaded, Rin = 10k    :   8.000 V   error 4.000 V
divider current      : 1.20 mA, power per resistor 14.4 mW
```

Read the numbers. A 100k receiver on a 10k divider steals almost 5 percent of the signal. A 10k receiver steals a third of it. This is not an academic curiosity: it is the reason a reading can change when you connect your meter, and one of the two structural weaknesses of voltage signaling that the 4-20 mA section will fix.

The power line matters too. Fourteen milliwatts is nothing, but scale the same arithmetic up: a 100 A feed through a connection with 10 milliohms of unwanted contact resistance dissipates `I^2 R = 100 W` in a lug the size of your thumb. Loose power connections do not fail quietly; they cook. This is why install checklists torque every lug to spec and why thermal cameras are pointed at panels during commissioning.

## AC and three-phase power

Everything so far was DC, which is what the tool's sensors and logic run on. But the energy arrives as AC, and the machines that move things (pumps, spindles, platens) mostly run on AC. You need to know why, and you need the three-phase arithmetic, because the numbers 480, 277, 208, and 120 are all over every tool's facilities documentation.

### RMS: the honest average of a sine wave

An AC voltage `v(t) = Vp * sin(2*pi*f*t)` averages to zero, yet it clearly delivers power. The useful average is the **RMS** (root mean square) value: the DC voltage that would deliver the same average power into a resistor. Power into a resistor is `v^2 / R`, so we average `v^2`:

```text
   mean of sin^2 over a cycle = 1/2
   so mean power = Vp^2 / (2R)
   the equivalent DC voltage satisfies Vrms^2 / R = Vp^2 / (2R)
   therefore Vrms = Vp / sqrt(2)  ~ 0.707 * Vp
```

When someone says "120 volts" they mean RMS; the actual peak is about 170 V, and the peak-to-peak swing is about 340 V. Meters read RMS. Insulation and semiconductor ratings care about peaks. Keep both in your head.

### Impedance: resistance grows up

Resistors oppose current the same way at any frequency. Inductors and capacitors oppose it in a frequency-dependent way called **reactance**:

- An inductor's reactance is `XL = 2*pi*f*L`. It rises with frequency: an inductor passes DC and fights fast change, because a changing current builds a changing magnetic field that pushes back (Faraday's law from 19.1).
- A capacitor's reactance is `XC = 1 / (2*pi*f*C)`. It falls with frequency: a capacitor blocks DC and passes fast change, because current only flows while the stored charge is changing.

**Impedance** is the generalization of resistance that includes both, and in reactive components the current is shifted in time relative to the voltage (up to a quarter cycle). The practical consequence is **power factor**: in a partly reactive load, some current sloshes back and forth without delivering net energy, so the real power is `P = Vrms * Irms * pf`, where the power factor `pf` is 1 for a heater and typically 0.8 to 0.9 for an induction motor. The wires must carry the full current either way, which is why facilities engineers care about pf even though the "extra" current does no work.

### Why AC won distribution, and why three phases

AC won the grid because of the transformer: two coils on a shared iron core convert AC between voltage levels with high efficiency and no moving parts, and it only works with AC because only a changing current induces a voltage. High voltage means low current for the same power, and low current means thin, cheap, cool wires (that `I^2 R` again). So the grid ships energy at high voltage and transformers step it down to what each load wants.

**Three-phase** power is three sine waves of the same amplitude and frequency, offset by 120 degrees. It exists for two reasons:

1. **Constant power delivery.** Single-phase power pulses at twice the line frequency (the load gets zero power every time the sine crosses zero). The three phase powers pulse too, but their sum is constant in time for a balanced load. Motors fed constant power run smoother, and conductors are used more efficiently: three-phase delivers three times the power of one phase while adding only one wire (three hots, sharing a return that carries zero current when balanced).
2. **Rotating fields for free.** Three windings spaced 120 degrees apart in a motor, fed by three phases offset 120 degrees in time, produce a smoothly rotating magnetic field with no electronics at all. That fact is the entire basis of the induction motor, next section.

### Line vs phase voltage, and the numbers on the nameplate

In a **wye (star)** connection, three windings share a neutral point.
The **phase voltage** is measured from one line to neutral; the **line voltage** is measured between two lines. Because the two phases you are measuring between are 120 degrees apart, the line voltage is not double the phase voltage but `sqrt(3)` times it (the phasor difference of two unit vectors 120 degrees apart has length sqrt(3)). That single factor explains the standard US pairs:

```text
   480 V line-to-line  /  277 V line-to-neutral     (480 = sqrt(3) * 277)
   208 V line-to-line  /  120 V line-to-neutral     (208 = sqrt(3) * 120)
```

A US fab typically feeds big process tools with **480V three-phase at 60 Hz**, uses 208V three-phase or 120V single-phase for lighter loads, and the tool's internal transformers and power supplies make everything else (24V DC for logic and sensors is the workhorse). Other regions differ: Japan uses a 200V class and is split between 50 Hz (east) and 60 Hz (west), which is why tool power specs always list acceptable voltage and frequency ranges, and why the same tool model ships with different transformer taps per region. When you read a tool's facilities spec during pre-install, the electrical table (voltage, phases, frequency, full-load amps, breaker size) is the first thing the site's electricians will ask you about.

Three-phase power for a balanced load is:

```text
   P = 3 * Vphase * Iphase * pf = sqrt(3) * Vline * Iline * pf
```

Worked example: a CMP tool drawing 40 A per line at 480V with pf = 0.85 consumes `sqrt(3) * 480 * 40 * 0.85 ~ 28.3 kW`. Run the same tool at 208V and it would need 92 A lines: heavier cable, bigger breakers, more loss. That is the whole argument for 480V in one comparison.

Let's verify all of it numerically.

```python
import numpy as np

f = 60.0                       # Hz
V_LINE = 480.0                 # line-to-line RMS
V_PH = V_LINE / np.sqrt(3)     # line-to-neutral RMS (wye)
Vp = V_PH * np.sqrt(2)         # peak of each phase
t = np.linspace(0, 2 / f, 4000, endpoint=False)   # two full cycles

phases = np.array([Vp * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * t - k * 2 * np.pi / 3)
                   for k in range(3)])            # A, B, C

# 1) RMS check: numerical RMS should equal V_PH
rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(phases ** 2, axis=1))
print("phase RMS voltages  :", np.round(rms, 1), f"(theory {V_PH:.1f})")

# 2) line-to-line = sqrt(3) * phase
v_ab = phases[0] - phases[1]
print(f"line-line RMS       : {np.sqrt(np.mean(v_ab**2)):.1f} "
      f"(theory {V_LINE:.1f})")

# 3) balanced resistive load, 12 ohms per phase: instantaneous power
R = 12.0
p_each = phases ** 2 / R              # per-phase instantaneous power
p_total = p_each.sum(axis=0)
print(f"per-phase power     : pulses between 0 and "
      f"{p_each[0].max()/1000:.1f} kW")
print(f"total 3-phase power : min {p_total.min()/1000:.3f} kW, "
      f"max {p_total.max()/1000:.3f} kW  <- constant")

# 4) the nameplate formula
I_line = V_PH / R                     # wye, resistive: line I = phase I
P_formula = np.sqrt(3) * V_LINE * I_line * 1.0
print(f"sqrt(3)*VL*IL*pf    : {P_formula/1000:.3f} kW "
      f"(matches {np.mean(p_total)/1000:.3f} kW)")
```

```text
phase RMS voltages  : [277.1 277.1 277.1] (theory 277.1)
line-line RMS       : 480.0 (theory 480.0)
per-phase power     : pulses between 0 and 12.8 kW
total 3-phase power : min 19.200 kW, max 19.200 kW  <- constant
sqrt(3)*VL*IL*pf    : 19.200 kW (matches 19.200 kW)
```

Each phase alone pulses from zero to 12.8 kW and back, 120 times a second. The sum of all three never wavers from 19.2 kW. That flat line is why three-phase motors do not vibrate at twice line frequency and why nearly every motor larger than a kitchen appliance is three-phase.

## Motors and drives

A CMP tool is, mechanically, a collection of rotating things: the platen, the carrier head spindle, the conditioner arm, slurry pumps, fans. Almost all of them are AC motors fed by variable-frequency drives. This section is the one-page theory and the practical picture.

### The induction motor in one page

Feed three-phase current into three stator windings spaced 120 degrees apart and, as computed above, you get a magnetic field rotating at the **synchronous speed**:

```text
   Ns = 120 * f / p    (rpm, f in Hz, p = number of magnetic poles)
```

A 4-pole motor on 60 Hz has Ns = 1800 rpm. The rotor is a **squirrel cage**: bare conductive bars shorted by end rings, no brushes, no magnets, no electrical connection at all. The rotating stator field sweeps past the rotor bars, induces currents in them (Faraday again), and those currents in the field produce torque that drags the rotor around after the field.

The subtlety that makes the whole machine work: the rotor can never quite catch up. If it spun at exactly synchronous speed, the field would be stationary relative to the bars, no current would be induced, and torque would vanish. So the rotor runs slightly behind, and the lag fraction is the **slip**:

```text
   s = (Ns - N) / Ns
```

Slip is the motor's throttle. At no load, slip is near zero. Load the shaft and the rotor slows a little, the field sweeps the bars faster, induced currents and torque grow until torque balances the load. In the normal operating region (slip of roughly 1 to 5 percent), torque is close to proportional to slip, so the motor self-regulates like a spring: a 4-pole, 60 Hz motor with 3 percent rated slip runs at about 1746 rpm at full load and creeps toward 1800 as load is removed. No controller needed. This ruggedness, plus having no brushes to wear, is why the induction motor runs most of the industrial world.

### VFDs: how you actually set the platen speed

The catch is that an induction motor's speed is chained to line frequency. On 60 Hz a 4-pole motor runs near 1800 rpm, period. A CMP recipe wants the platen at 37 rpm for one step and 93 for the next, so we must change the frequency the motor sees. That is the **variable-frequency drive (VFD)**, and its architecture is worth knowing because you will meet its failure modes:

```text
   480V 3-ph ──► [ rectifier ] ──► [ DC bus + capacitors ] ──► [ inverter ] ──► motor
   60 Hz          diodes make        ~650V DC, energy           six switches
                  lumpy DC           storage                    (IGBTs) chop DC
                                                                into PWM "AC" at
                                                                any frequency
```

1. **Rectify.** Six diodes turn the incoming three-phase AC into DC.
2. **DC bus.** Big capacitors smooth it to a stiff DC rail (around 650V DC from a 480V feed, since the bus charges toward the line peak, `480 * sqrt(2) ~ 679V`). These capacitors hold lethal charge for minutes after power-off, which the safety section will return to.
3. **Invert.** Six transistor switches chop the DC into a **PWM** (pulse-width modulated) waveform: a train of rectangular pulses whose widths are modulated so the average traces a sine wave at whatever frequency the controller commands. The motor's inductance smooths the pulses into nearly sinusoidal current.

One rule governs the output: **constant volts per hertz**. The magnetic flux in the motor is proportional to V/f, and the iron saturates above design flux, so as the drive lowers frequency it lowers voltage in proportion (30 Hz gets about 240V from a 480V drive). Below rated speed the motor can then deliver full torque at any speed; above rated frequency the voltage cannot rise further, so available torque falls off. Modern drives layer smarter control (vector control) on top, but V/f is the mental model that explains the behavior you observe.

Why this matters on a CMP tool specifically: Preston's equation from [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md) says removal rate is proportional to pressure times velocity. The velocity term is set by platen and head rpm, so recipe-controlled, repeatable, smoothly-ramped speed is a process parameter, not a convenience. The tool controller sends a speed setpoint to the VFD (over a fieldbus or analog signal, both later in this chapter), the VFD ramps the frequency at a configured acceleration to avoid mechanical shock and current spikes, and it reports back actual speed and current. Drive fault codes (overcurrent, DC bus overvoltage, overtemperature) are among the most common alarms an FSA chases.

**Regenerative braking** is the elegant wrinkle. Command a VFD to decelerate a heavy platen and the motor becomes a generator: the load's kinetic energy flows backward into the DC bus and the bus voltage climbs. The drive must do something with that energy: burn it in a **braking resistor** (the usual answer, watch for a big finned resistor near the drive), or feed it back to the line if it is a fancier regenerative unit. A "DC bus overvoltage" trip during deceleration usually means the ramp is too fast for the braking hardware, a fault you can diagnose from this paragraph alone.

### The motor family at a glance

| Type | Speed behavior | Feedback | Where on a CMP tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Induction + VFD** | near-synchronous, slips under load | optional encoder | platen, pumps, fans, spindles |
| **Servo** (permanent-magnet + drive) | exactly follows commanded position/velocity | always (encoder/resolver) | robot axes, head sweep, precise positioning |
| **Stepper** | moves in fixed angular steps, open loop | usually none | small axes: slurry valves, conditioner index |

The trade is cost and complexity versus precision. Induction motors are cheap, rugged, and fine wherever "spin at roughly this speed" is enough. Servos close a feedback loop around an encoder and will hold position against disturbance, which is what a wafer-handling robot needs. Steppers are cheap open-loop positioners that simply trust that no step was ever missed, acceptable for light, low-speed loads, and the source of the classic failure where a stepper axis silently loses position after a mechanical jam.

## Sensors and the signal chain

Now the nervous system. A **signal chain** is the full path from physical quantity to number in software: sensor, wiring, signal conditioning, ADC, and finally a floating-point value a program like the ones in 17.7 can act on. Every stage can lie to you, and this section is about how.

### Temperature: thermocouples vs RTDs

A **thermocouple** exploits the Seebeck effect: a junction of two dissimilar metals develops a small voltage that depends on temperature. A type K thermocouple produces roughly 40 microvolts per degree C. It is cheap, rugged, fast, and works to extreme temperatures, but the signal is tiny (a fab-relevant 60 C reads about 2.4 mV), it is nonlinear, and it measures the temperature *difference* between the hot junction and the point where the thermocouple wires meet copper, so the instrument must measure its own terminal temperature (**cold-junction compensation**) and add it back. Microvolt signals on long wires are exactly the kind of nerve that noise attacks.

An **RTD** (resistance temperature detector), typically **Pt100** (platinum, 100 ohms at 0 C), changes resistance with temperature: about 0.385 ohms per degree C. It is more accurate and more stable than a thermocouple, but slower, more fragile, more expensive, and it needs an excitation current to read a resistance, which brings two subtleties: the lead wires' own resistance adds to the measurement (fixed by 3-wire and 4-wire connections that measure and subtract the leads), and the excitation current self-heats the element, so it is kept small. CMP cares about temperature more than you might guess: slurry chemistry and pad behavior are temperature-dependent, and platen temperature drifts over a polish run, so you will find RTDs in slurry lines and platens.

### Pressure, flow, force

- **Pressure transducers**: a diaphragm deflects under pressure and strain gauges on it (a Wheatstone bridge, which is two voltage dividers measured against each other) convert deflection to voltage. On a CMP tool these watch membrane zone pressures, downforce air, and facility supplies.
- **Capacitance manometers** (the vacuum world's precision gauge, met in 17.7's vacuum section): the diaphragm is one plate of a capacitor, and deflection changes capacitance. The reading depends only on force per area, not on which gas is pushing, which is why process chambers trust them.
- **Flow meters** get one sentence here because the fluid mechanics that make them interesting live in [30.2](30.2-chemical-engineering-for-cmp.md): slurry and DI water loops meter flow, and a "flow OK" bit is a common interlock input.
- **Load cells** are strain-gauge bridges in a structural element, reading force directly; on a CMP head they are how you verify that commanded downforce is real downforce during calibration.

### Encoders: position as a digital signal

A **rotary encoder** turns shaft angle into pulses. The **incremental quadrature** type outputs two square waves, A and B, 90 degrees out of phase.
Each edge is a fixed fraction of a revolution, and the phase relationship encodes direction: if A leads B the shaft turns one way, if B leads A the other. The two bits step through the **Gray-code** sequence `00, 01, 11, 10`, exactly one bit changing per step, so a single glitched read cannot look like a legal two-step move.

Decoding is a 16-entry lookup table on (previous state, current state), and it is worth writing once so you never find it mysterious again:

```python
# Quadrature decoder: (prev, curr) -> delta counts.
# Legal transitions move one step around the cycle 00 -> 01 -> 11 -> 10.
SEQ = [0b00, 0b01, 0b11, 0b10]          # Gray order, one bit flips per step

DELTA = {}
for i, prev in enumerate(SEQ):
    DELTA[(prev, prev)] = 0                       # no movement
    DELTA[(prev, SEQ[(i + 1) % 4])] = +1          # forward one step
    DELTA[(prev, SEQ[(i - 1) % 4])] = -1          # backward one step
    DELTA[(prev, SEQ[(i + 2) % 4])] = None        # illegal: 2 steps at once

def encoder_states(positions):
    """Map a list of integer positions to (A,B) quadrature states."""
    return [SEQ[p % 4] for p in positions]

# Simulate a spindle: forward 6 counts, dwell, back 4, then a glitch where
# the sampling loop was too slow and missed a state (position jumps by 2).
true_pos = list(range(0, 7)) + [6, 6] + list(range(6, 2, -1)) + [1]  # jump 3->1
states = encoder_states(true_pos)

count, errors = 0, 0
for prev, curr in zip(states, states[1:]):
    d = DELTA[(prev, curr)]
    if d is None:
        errors += 1          # real firmware raises an alarm here
    else:
        count += d
print(f"true net movement    : {true_pos[-1] - true_pos[0]:+d} counts")
print(f"decoded count        : {count:+d} counts")
print(f"illegal transitions  : {errors} (each one means missed steps)")
```

```text
true net movement    : +1 counts
decoded count        : +3 counts
illegal transitions  : 1 (each one means missed steps)
```

The decoder caught the glitch: the count is off by exactly the two steps hidden inside the illegal transition, and the error counter tells you the position can no longer be trusted. Real motion controllers do this in hardware at megahertz rates, and "encoder fault" alarms are this table firing. The lesson generalizes: sample too slowly relative to how fast the signal changes and you do not just lose detail, you get confidently wrong answers. That is **aliasing**, and it applies to every sampled signal in the tool, from encoder edges to the pressure traces in the Build it section.

### 0-10V vs 4-20 mA: why current loops win

Analog sensor values travel between boxes in two standard formats: a **0-10V voltage signal** or a **4-20 mA current loop** (4 mA represents the bottom of the range, 20 mA the top). Voltage signaling looks natural and works fine across a circuit board. Across 30 meters of fab cable tray, it has two structural flaws, and the current loop fixes both. This deserves a real derivation, because it is the single most useful piece of instrumentation theory on the floor.

**Flaw 1: the wire is a voltage divider.** A voltage receiver draws some input current, and that current flowing through the wire's resistance drops voltage that the receiver never sees. From the nodal-analysis section: with 50 ohms of round-trip wire and a 10k receiver, you lose 0.5 percent of the signal; corrosion in a connector can add far more, and the error changes as connectors age. The signal you read depends on the health of every junction between sensor and receiver.

**Flaw 2: any voltage inserted in the loop adds to the signal.** If the sensor's ground and the receiver's ground differ by 300 mV (they will, see the next section), that 300 mV adds directly to a 0-10V signal: a 3 percent error you cannot distinguish from a real pressure change.

Now the current loop. The transmitter is not a voltage source; it is a **current regulator**. It measures the loop current itself and adjusts its internal drop until exactly the commanded current flows, using whatever voltage headroom the 24V loop supply provides. Follow KCL around the single loop: the current is the same at every point, through the wire resistance, through corroded connectors, through the 250 ohm sense resistor at the receiver. Wire resistance drops voltage, but the transmitter simply compensates, and the *current*, which carries the information, is untouched, as long as the total loop resistance stays within the transmitter's compliance budget:

```text
   24V supply, transmitter needs ~8V to operate, so the budget is
   (24 - 8) V / 0.020 A = 800 ohms of total loop resistance at 20 mA.
   250 (sense) + 50 (wire) + even 100 ohms of corroded connector = 400. Fine.
```

A series ground-potential difference is just more loop drop, compensated the same way. Both flaws gone by construction.

And the choice of 4 mA instead of 0 mA for the bottom of the range, the **live zero**, is a free diagnostic channel. A healthy loop never carries less than 4 mA, so 0 mA has an unambiguous meaning: broken wire, dead transmitter, or blown fuse. A 0-10V system reading 0V cannot tell "measured value is zero" from "wire fell off." The live zero also lets the transmitter power itself from the loop (it can always burn up to 4 mA without disturbing the signal), which is why a two-wire pressure transmitter needs no separate power cable. The Build it section simulates all of this side by side.

### ADC: where analog becomes a number

The **ADC** (analog-to-digital converter) samples the conditioned signal and quantizes it to an n-bit integer. Two numbers characterize it:

- **Resolution**: an n-bit ADC over a span V has a step (LSB) of `V / 2^n`. A 12-bit ADC on 0-10V resolves 2.44 mV; 16-bit resolves 0.153 mV. Map that through the sensor: 12 bits on a 0-100 psi transducer is 0.024 psi per count. Everything below one LSB is invisible, and the quantized value carries up to half an LSB of error by construction.
- **Sample rate**: how often it converts. The aliasing warning from the encoder section applies in full: any frequency content above half the sample rate folds back and masquerades as a lower frequency. The 60 Hz hum that a ground loop injects, sampled at 100 Hz, will appear in your data as a clean, entirely fictitious 40 Hz oscillation. If you have not read a signals text, hold onto the rule of thumb: sample several times faster than anything you care about, and filter out (in analog, before the ADC) anything you do not.

## Noise, grounding, and EMI

Why do signals need protecting at all? Because a fab tool is one of the noisiest electromagnetic environments a signal will ever cross. The chief villain is usually a neighbor you just met: the VFD. Its inverter switches hundreds of volts in fractions of a microsecond, thousands of times per second, and every one of those edges sprays high-frequency energy into everything nearby, by capacitive coupling (fast-changing voltage), inductive coupling (fast-changing current), and conducted paths through shared wiring. When an analog reading gets jumpy every time the platen spins up, suspect the drive, and suspect the routing of the analog cable next to the motor cable.

The defenses, in the order you should think of them:

- **Separation.** Power and signal cables in separate trays, crossing at right angles when they must cross. The cheapest fix and the first thing to check on a troublesome install.
- **Twisted pair.** Twist the signal and return wires together and any magnetic field induces nearly equal voltage in each twist, alternating sign, canceling along the length. Ethernet, fieldbuses, thermocouple extension wire: twisted for this reason.
- **Shielding.** A grounded conductive jacket around the pair intercepts capacitive coupling and drains it to ground. The rule that prevents the next problem: ground the shield at **one end only** (usually the controller end), because a shield grounded at both ends becomes a ground loop.
- **Differential signaling.** Send the signal as the *difference* between two wires and let the receiver subtract. Noise coupled onto both wires equally (common-mode) cancels in the subtraction. The 4-20 mA loop is inherently differential; RS-485 fieldbuses and encoder line drivers are explicitly so.

**Ground loops** deserve their own paragraph because they generate the most mysterious symptoms. "Ground" is not an ideal zero; it is copper with resistance, shared by every load in the building. When a motor drive dumps amps of high-frequency current into the ground network, the ground potential at the tool end of a cable and at the controller end differ by real, fluctuating millivolts to volts. Connect a signal's return to ground at both ends and you have built a loop: the ground potential difference drives current around it, through your signal return, adding an offset plus a 60 Hz-and-harmonics hum to the reading. The classic signatures are a reading that shifts when some unrelated machine starts, or hum that vanishes when you lift one ground connection. The architectural fix is the **star ground**: every subsystem's ground runs back to a single point, so no signal return ever carries another circuit's ground current. Tool electrical designs specify separate ground conductors (safety ground, signal ground, sometimes a dedicated "clean" instrument ground) that meet at one star point, and an FSA who lands a shield at the wrong end during a repair can undo that design with one screw.

## The control layer: relays, PLCs, and interlocks

Between the 480V circulatory system and the tool-controller brain sits the reflex layer. It is deliberately simple, deliberately fast, and, for the most safety-critical paths, deliberately not software.

### Relays and contactors

A **relay** is a switch operated by an electromagnet: energize a coil with a small control current and it closes (or opens) contacts that switch a bigger circuit. It provides both amplification (24V logic switching a 480V load) and isolation (no electrical connection between coil side and contact side). A **contactor** is a relay grown up: heavy contacts rated for motor currents, built to break arcs. The distinctive *clack* you hear when a tool starts a pump is a contactor closing.
Contacts come **normally open (NO)**, closed only when the coil is energized, and **normally closed (NC)**, opened by the coil. The choice encodes fail-safe logic: safety circuits use NC contacts wired so that a broken wire or dead coil looks identical to "unsafe," stopping the machine rather than hiding the fault.
This **fail-safe by de-energization** principle repeats everywhere: E-stops, door switches, and brake circuits all use "energized = permitted to run."

### PLCs and ladder logic in ten minutes

A **PLC** (programmable logic controller) is an industrial computer built for exactly this layer: rugged, deterministic, and programmed in **ladder logic**, a notation drawn like the relay panels it replaced.
A program is a list of **rungs** between two vertical power rails. Contacts (inputs) in series mean AND, contacts in parallel mean OR, an NC contact means NOT, and the rung ends in a **coil** (an output) that energizes when the logic path is true:

```text
   |   start    stop   estop_ok  flow_ok        motor   |
   |──┤ ├──┬────┤/├──────┤ ├──────┤ ├──────────( M )────|
   |  motor│                                            |
   |──┤ ├──┘         ┤ ├ = NO contact   ┤/├ = NC        |
```

That rung is the **seal-in** (latch) pattern, the atom of industrial control. Pressing `start` energizes the motor coil; the coil's own contact in parallel with the start button then keeps the rung true after the button is released. Anything in series (the NC stop button, the E-stop-healthy bit, the flow switch) breaks the latch the instant it opens, and the motor stays off until a human presses start again. Faults stop things and stay stopped: no auto-restart when the fault clears, because the fault clearing while someone's hands are in the machine must not start the motor.

The PLC executes this with a **scan cycle**: read all inputs into memory, evaluate every rung top to bottom, write all outputs, repeat, typically every few milliseconds, with a watchdog that faults the controller if a scan runs long. Compare that with the garbage-collected, thread-scheduled world of the tool controller above it and you see why the reflex layer exists. Here is the scan cycle and the seal-in rung as runnable Python:

```python
# A miniature ladder-logic engine: each rung is (coil_name, function of io).
# The io dict holds inputs and coils; rungs are evaluated in order each scan,
# exactly like a PLC scan cycle.

def scan(io, rungs):
    for coil, logic in rungs:
        io[coil] = logic(io)

# Rung 1: slurry pump seal-in with stop, E-stop chain, and flow interlock.
# Rung 2: platen enable requires the pump to already be running (sequencing).
rungs = [
    ("pump",   lambda io: (io["start_pb"] or io["pump"])
                          and not io["stop_pb"]
                          and io["estop_ok"] and io["flow_ok"]),
    ("platen", lambda io: io["pump"] and io["platen_req"]),
]

io = {"start_pb": False, "stop_pb": False, "estop_ok": True,
      "flow_ok": True, "platen_req": False, "pump": False, "platen": False}

events = [
    ("operator presses START",        {"start_pb": True}),
    ("operator releases START",       {"start_pb": False}),
    ("recipe requests platen",        {"platen_req": True}),
    ("slurry flow sensor drops out",  {"flow_ok": False}),
    ("flow restored",                 {"flow_ok": True}),   # note: no restart
    ("operator presses START again",  {"start_pb": True}),
]

print(f"{'event':32s} {'pump':>5s} {'platen':>7s}")
for name, changes in events:
    io.update(changes)
    scan(io, rungs)
    print(f"{name:32s} {str(io['pump']):>5s} {str(io['platen']):>7s}")
```

```text
event                             pump  platen
operator presses START            True   False
operator releases START           True   False
recipe requests platen            True    True
slurry flow sensor drops out     False   False
flow restored                    False   False
operator presses START again      True    True
```

Walk the output against the rungs. The seal-in holds the pump on after the button is released; the platen follows only when both the pump runs and the recipe asks; the flow dropout kills both; and, the line that matters most, restoring flow does *not* restart anything. The latch was broken and stays broken until a deliberate start.

### Interlocks: hardwired vs software

An **interlock** is a condition that must be true for something to be permitted. Tools implement them in layers:

- **Hardwired**: a physical chain of NC contacts in series with a contactor coil. The **E-stop chain** is the canonical example: every E-stop button, door switch, and overtravel limit wired nose to tail, so opening any one drops a master safety relay and cuts motion power. No processor in the path; a cut wire equals a pressed button.
- **PLC-enforced**: the ladder layer, milliseconds fast, harder to bypass than software, and the home of sequencing interlocks (pump before platen).
- **Software**: the tool controller from 17.7 checks interlocks too, mostly to fail gracefully and log usefully. It is the brain noticing what the spinal cord already did.

A working FSA rule: the layers exist because each one backstops the failures of the layer above, and defeating a lower layer to "get the tool running" removes the backstop precisely when it is most needed. The Common mistakes section returns to this.

### Fieldbuses: the nerves bundled into trunks

Point-to-point wiring (one cable per signal) does not scale to a tool with thousands of I/O points, so signals ride shared digital buses:

- **Analog and discrete I/O** still exists at the edges: a 4-20 mA transducer or a 24V proximity switch typically lands on a nearby I/O block, and the *block* sits on a bus.
- **DeviceNet** is an older CAN-based bus (the same CAN as in cars), common on the installed base you will service: modest speed, very rugged, devices identified by node number, and famous for baud-rate and termination-resistor misconfiguration symptoms.
- **EtherCAT** is a modern industrial Ethernet: frames pass through every device in a ring and each device reads and writes its slice on the fly, giving microsecond-class synchronization, which is what coordinated multi-axis motion (a wafer robot) needs.

Above all of this sits the tool controller, and above that SECS/GEM to the fab host, the "phone line to headquarters" you already know from [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md). It is one continuous chain: a pressure diaphragm flexes, a transmitter regulates a loop current, an I/O block digitizes it, EtherCAT carries it, the PLC checks it against an interlock, the tool controller logs it, and SECS/GEM reports it to the MES. You can now name every hop.

## Electrical safety on the fab floor

This section is practical orientation, not legal advice and not a substitute for your employer's training. But the framing matters before the training makes sense: as an FSA you will work around open 480V panels during install and hookup, and 480V three-phase is not "strong tingle" territory. It is arc-flash territory, and the discipline below is how people work around it for an entire career without an event.

**Lockout/tagout (LOTO)** is the protocol for making a machine safe to work on, and the point is that it is a *protocol*, a fixed sequence performed the same way every time, not a judgment call made when convenient:

1. **Identify** every energy source feeding the work: electrical feeds, but also pneumatic, hydraulic, chemical, and stored mechanical energy. A tool's documentation includes an energy-control procedure listing all of them.
2. **Notify** affected people and shut the equipment down normally.
3. **Isolate**: open the disconnect, close the valves, physically break every path.
4. **Lock and tag**: your own padlock, to which only you hold the key, on each isolation point, with a tag naming you. Multiple workers means multiple locks on a group hasp; the machine cannot be re-energized until every person has removed their own lock.
5. **Dissipate stored energy**: those VFD DC bus capacitors hold hundreds of volts for minutes after disconnect; springs, raised loads, and pressurized lines all count. Wait out or discharge per procedure.
6. **Verify zero energy.** Test your meter on a known live source, test the isolated circuit and confirm zero, then test the meter on a known live source again (live-dead-live). A meter with a blown fuse reads zero volts on everything, and this sequence is the only thing standing between that failure and your hands. "Verify" is the step that makes the rest real: you never trust a breaker handle, a label, or a colleague's word over your own measurement.

**Arc flash** is why distance and PPE matter even without touching anything. If a fault bridges phases in a 480V panel (a dropped tool, a slipped probe), the resulting arc is an explosion: a conductive plasma at temperatures that vaporize copper, with a pressure wave and molten-metal spray.
Panels are labeled with an **incident energy** rating (in calories per square centimeter at a working distance) and a required **PPE category**, running roughly from category 1 (arc-rated shirt and pants, safety glasses, gloves) up to category 4 (a full multi-layer arc-rated suit and hood). The label, not your sense of how quick the task is, sets the gear. In the US this framework comes from the NFPA 70E standard; other regions have equivalents, and your site's rules govern. The FSA-shaped summary: know which work is yours (control circuits, sensors, signal-level troubleshooting) and which belongs to qualified electricians (energized power work), and never let schedule pressure move that line.

## Build it: a signal chain simulator

Time to make the 4-20 mA argument quantitative. We will simulate one pressure measurement two ways, through a 0-10V link and through a 4-20 mA loop, over the same awful 30-meter cable run past a VFD, then digitize, filter, and run fault detection. Everything from the last three sections appears: wire resistance, ground-loop offset and hum, ADC quantization, aliasing, moving-average and median filters, and live-zero fault detection.

```python
import numpy as np
np.random.seed(42)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------- setup ----
FS = 100.0                          # controller sample rate, Hz
T_END = 20.0
t = np.arange(0.0, T_END, 1.0 / FS)
N = len(t)

# True membrane-zone pressure, 0-100 psi transducer range:
# 60 psi with a slow recipe drift, a setpoint step at t=12 s.
p_true = 60.0 + 3.0 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.05 * t)
p_true[t >= 12.0] += 8.0

R_WIRE = 50.0                        # ohms, round trip, long corroded run

# ------------------------------------------------- path 1: 0-10 V signal ---
R_IN = 10_000.0                      # receiver input resistance
v_src = 10.0 * p_true / 100.0        # transducer's intended output
v_after_divider = v_src * R_IN / (R_IN + R_WIRE)     # flaw 1: loading loss

# Flaw 2: ground potential difference between tool and controller:
# a DC offset plus 60 Hz hum from the VFD's ground currents.
ground_loop = 0.25 + 0.20 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 60.0 * t)
v_received = v_after_divider + ground_loop \
             + np.random.normal(0.0, 0.02, N)         # coupled random noise
# NOTE: 60 Hz sampled at 100 Hz aliases to |100-60| = 40 Hz in the data.

# ------------------------------------------------ path 2: 4-20 mA loop -----
i_loop = 0.004 + 0.016 * p_true / 100.0               # transmitter regulates
i_loop = i_loop + np.random.normal(0.0, 15e-6, N)     # transducer noise
# Compliance check: 24 V supply, transmitter needs 8 V, sense R = 250 ohm.
worst_drop = 0.020 * (R_WIRE + 250.0)
assert worst_drop < 24.0 - 8.0, "loop resistance over budget"
# Wire resistance and ground offset drop voltage the transmitter absorbs;
# the CURRENT through the 250 ohm sense resistor is unchanged.
v_sense = i_loop * 250.0                              # 1-5 V at receiver

# A broken wire at t = 17 s: loop current collapses to zero.
broken = t >= 17.0
v_sense_flt = np.where(broken, 0.0, v_sense)
v_received_flt = np.where(broken, 0.0, v_received)    # 0 V looks "valid"...

# ----------------------------------------------------------- 12-bit ADC ----
def adc(v, bits=12, span=10.0):
    codes = np.clip(np.round(v / span * (2**bits - 1)), 0, 2**bits - 1)
    return codes * span / (2**bits - 1)

v_adc_volt = adc(v_received_flt)
v_adc_loop = adc(v_sense_flt)
lsb = 10.0 / 4095                    # one ADC step in volts
# loop scale: 1-5 V spans 100 psi, so 25 psi per volt
print(f"ADC LSB: {lsb*1000:.2f} mV = {lsb*25.0:.3f} psi on the loop scale")

p_volt = v_adc_volt / 10.0 * 100.0                     # naive scaling
p_loop = (v_adc_loop / 250.0 - 0.004) / 0.016 * 100.0

# ------------------------------------------------------------- filtering ---
def moving_average(x, w):
    return np.convolve(x, np.ones(w) / w, mode="same")

def median_filter(x, w=9):
    pad = w // 2
    xp = np.pad(x, pad, mode="edge")
    return np.array([np.median(xp[i:i + w]) for i in range(len(x))])

p_loop_filt = moving_average(median_filter(p_loop), 10)
p_volt_filt = moving_average(median_filter(p_volt), 10)

# ------------------------------------------------------ fault detection ----
loop_ma = i_loop.copy(); loop_ma[broken] = 0.0
fault_live_zero = loop_ma < 0.0035          # < 3.5 mA: wire break / dead xmtr
fault_overrange = loop_ma > 0.021           # > 21 mA: transmitter fault
first_fault = t[np.argmax(fault_live_zero)]
print(f"loop fault detection : live-zero trip at t = {first_fault:.2f} s "
      f"(wire broke at 17.00 s)")

# ------------------------------------------------------------- scoreboard --
healthy = ~broken & (t > 1.0) & (t < 11.0)  # steady window before the step
def err(x): return np.mean(np.abs(x[healthy] - p_true[healthy]))
print()
print(f"{'signal path':34s}{'mean |error| (psi)':>20s}")
print(f"{'0-10 V raw':34s}{err(p_volt):>20.3f}")
print(f"{'0-10 V median+moving avg':34s}{err(p_volt_filt):>20.3f}")
print(f"{'4-20 mA raw':34s}{err(p_loop):>20.3f}")
print(f"{'4-20 mA median+moving avg':34s}{err(p_loop_filt):>20.3f}")
print()
print("after the break at t=17s:")
print(f"  0-10 V path reads {p_volt[-1]:6.1f} psi  "
      "(a plausible-looking LIE)")
print(f"  4-20 mA path reads {p_loop[-1]:6.1f} psi -> below live zero, "
      "flagged FAULT")
```

```text
ADC LSB: 2.44 mV = 0.061 psi on the loop scale
loop fault detection : live-zero trip at t = 17.00 s (wire broke at 17.00 s)

signal path                         mean |error| (psi)
0-10 V raw                                       2.204
0-10 V median+moving avg                         2.197
4-20 mA raw                                      0.076
4-20 mA median+moving avg                        0.024

after the break at t=17s:
  0-10 V path reads    0.0 psi  (a plausible-looking LIE)
  4-20 mA path reads  -25.0 psi -> below live zero, flagged FAULT
```

The comparison is the point. The two paths cross the *same* 50 ohm wire with the *same* ground potential difference. The current loop's raw error is under a tenth of a psi, dominated by transducer noise and ADC quantization. The voltage path is off by more than 2 psi, and notice that filtering barely helps: the median and moving-average filters remove the hum's wiggle but cannot remove the DC ground-loop offset, because an offset is not noise, it is a bias. No amount of averaging fixes a biased sensor path. And after the wire break, the voltage path reports 0.0 psi, a perfectly in-range value indistinguishable from a vented zone, while the loop back-computes to an impossible -25 psi and the live zero raises its hand within one sample. If a process decision (is the membrane really at 68 psi?) rides on that reading, the wiring standard *is* process quality.

Two software lessons hide in here as well. The median filter earns its keep against spikes (try injecting a few single-sample glitches), while the moving average trades response speed for smoothness: widen the window and watch the t=12 s step smear. And the 60 Hz hum really does appear at 40 Hz in the sampled data, a fake frequency created purely by sampling, which is why analog anti-alias filtering ahead of the ADC, not clever code after it, is the correct fix.

## Mini-project: a three-phase VFD ramp simulator

Now the circulatory side gets its own simulator: three-phase power feeding a VFD that ramps a platen-like motor against a fan-law load, plus the two power-quality faults every drive manual warns about, phase loss and imbalance. Build this, extend it, and put it in the same portfolio repo as the CMP simulator from 17.7.

The model, kept honest and simple:

- The VFD holds **V/f constant** below 60 Hz, so flux and the torque-per-slip stiffness stay roughly constant.
- In the normal operating region, motor torque is approximately proportional to **slip speed** in rpm: `T = k * (Ns - N)`, with k calibrated from the nameplate (rated torque at rated slip).
- The load is a fan-law load, torque proportional to speed squared (a good stand-in for pumps, fans, and fluid drag on a platen): `T_load = T_rated * (N / N_rated)^2`.
- At each commanded frequency, the steady operating speed is where the two torque curves cross, which is a quadratic you can solve exactly.

```python
import numpy as np
np.random.seed(7)

# ------------------------------------------------ motor nameplate ----------
POLES = 4
F_RATED = 60.0
NS_RATED = 120.0 * F_RATED / POLES          # 1800 rpm synchronous
SLIP_RATED = 0.03
N_RATED = NS_RATED * (1 - SLIP_RATED)       # 1746 rpm at full load
P_RATED = 5000.0                            # 5 kW platen motor
W_RATED = N_RATED * 2 * np.pi / 60.0
T_RATED = P_RATED / W_RATED                 # ~27.3 N*m
K_TORQUE = T_RATED / (NS_RATED - N_RATED)   # N*m per rpm of slip speed

def operating_point(f_cmd):
    """Steady state: k*(Ns - N) = T_rated*(N/N_rated)^2. Solve for N."""
    ns = 120.0 * f_cmd / POLES
    a = T_RATED / N_RATED**2                # quadratic coefficient
    # a*N^2 + k*N - k*ns = 0  ->  positive root
    n = (-K_TORQUE + np.sqrt(K_TORQUE**2 + 4 * a * K_TORQUE * ns)) / (2 * a)
    torque = K_TORQUE * (ns - n)
    p_mech = torque * n * 2 * np.pi / 60.0
    return ns, n, torque, p_mech

print("VFD ramp, constant V/f, fan-law load (T ~ N^2)")
print(f"{'f(Hz)':>6} {'V/f volts':>10} {'Ns rpm':>8} {'N rpm':>8} "
      f"{'slip%':>6} {'T(N*m)':>7} {'P(kW)':>6}")
for f_cmd in [6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, 54, 60]:
    v_out = 480.0 * f_cmd / F_RATED
    ns, n, torque, p_mech = operating_point(f_cmd)
    print(f"{f_cmd:>6} {v_out:>10.0f} {ns:>8.0f} {n:>8.0f} "
          f"{100*(ns-n)/ns:>6.2f} {torque:>7.1f} {p_mech/1000:>6.2f}")

# ------------------------------ waveforms and power at a mid-ramp point ----
f_now, amp_scale = 30.0, 30.0 / 60.0        # V/f: half voltage at half freq
V_PH_PK = 480.0 / np.sqrt(3) * np.sqrt(2) * amp_scale
tt = np.linspace(0, 2 / f_now, 3000, endpoint=False)
_, n, torque, p_mech = operating_point(f_now)
R_EQ = 3 * (V_PH_PK / np.sqrt(2))**2 / (p_mech / 0.88)  # 88% motor efficiency

def phase_set(amps=(1.0, 1.0, 1.0)):
    return np.array([a * V_PH_PK * np.sin(2*np.pi*f_now*tt - k*2*np.pi/3)
                     for k, a in zip(range(3), amps)])

def diagnose(v, label):
    rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(v**2, axis=1))
    avg = rms.mean()
    imbalance = 100.0 * np.max(np.abs(rms - avg)) / avg
    lost = [chr(65 + i) for i, r in enumerate(rms) if r < 0.5 * avg]
    status = (f"PHASE LOSS ({','.join(lost)})" if lost
              else "IMBALANCE" if imbalance > 2.0 else "ok")
    p_avg = np.mean(np.sum(v**2 / R_EQ, axis=0))
    print(f"{label:24s} rms={np.round(rms,1)} "
          f"imb={imbalance:5.1f}%  Pavg={p_avg/1000:5.2f} kW  [{status}]")

print(f"\nat {f_now:.0f} Hz: N = {n:.0f} rpm, shaft {p_mech/1000:.2f} kW; "
      "electrical diagnostics:")
diagnose(phase_set((1.0, 1.0, 1.0)),  "balanced")
diagnose(phase_set((1.0, 1.0, 0.93)), "phase C sagging 7%")
diagnose(phase_set((1.0, 0.0, 1.0)),  "phase B open")
```

VFD ramp, constant V/f, fan-law load (T ~ N^2)
 f(Hz)  V/f volts   Ns rpm    N rpm  slip%  T(N*m)  P(kW)
     6         48      180      179   0.32     0.3   0.01
    12         96      360      358   0.63     1.1   0.04
    18        144      540      535   0.94     2.6   0.14

The last table row landing exactly on the nameplate numbers, 1746 rpm, 27.3 N*m, 5.00 kW, is the built-in sanity check. Read the slip column: at low speed the fan load is nearly nothing, so slip is a fraction of a percent, and slip grows with the cube-law power demand until it reaches the rated 3 percent at 60 Hz. This is the quantitative version of "a fan load is easy to start," and it is why fan and pump drives are sized to the running load, not to some huge starting torque.

The diagnostics section is your fault detector. Per-phase RMS over a window is cheap to compute and catches the two classic supply problems: a **phase loss** (blown fuse, broken conductor upstream) shows one RMS collapsing, and a single-digit percentage **imbalance** between phases, which looks minor, produces disproportionate heating in a real motor (a few percent voltage imbalance can push current imbalance several times larger), so drives trip on it deliberately. When a tool's drive faults on "input phase loss" at 6 AM, this arithmetic is what the drive did.

Extensions if you want to go further: add an acceleration-limited frequency ramp and integrate rotor speed through time instead of jumping between steady states; model the DC bus voltage rising during a fast deceleration and trip an overvoltage fault unless a braking resistor duty cycle absorbs it; add a fourth "current" table column using a simple magnetizing-plus-torque current model.

## Common mistakes

1. **Trusting a reading without knowing its chain.** A value on a screen passed through a transducer, a cable, an ADC, scaling code, and maybe a filter. When a number looks wrong, walk the chain from the physics end: is the sensor plausible, is the wiring intact, does the raw ADC value match the displayed value? The 0-10V path in the Build it section reported a confident, wrong number for three seconds after its wire broke.
2. **Bypassing an interlock to "just test something."** The layered interlock design assumes every layer is present. Jumpering a door switch to run a motor with the cover off removes the exact protection designed for the situation you just created. Vendors treat interlock defeats found in the field as serious events; so should you.
3. **Grounding a shield at both ends.** It feels thorough and it builds a ground loop. One end, controller side, per the tool's grounding drawing.
4. **Routing signal cables in the power tray.** The VFD's PWM edges couple into everything parallel to the motor leads. Separation and right-angle crossings are free; intermittent analog weirdness discovered after install is not.
5. **Confusing RMS and peak.** "480V" mains peaks near 679V, and a VFD DC bus sits near that peak. Meter ratings, insulation, and your expectations must all be set by peaks, while power arithmetic uses RMS.
6. **Assuming zero volts means zero energy.** Capacitors hold charge after disconnect, and a meter with a blown fuse reads zero on a live bus. Live-dead-live verification exists because both failures happen.
7. **Filtering a bias and calling it fixed.** Averaging removes noise, never offset. The ground-looped voltage signal filtered clean and smooth and *wrong* by more than 2 psi. If a reading needs a fudge factor, find the physical cause.
8. **Treating a stepper's commanded position as truth.** Open-loop means unverified. After any jam or stall, a stepper axis must be re-homed; a servo would have noticed, a stepper cannot.

## On the job / interview angle

For a field service or applications engineer at a CMP vendor (Ebara, Applied Materials, and peers), this chapter is the daily-use toolbox:

6. A signal is sampled at 500 Hz. VFD switching noise appears in the analog line at 2.2 kHz. At what frequency does it appear in the sampled data? Show the folding arithmetic.
7. Extend the ladder simulator: add a second rung so the conditioner arm coil requires the platen to be on AND a `pad_present` input, and add a `reset_pb` that is required (one scan, momentary) after any flow fault before the pump can restart, i.e. a fault latch. Show a scan sequence proving the pump cannot restart on start alone after a fault.

### Solutions

**1.** Unloaded: `Vout = 24 * 3300 / (4700 + 3300) = 24 * 0.4125 = 9.90 V`. Loaded: R2 parallel with 47k is `3300*47000/(3300+47000) = 3083.5 ohms`, so `Vout = 24 * 3083.5/(4700+3083.5) = 24 * 0.3962 = 9.51 V`. Error: `(9.90-9.51)/9.90 = 3.9 percent` low.

**2.** `P = sqrt(3) * 208 * 12 * 1.0 = 4323 W`, about 4.3 kW. At 480V for the same power: `I = P / (sqrt(3) * 480) = 4323 / 831.4 = 5.2 A`. Same energy, 43 percent of the current, which is the entire case for higher distribution voltage.

**3.** `Ns = 120 * 50 / 6 = 1000 rpm`. Shaft: `1000 * (1 - 0.04) = 960 rpm`. At 35 Hz: `Ns = 120*35/6 = 700 rpm`, shaft `700 * 0.96 = 672 rpm`.

**4.** Fraction of span: `(11.2 - 4) / 16 = 0.45`, so `T = 0.45 * 200 = 90 C`. A broken wire gives 0 mA, which back-computes to a nonsensical `(0-4)/16 * 200 = -50 C`; the receiver flags any current below about 3.5 mA as a fault rather than a reading. A cold process at 0 C still drives a healthy 4.0 mA, so the two cases are unambiguous. That distinction is the live zero.

**5.** The sense resistor maps 4-20 mA to 1-5 V. The ADC step is `10 / 65535 = 152.6 uV`. The active span is 4 V, so `4 / 10 * 65535 = 26214 counts` cover 0-200 C, giving `200 / 26214 = 0.0076 C` per count. Note that 60 percent of the ADC's range (0-1 V and 5-10 V) is spent on nothing; instrumentation inputs often use a gained range for exactly this reason.

**6.** Frequencies fold around multiples of the sampling rate. `2200 Hz` sampled at `500 Hz`: subtract the nearest multiple of 500, `2200 - 4*500 = 200 Hz`. Since 200 is below the Nyquist frequency of 250, the noise appears as a clean 200 Hz signal. No digital filter can distinguish it from a real 200 Hz component afterward, so the fix must be analog, before the ADC.

**7.**

```python
def scan(io, rungs):
    for coil, logic in rungs:
        io[coil] = logic(io)

rungs = [
    # fault latch: sets on flow loss while pump runs, clears only on reset
    ("fault",  lambda io: (io["fault"] or (io["pump"] and not io["flow_ok"]))
                          and not io["reset_pb"]),
    ("pump",   lambda io: (io["start_pb"] or io["pump"])
                          and not io["stop_pb"] and io["estop_ok"]
                          and io["flow_ok"] and not io["fault"]),
    ("platen", lambda io: io["pump"] and io["platen_req"]),
    ("cond",   lambda io: io["platen"] and io["pad_present"]),
]

io = {k: False for k in ["start_pb","stop_pb","reset_pb","platen_req",
                         "pump","platen","cond","fault"]}
io.update({"estop_ok": True, "flow_ok": True, "pad_present": True})

seq = [("start", {"start_pb": True}), ("release", {"start_pb": False}),
       ("platen req", {"platen_req": True}),
       ("flow lost", {"flow_ok": False}),
       ("flow back", {"flow_ok": True}),
       ("start (no reset!)", {"start_pb": True}),
       ("release", {"start_pb": False}),
       ("reset", {"reset_pb": True}), ("release reset", {"reset_pb": False}),
       ("start", {"start_pb": True})]
for name, chg in seq:
    io.update(chg); scan(io, rungs)
    print(f"{name:20s} fault={io['fault']!s:5s} pump={io['pump']!s:5s} "
          f"cond={io['cond']!s:5s}")
```

The printout shows `start (no reset!)` leaving the pump off because the fault latch is still set; only after `reset` does a start succeed. The fault rung is itself a seal-in: the fault bit holds itself on through the OR, and only `reset_pb` in series can break it. Requiring a deliberate reset after a fault is standard machine-control practice, for the same reason the basic seal-in does not auto-restart.
