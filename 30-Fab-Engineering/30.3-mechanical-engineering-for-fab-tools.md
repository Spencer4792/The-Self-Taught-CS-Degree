## Start here: a record player built to sand the record flat

Picture a record player. A platter spins at constant speed, a tonearm reaches in from the side and rides the surface. Now rebuild it with one absurd goal: instead of reading the disc, it must sand the disc perfectly flat, to within about a hundred atoms of flatness, while a robot swaps discs every ninety seconds, around the clock, for years.

That machine is a rotary CMP tool, and every mechanical topic in this chapter is one of the upgrades the record player needs:

- The tonearm becomes a **carrier head** that presses the disc face-down against the platter with a precisely controlled force. How much force, and where it goes, is statics.
- The plastic chassis becomes half a tonne of steel and polymer-composite structure, because a frame that flexes even a few microns under load sands a wave into the disc. That is materials and stiffness.
- The platter and the disc both spin, and the sanding rate at each point depends on how fast the surface moves under it. Getting every point of the disc to see the same speed is kinematics, and it has a clean, satisfying answer we will derive.
- A record player hums when the motor is unbalanced and howls when feedback couples through the chassis. Our sander has the same diseases at higher stakes: bearings, imbalance, and resonance are the vibration section.
- The disc-changing robot runs on compressed air and vacuum. Sizing those actuators is a one-line formula used carefully.
- And the whole machine only works if error is managed like money: every part is allowed to spend a few microns from a fixed budget, and the design's job is to make sure the total stays inside spec. That is precision engineering.

Keep the record player in mind. Each section upgrades one subsystem, and by the end you will be able to walk around a real polisher and name what each chunk of metal is for.

## Statics on the fab floor

A machine that is not accelerating is in **static equilibrium**: the forces sum to zero and the torques sum to zero. That sounds too simple to be useful, and it is exactly what you use to answer questions like "how hard is the head actually pushing?" and "can this crane pick lift the head assembly safely?"

The tool for these questions is the **free-body diagram**: isolate one part, draw every force acting on it, and require the sums to vanish. Let's isolate the wafer during polish. Three things touch it: the carrier membrane pushing down from behind, the pad pushing up on its face, and the retaining ring guarding its edge.

```text
              carrier head body
        ┌───────────────────────────┐
        │   pressurized membrane    │
        │   (zones push down, P)    │
   ring │ ┌───────────────────────┐ │ ring
    ▼   ▼ ▼   wafer, face down  ▼ ▼   ▼
  ══════╪═══════════════════════════╪══════   pad (pushes up, N)
        │◄──────── 300 mm ─────────►│
   friction from moving pad drags the wafer sideways;
   the retaining ring reacts that drag force
```

Vertically: the membrane pressure P acts over the wafer's back area A, the pad reacts with a normal force N, and equilibrium gives N = P times A (wafer weight is negligible next to the pneumatic force, as you are about to see). Horizontally: the pad slides under the wafer and drags it sideways with friction, and the only thing stopping the wafer from squirting out of the head is the **retaining ring**. That is why the ring is a wear item and why its condition shows up in edge results, a thread [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md) picks up.

Now put numbers on it. Recipes state pressure in psi because the industry grew up in the US; physics wants pascals. One psi is 6894.76 Pa. A 300 mm wafer has radius 0.150 m.

```python
import math

PSI = 6894.757          # pascals per psi

# Downforce on a 300 mm wafer at 2 psi
r_wafer = 0.150                       # m
area = math.pi * r_wafer**2           # m^2
p = 2.0 * PSI                         # Pa
force = p * area                      # N

print(f"Wafer area          : {area*1e4:7.1f} cm^2 ({area:.5f} m^2)")
print(f"2 psi in SI         : {p/1000:7.2f} kPa")
print(f"Downforce           : {force:7.1f} N  (~{force/9.81:.0f} kg-force, ~{force/4.448:.0f} lbf)")

# Retaining ring: annulus from 152 mm to 168 mm diameter at 3 psi
a_ring = math.pi * (0.084**2 - 0.076**2)
f_ring = 3.0 * PSI * a_ring
print(f"Ring area           : {a_ring*1e4:7.1f} cm^2")
print(f"Ring force at 3 psi : {f_ring:7.1f} N")

# Overhung load during rigging: 200 kg head on a 0.5 m lever arm
m_head, arm = 200.0, 0.5
moment = m_head * 9.81 * arm
print(f"Overhung moment     : {moment:7.1f} N*m  (200 kg at 0.5 m)")
```

```text
Wafer area          :   706.9 cm^2 (0.07069 m^2)
2 psi in SI         :   13.79 kPa
Downforce           :   974.7 N  (~99 kg-force, ~219 lbf)
Ring area           :    40.2 cm^2
Ring force at 3 psi :    83.2 N
Overhung moment     :   981.0 N*m  (200 kg at 0.5 m)
```

Sit with that first number. A "gentle" 2 psi polish presses the wafer into the pad with the weight of an adult passenger, about 975 N, spread over the disc. This is why nothing about a carrier head is dainty: the spindle, the gimbal, and the head structure all carry passenger-scale loads while holding micron-scale geometry.

The second calculation in the block matters during install week rather than during polish. Torque is force times lever arm, and **overhung loads**, masses hanging off to one side of their support, generate moments that tip things over. A 200 kg head assembly on a 0.5 m arm produces about 981 N*m of moment; whatever bolts, crane pick points, or temporary fixtures react that moment must be rated for it. Rigging plans for tool install (the daily bread of [30.5](30.5-field-service-and-tool-install.md)) are applied statics: find the center of gravity, keep it between the pick points, and never let a lever arm sneak up on you. Most rigging accidents are torque-balance mistakes, not strength mistakes.

## Materials and stiffness: why the frame is the quietest part on the tool

Push on any solid and it deforms. **Stress** is force per unit area, sigma = F/A, in pascals. **Strain** is fractional deformation, epsilon = delta-L / L, dimensionless. For small loads they are proportional:

```text
   sigma = E * epsilon        (Hooke's law for materials)

   E = Young's modulus, a property of the material:
       steel    ~ 200 GPa
       aluminum ~  69 GPa
       granite  ~  50 GPa (varies)
       polyurethane pad ~ 0.05 GPa or less
```

Load a metal gently and it springs back when released: **elastic** behavior. Load it past its yield strength and it deforms permanently: **plastic** behavior. This gives us the single most useful distinction in machine design, one that trips up almost everyone at first:

- **Strength** is the stress a part tolerates before it yields or breaks.
- **Stiffness** is how little it deflects while carrying a load it easily tolerates.

A machine frame is almost never a strength problem. It is almost always a stiffness problem. Watch the numbers:

```python
# Stiffness and thermal expansion at micron scale
E_STEEL = 200e9      # Pa, Young's modulus
E_AL    = 69e9       # Pa
ALPHA_AL    = 23e-6  # 1/K, thermal expansion coefficient
ALPHA_STEEL = 11.7e-6

import math

# 1) Elastic compression of a spindle shaft under CMP downforce
F = 975.0                            # N (from the statics section)
d_shaft = 0.020                      # 20 mm shaft
A = math.pi * (d_shaft/2)**2
stress = F / A                       # Pa
strain = stress / E_STEEL
shorten = strain * 0.300             # over a 300 mm length
print(f"Shaft stress   : {stress/1e6:6.2f} MPa (yield of mild steel ~250 MPa)")
print(f"Shaft strain   : {strain*1e6:6.1f} microstrain")
print(f"Shortening     : {shorten*1e6:6.2f} um over 300 mm")

# 2) Thermal growth of a 300 mm fixture over a 5 C fab excursion
L, dT = 0.300, 5.0
grow_al    = ALPHA_AL * L * dT
grow_steel = ALPHA_STEEL * L * dT
print(f"Aluminum grows : {grow_al*1e6:6.1f} um")
print(f"Steel grows    : {grow_steel*1e6:6.1f} um")
print(f"Differential   : {(grow_al-grow_steel)*1e6:6.1f} um  <- an Al bracket on a steel frame fights itself")
```

```text
Shaft stress   :   3.10 MPa (yield of mild steel ~250 MPa)
Shaft strain   :   15.5 microstrain
Shortening     :   4.66 um over 300 mm
Aluminum grows :   34.5 um
Steel grows    :   17.6 um
Differential   :   16.9 um  <- an Al bracket on a steel frame fights itself
```

Read the first result closely. The spindle shaft is loaded to about one percent of its yield strength: by strength standards it is comically overbuilt. Yet it still compresses almost 5 um, and 5 um is enormous against a planarity target measured in tens of nanometers. Strength was never the issue; the fight is entirely about deflection. That is why tool bases are thick castings, granite slabs, or heavily ribbed weldments. Mass and section make them stiff, and cast iron and granite bring a second gift, **damping**: internal friction that swallows vibration energy instead of ringing like a bell. Our record player got a half-tonne plinth not to survive the load but to refuse to move under it.

The thermal numbers teach the second lesson: at micron scale, **temperature is a load**. Every material grows with temperature by its expansion coefficient alpha, and a routine 5 C drift grows a 300 mm aluminum part by 34 um. Worse than uniform growth is differential growth: bolt an aluminum bracket to a steel frame and the pair bends as temperature moves, because the two metals want different lengths. Precision fixtures either use one material throughout, use low-expansion alloys where it counts, or are designed so growth happens in a direction that does not matter.

One more materials note specific to CMP: **corrosion pairing**. Slurry is an electrolyte, often oxidizing by design (see [30.2](30.2-chemical-engineering-for-cmp.md)). Put two dissimilar metals in contact in an electrolyte and you have built a battery; the more active metal corrodes preferentially. This is **galvanic corrosion**, and it is why wetted hardware is 316 stainless, titanium, or polymer, why an innocent carbon-steel washer swapped into a wet area during a repair will bloom rust within weeks, and why "same part, wrong material" is a real failure mode in spare-parts logistics.

## The kinematics of rotary CMP: the matched-speed result

Here is the centerpiece of the chapter. Preston's equation from [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md) says removal rate is RR = Kp * P * v, where v is the relative sliding speed between wafer and pad. If v differs from point to point across the wafer, removal differs from point to point, and the wafer comes out wedge-shaped or dished. So the mechanical question underneath uniformity is: what does the field of relative speed look like across the wafer, and can we make it uniform?

Set up coordinates. Put the platen's rotation axis at the origin, spinning at angular velocity omega_p (counterclockwise positive, radians per second). The carrier head's axis sits at position vector d, a fixed distance d from the origin, and spins the wafer at omega_c. Take any point on the wafer, at position vector r measured from the carrier center, so its position from the platen center is d + r.

A rigid body rotating at omega about the origin moves each of its points at velocity omega x position (the cross product with the angular velocity vector along z). So:

```text
  velocity of the pad under our point:
      v_pad   = omega_p x (d + r)

  velocity of the wafer material at that point:
      v_wafer = omega_c x r          (the carrier center is fixed)

  relative sliding velocity, pad with respect to wafer:
      v_rel = v_pad - v_wafer
            = omega_p x d + omega_p x r - omega_c x r
            = omega_p x d + (omega_p - omega_c) x r
```

Look at the structure of that last line. The first term, omega_p x d, is the same for every point on the wafer: it does not contain **r** at all. The second term is proportional to (omega_p - omega_c). So:

**If omega_c = omega_p, the second term vanishes and every point on the wafer sees exactly the same relative velocity, v_rel = omega_p x d, with magnitude omega * d.** Not just the same speed: the same vector, at every point, at every instant. As the wafer turns, the direction of that vector sweeps around in the wafer's own frame, so the abrasion direction rotates evenly over each point too. This is the algebraic identity behind a rule you will see in nearly every rotary CMP recipe: platen and head speeds are set equal or nearly equal. At 60 rpm (omega = 6.28 rad/s) with the head axis 170 mm from the platen axis, every point on the wafer slides at 6.28 * 0.170 = 1.07 m/s, which is the "around a meter per second" figure quoted in 17.7.

Notice what the result does not depend on: the wafer's radius never appears. The same trick works for a 200 mm wafer, a 300 mm wafer, or a wafer the size of the platen, as long as it stays on the pad. It also tells you the two knobs that set the sliding speed: spin both faster, or move the head axis farther out.

When the speeds are mismatched, the (omega_p - omega_c) x r term switches on and the speed varies across the wafer, fastest on the side of the wafer where the two terms align. Let's compute the field:

```python
import numpy as np

def relative_speed_field(omega_p, omega_c, d, r_wafer=0.150, n=41):
    """Instantaneous |v_rel| at a grid of wafer points.
    Platen center at origin, carrier center at (d, 0)."""
    xs = np.linspace(-r_wafer, r_wafer, n)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, xs)                 # wafer coords, origin at carrier center
    mask = X**2 + Y**2 <= r_wafer**2
    dw = omega_p - omega_c
    # v_rel = omega_p (z x d_vec) + dw (z x r); z x (x, y) = (-y, x); d_vec = (d, 0)
    vx = -dw*Y
    vy = omega_p*d + dw*X
    speed = np.sqrt(vx**2 + vy**2)[mask]
    return speed

rpm = 2*np.pi/60.0
omega_p = 60 * rpm            # platen at 60 rpm
d = 0.170                     # carrier center 170 mm from platen center

for ratio in (0.80, 0.95, 1.00, 1.05, 1.20):
    s = relative_speed_field(omega_p, ratio*omega_p, d)
    nu = 100.0 * (s.max() - s.min()) / (2.0 * s.mean())
    print(f"head/platen = {ratio:4.2f} | v_rel m/s: min {s.min():.3f} "
          f"max {s.max():.3f} mean {s.mean():.3f} | range NU {nu:5.1f}%")
```

```text
head/platen = 0.80 | v_rel m/s: min 0.880 max 1.257 mean 1.072 | range NU  17.6%
head/platen = 0.95 | v_rel m/s: min 1.021 max 1.115 mean 1.068 | range NU   4.4%
head/platen = 1.00 | v_rel m/s: min 1.068 max 1.068 mean 1.068 | range NU   0.0%
head/platen = 1.05 | v_rel m/s: min 1.021 max 1.115 mean 1.068 | range NU   4.4%
head/platen = 1.20 | v_rel m/s: min 0.880 max 1.257 mean 1.073 | range NU  17.6%
```

At matched speed the instantaneous field is perfectly flat. A 20 percent mismatch spreads instantaneous speeds over a 35 percent band. Preston says removal tracks v, so uniform v is half the uniformity battle won by pure geometry, before any pressure tuning. The other half, pressure profiles, pad state, and slurry, belongs to [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md).

There is a subtlety hiding here that we will expose properly in the Build it section: because the wafer rotates, each material point samples many positions in that instantaneous field, and the time-averaged speed comes out much more uniform than the instantaneous snapshot suggests. The matched-speed rule survives the subtlety, but for a more interesting reason than the snapshot shows, so hold that thought.

Two real-world footnotes. First, real recipes often run the head a few rpm off the platen (60/63 is a classic pairing) rather than exactly locked. A small offset makes each wafer point trace a path that drifts across the pad's grooves and conditioning marks instead of retracing the same track, averaging out pad-level texture. The kinematic penalty, as the simulator will show, is tiny. Second, heads also sweep slowly back and forth along the platen radius, which changes d over time; since v_rel = omega * d at matched speed, the sweep modulates the removal rate and spreads wear across the pad.

## Rotating machinery: bearings, gimbals, and seals

The record player had one bearing that mattered. Our sander has dozens, and when one gets sick the wafers know before the humans do.

### Bearings

A **bearing** constrains a shaft to pure rotation. The families you will meet:

- **Ball bearings**: hardened balls rolling between an inner and outer race. Cheap, stiff enough for most jobs, and by far the most common. Point contact limits load.
- **Roller bearings**: cylinders or tapered rollers instead of balls; line contact carries more load. Tapered pairs carry combined radial and axial (thrust) loads.
- **Air bearings**: the shaft floats on a thin pressurized air film. No contact, so no wear and near-zero friction and runout; used in metrology spindles. Low stiffness compared to steel-on-steel and it needs clean dry air, always.
- **Hydrostatic bearings**: same floating idea with pressurized oil or water; very stiff and very well damped, at the cost of a pump and plumbing.

Precision spindle bearings are installed with **preload**: a deliberate built-in axial squeeze that eliminates internal clearance. Without preload the balls rattle in their races and the shaft has a tiny dead zone of free motion; with preload the bearing acts like a stiff spring with no slack. Preload is set by shimming, spring washers, or matched-ground bearing pairs, and getting it wrong in either direction is fatal: too little brings back the rattle, too much overheats the bearing and cooks its grease.

Spindle bearings die from contamination (one slurry droplet inside a bearing is sandpaper in the raceway), lost or degraded lubricant, brinelled races from an impact (dropping a head assembly two centimeters onto a bench can dent races through the balls), and plain fatigue: race surfaces eventually flake, a process called spalling. And they announce their illness acoustically, in stages: a healthy bearing hisses smoothly; early damage adds a periodic tick as each ball rolls over a spall; late damage becomes a rough growl you can feel through the frame. The ticking has arithmetic behind it. A defect on the outer race gets struck once per ball passage, at the **ball pass frequency, outer race (BPFO)**, roughly 0.4 times the ball count times the shaft rotation frequency; an inner-race defect shows up near 0.6 times ball count times shaft frequency. Those defect tones and their harmonics are exactly what the vibration watchdog in the mini-project listens for.

### Runout, imbalance, and the gimbal

**Runout** is the wobble of a rotating surface: mount a dial indicator against a spinning shaft and the needle's swing is the runout, the sum of bearing imperfection, shaft bend, and mounting error. **Imbalance** is mass off the rotation axis; it produces a rotating centrifugal force F = m * e * omega^2 (mass times eccentricity times angular speed squared) that shakes the machine once per revolution. Note the omega squared: doubling speed quadruples the shaking force, which is why a platen that is smooth at 30 rpm can buzz at 90.

Now a question the record player raises directly: the tonearm had a counterweight and a pivot so the stylus could follow a warped record. What follows the pad for us? The pad surface is never perfectly parallel to the wafer, the platen has runout, and the pad wears unevenly. If the carrier head were rigidly fixed, all that error would become pressure variation across the wafer, exactly what we cannot afford. So the head is built on a **gimbal**: a pivot (a spherical bearing, flexure, or ball joint) that lets the wafer plane tip a fraction of a degree to follow the pad while still transmitting the axial downforce and the drive torque. The membrane behind the wafer then handles the fine-grained pressure distribution on top of the gimbal's coarse alignment. Gimbal plus membrane is the tool's two-stage answer to "the pad is not flat."

```text
   spindle (spins, pushes down ~1000 N)
        │
     [gimbal pivot]  ◄─ head tips +/- a fraction of a degree
        │
   ┌────┴────┐
   │ membrane│  ◄─ zone pressures shape the force distribution
   └─┬─────┬─┘
     │wafer│    ◄─ follows the pad's tilt AND its texture
  ═══╧═════╧═══  pad (never quite flat, never quite level)
```

### Seals

A CMP tool is a machine full of spinning shafts passing through walls that separate slurry from bearings, and seals are the border guards:

- **O-rings** seal static joints. Two numbers define one: its size and its **durometer** (rubber hardness). Material matters as much as size: nitrile swells in some slurries, EPDM and fluoroelastomers each have chemistries they tolerate and chemistries they do not. An o-ring in the wrong groove, twisted during assembly, or pinched at a groove corner will seal at first and weep later; the groove is designed for a specific compression percentage, and the wrong cross-section gives either too little squeeze (leak) or too much (extrusion and tearing).
- **Lip seals** ride on rotating shafts: a flexible polymer lip, often spring-loaded, drags lightly on the shaft. Cheap, slightly lossy, and they wear a groove into the shaft over years.
- **Mechanical seals** press two lapped hard faces together, one spinning and one stationary, with a liquid film between them. This is the serious option for slurry-wetted rotating joints and it is a precision part: mishandle the faces during a rebuild and it leaks from day one.

Torque gets from motors into spindles through **couplings** (rigid, or flexible ones that forgive small misalignment between shafts) and **belts**, which are quiet and cheap but stretch, wear, and can slip. The motors themselves, and the drives that spin them, are [30.1](30.1-electrical-engineering-for-the-fab.md)'s territory; mechanically, what matters is that every coupling and belt is a compliance (a spring) in the torque path, and springs in the torque path come back to haunt us in the next section.

## Vibration: the machine as an orchestra of springs

Every stiff structure is a spring, every mass on a spring oscillates, and a fab tool is masses on springs all the way down. The model that explains most of what you will see is one mass m on one spring k with one damper c, driven by a force F(t):

```text
   m * x'' + c * x' + k * x = F(t)

   natural frequency:  omega_n = sqrt(k/m)     f_n = omega_n / 2*pi
   damping ratio:      zeta = c / (2*sqrt(k*m))
```

Left alone after a tap, the system rings at f_n and the ringing decays at a rate set by zeta. Driven by a sinusoidal force, it settles into steady oscillation at the drive frequency, with an amplitude that depends violently on how close the drive is to f_n. Drive well below f_n and the mass follows the force quasi-statically. Drive well above and the mass barely moves; it cannot keep up. Drive at f_n and the response is limited only by damping: this is **resonance**, and lightly damped steel structures (zeta of a few percent) amplify by factors of ten or more.

Where does the driving force come from in a polisher? Mostly rotating imbalance: that F = m e omega^2 force sweeping around once per revolution, at exactly the rotation frequency. So the machine is a set of resonators being frequency-swept every time a spindle changes speed. Let's watch a 50 kg spindle assembly with a 30 Hz natural frequency get swept through resonance, integrating the equation of motion numerically and checking against the closed-form steady-state amplitude X = F0 / sqrt((k - m omega^2)^2 + (c omega)^2):

```python
import math

# Spring-mass-damper driven by a rotating-imbalance force F0*sin(2*pi*f*t)
m = 50.0                      # kg   (a spindle assembly)
f_n = 30.0                    # Hz   natural frequency we chose
k = m * (2*math.pi*f_n)**2    # N/m
zeta = 0.05                   # 5 percent damping, typical bolted steel
c = 2*zeta*math.sqrt(k*m)
F0 = 100.0                    # N drive amplitude

def steady_amplitude(f_drive, t_end=2.0, dt=2e-4):
    """Semi-implicit Euler, measure peak x over the last 25 percent."""
    w = 2*math.pi*f_drive
    x, v = 0.0, 0.0
    peak, t = 0.0, 0.0
    steps = int(t_end/dt)
    for i in range(steps):
        a = (F0*math.sin(w*t) - c*v - k*x)/m
        v += a*dt
        x += v*dt
        t += dt
        if i > 0.75*steps:
            peak = max(peak, abs(x))
    return peak

print(f"f_n = {f_n:.1f} Hz, k = {k/1e6:.2f} MN/m, c = {c:.0f} N*s/m, zeta = {zeta}")
print(f"{'drive Hz':>9} | {'sim amp um':>10} | {'theory um':>9}")
for f in (10, 20, 25, 28, 30, 32, 35, 45, 60):
    w = 2*math.pi*f
    x_theory = F0/math.sqrt((k - m*w*w)**2 + (c*w)**2)
    x_sim = steady_amplitude(f)
    print(f"{f:9.0f} | {x_sim*1e6:10.1f} | {x_theory*1e6:9.1f}")
```

```text
f_n = 30.0 Hz, k = 1.78 MN/m, c = 942 N*s/m, zeta = 0.05
 drive Hz | sim amp um | theory um
       10 |       63.3 |      63.3
       20 |      100.4 |     100.6
       25 |      177.0 |     177.7
       28 |      350.7 |     353.7
       30 |      562.9 |     562.9
       32 |      326.5 |     323.1
       35 |      149.4 |     148.3
       45 |       44.9 |      44.7
       60 |       18.8 |      18.7
```

A 100 N wiggle produces 63 um of motion at low frequency but 563 um at resonance, a 9x amplification, and this with only 5 percent damping, which is on the good side for bolted steel. Two working rules fall out. First, never park a rotation speed on a structural resonance; if 1800 rpm (30 Hz) rings the frame, run 1740 or 1860. Second, damping is what caps the peak, which is another vote for cast iron, granite, and polymer-concrete bases over welded aluminum.

**Isolation** uses the same physics in reverse. Above roughly 1.4 times f_n, the mass moves less than the base shakes; far above f_n, the transmitted motion falls off as the square of the frequency ratio. So to protect a tool from floor vibration you mount it on soft springs, usually **air mounts**: rubber-diaphragm air springs giving the tool a natural frequency of a few hertz. A 2 Hz mount attenuates a 30 Hz disturbance by roughly a factor of (30/2)^2 - 1, about 224, transmitting under half a percent. The fab building plays the same trick at scale: process floors are massive, stiff waffle slabs on their own columns, isolated from foot traffic and mechanical rooms, because the floor is the zeroth spring under every tool. This is why tools get re-qualified for vibration after a move: same tool, different spot on the slab, different base excitation.

One more vibration species matters to CMP specifically: **stick-slip**. Sliding friction is often higher when surfaces are barely moving than when they slide fast. Put that negative slope in series with a compliant drive (a belt, a long shaft, the pad's own elasticity) and the system self-oscillates: grab, wind up, release, grab again, thousands of times per second. That is what a squealing pad is, the same mechanism as a squeaking door hinge and a bowed violin string. Pad squeal in CMP tracks the friction state at the wafer-pad interface, which is why experienced engineers treat a change in the tool's sound as process data, and why friction-based endpoint (watching motor current, per 17.7) works at all.

## Pneumatics and vacuum: the tool's muscles

Nearly everything on a fab tool that moves in a straight line moves on compressed air, and nearly everything that holds a wafer holds it with vacuum. The supply is **CDA**, clean dry air: filtered, oil-free, dried to a very low dew point, distributed at roughly 550 to 700 kPa gauge (80 to 100 psi). A **regulator** drops and holds pressure at each point of use; an electro-pneumatic regulator does the same under electronic command and is the actuator behind the carrier head's zone pressures, sitting inside a feedback loop with a pressure transducer.

The whole subject runs on one formula, force equals pressure times area, applied with bookkeeping care:

```python
import math

PSI = 6894.757

# 1) Pneumatic cylinder: 50 mm bore at 6 bar (~87 psi) CDA
bore, rod = 0.050, 0.020
p = 6e5                                       # Pa gauge
a_push = math.pi*(bore/2)**2
a_pull = math.pi*((bore/2)**2 - (rod/2)**2)   # rod steals area on retract
print(f"Extend force  : {p*a_push:6.0f} N")
print(f"Retract force : {p*a_pull:6.0f} N (rod side is weaker)")

# 2) Vacuum end effector: two 15 mm suction pads at 70 kPa below atmosphere
a_pads = 2 * math.pi*0.0075**2
f_hold = 70e3 * a_pads
m_wafer = 2330 * math.pi*0.150**2 * 775e-6    # silicon density * volume
w_wafer = m_wafer * 9.81
mu = 0.3                                       # pad-on-silicon friction, rough
a_max = mu * f_hold / m_wafer
print(f"Wafer mass    : {m_wafer*1000:6.1f} g (weight {w_wafer:.2f} N)")
print(f"Holding force : {f_hold:6.1f} N -> normal safety factor ~{f_hold/w_wafer:.0f}x")
print(f"Max lateral acceleration before slip: {a_max:.0f} m/s^2 (~{a_max/9.81:.1f} g)")

# 3) Membrane carrier zone force: outer zone (120-150 mm radius) at 0.3 psi extra
a_zone = math.pi*(0.150**2 - 0.120**2)
print(f"Edge-zone area: {a_zone*1e4:6.1f} cm^2, +0.3 psi adds {0.3*PSI*a_zone:.1f} N at the edge")
```

```text
Extend force  :   1178 N
Retract force :    990 N (rod side is weaker)
Wafer mass    :  127.6 g (weight 1.25 N)
Holding force :   24.7 N -> normal safety factor ~20x
Max lateral acceleration before slip: 58 m/s^2 (~5.9 g)
Edge-zone area:  254.5 cm^2, +0.3 psi adds 52.6 N at the edge
```

Walk through the three results:

**Cylinders.** A pneumatic cylinder is a piston in a tube: pressurize one side and the piston extends, pressurize the other and it retracts. The rod occupies area on the retract side, so retract force is always lower, here 990 N versus 1178 N. Cylinders on tools raise heads, clamp doors, and swing slurry arms; each one is commanded by a **solenoid valve**, an electromagnet-driven spool that routes air to one side or the other. The valve is the weak link. Its spool rides in a close-fitting bore, and contamination, worn seals, or a tired return spring make it hesitate or stick partway; a sticking valve produces the classic maddening intermittent, an axis that "sometimes doesn't make it" and posts a timeout error once a shift. When an actuator misbehaves intermittently, suspect the valve before the cylinder, and check the exhaust mufflers too: a clogged muffler slows the vented side and the motion drags.

**Vacuum handling.** A robot end effector holds the wafer with small suction zones. The numbers show comfortable margin against gravity, about 20x, but the binding constraint is sideways: only friction resists lateral acceleration, and mu times the holding force caps robot acceleration near 6 g here, which real robots stay far below. There are two ways to make the vacuum: a **venturi ejector** (compressed air blown through a nozzle drags a vacuum from a side port; no moving parts, instant on, air-hungry, moderate vacuum) and a vacuum pump with a plumbed line (deeper and steadier, shared, and slower to respond at the point of use). Handling systems mostly use venturis for grip-release cycles; the deep process vacuum of 17.7 is a different world entirely.

**Zones.** The carrier head's membrane sits on several independently regulated concentric pressure chambers. The arithmetic shows why this is such an effective knob: a barely-perceptible 0.3 psi trim on the outer zone shifts 53 N onto the wafer's edge annulus. Preston converts pressure differences into removal-rate differences point for point, so the zone pressures are the primary within-wafer uniformity control, and tuning them against measured wafer profiles is a core [30.4](30.4-cmp-process-engineering.md) activity. Mechanically, your job is to make sure the plumbing tells the truth: a pinhole leak in a membrane, a kinked line, or a drifting transducer turns the process engineer's careful zone recipe into fiction.

## Precision engineering: spending the micron budget

Now the discipline that ties the chapter together. Three words that get confused daily:

- **Accuracy**: how close you land to the true target.
- **Repeatability**: how tightly your attempts cluster, wherever they land.
- **Resolution**: the smallest increment you can command or read.

A machine can repeat to 1 um yet be 50 um from nominal (repeatable, inaccurate: often fixable with calibration). A readout with 0.1 um resolution guarantees nothing about either. Machine builders chase repeatability first, because a repeatable error can be mapped and compensated; an unrepeatable one is noise forever.

The organizing tool is the **error budget**: list every contributor to position error at the point that matters, assign each a magnitude, and combine them (worst case, or root-sum-square for independent errors). We have already met the line items: elastic deflection under load (4.7 um from the spindle example), thermal growth (tens of um per 5 C), bearing runout (single-digit um for good rolling bearings), and vibration amplitude. The budget forces honesty: there is no point buying a 0.1 um encoder for an axis whose structure flexes 5 um.

The remaining line items each get a paragraph:

**Kinematic mounting.** A rigid body has exactly six degrees of freedom: three translations, three rotations. A **kinematic mount** constrains exactly six, no more, no fewer, classically with three balls resting in three v-grooves. Constrain fewer and the part can shift; constrain more (say, a flat plate bolted at four corners) and the part must bend to satisfy the redundant constraints, so its shape now depends on bolt torque and surface flatness. Exactly-six mounting gives position that is repeatable to microns after removal and replacement, which is why metrology fixtures and swappable precision assemblies use it.

**Screws, backlash, and direct drive.** Rotary motors become linear motion through a screw and nut, and the choice sets the axis's character:

- A **leadscrew** slides a plain nut along the thread: cheap, self-locking at steep ratios, high friction.
- A **ballscrew** recirculates hardened balls between screw and nut: efficient, precise, the default for served axes.
- Any screw-nut pair has **backlash**: reverse direction and the nut crosses its internal clearance before motion resumes, so the axis loses a small, direction-dependent amount every reversal. Preloaded nuts squeeze backlash out the same way bearing preload removes rattle.
- **Direct drive** (motor on the axis, no transmission) eliminates backlash and belt compliance entirely, at the price of a bigger motor; platens and carrier spindles are natural candidates because they spin continuously and care about torque smoothness.

Whatever the drive, an **encoder** measures the actual position and closes the loop; the control electronics are [30.1](30.1-electrical-engineering-for-the-fab.md)'s story, but where the encoder is mounted is a mechanical decision, and it leads to the most famous rule in precision engineering.

**Abbe error.** Measure a length along a line offset from the axis where the motion happens, and any small angular wobble theta of the moving part turns into a position error of offset times theta:

```text
        reading here ──►│#│  scale, offset L from the spindle line
                        │#│
   ┌────────────────────┴─┴┐
   │      carriage      (tips by tiny angle theta)
   └───────────■───────────┘
               ■ ◄── work happens on this line
   error at the work = L * theta
   (0.1 milliradian of tilt with L = 100 mm  ->  10 um of error
    that the scale cannot see)
```

The scale reads perfectly and is perfectly wrong. The **Abbe principle** says: put the measurement axis on the working axis, or account for the offset times every angle in your budget. This single idea explains a lot of otherwise-odd machine layouts, including why good height gauges and interferometers are arranged the way they are.

**Fasteners as preload devices.** A torque wrench is a force-control tool in disguise: tightening torque T stretches the bolt to a tension roughly F = T / (K * d), with d the bolt diameter and K a friction factor near 0.2 for dry threads. An M12 bolt at 80 N*m carries about 33 kN of preload, which is what actually holds the joint: the clamped parts are springs in compression, and the joint survives by never letting them separate. Now picture six bolts around a platen or a pump flange. Torque them unevenly, or in a circle instead of a star pattern, and you build an uneven stress field into the part; on a precision surface that arrives as warp you can measure, and on a gasketed flange as a leak at the last-tightened side. Hence the ritual you will follow on every rebuild: star (criss-cross) pattern, staged passes at 30, 60, then 100 percent of spec torque, calibrated wrench. The ritual is stress-field management, not superstition.

**Fits.** Look at a bearing drawing and you will see the shaft and bore dimensioned in microns, because the assembly's behavior is set by the fit: a clearance fit leaves a gap (parts slide), an **interference fit** makes the shaft a few microns larger than the bore (parts grip by elastic squeeze). Bearings typically take an interference fit on the rotating race so the race cannot creep and fret. You cannot push a 20 um interference together by hand, so you press it with a controlled ram or you cheat with temperature: heating a 50 mm bearing bore by 80 C expands it about 48 um (alpha * D * dT), and it drops on, then shrinks to grip. This is also the answer to a question every new engineer asks in the spares crib: no, the bearing is not "stuck," and no, we are not getting a bigger hammer.

## Build it: a CMP kinematics and removal simulator

Time to make the kinematics section fully quantitative. The earlier snippet computed the instantaneous speed field. But a wafer point does not sit still: it rides the carrier's rotation, sampling different parts of the field each instant. What determines removal is the time integral of Preston's equation along each material point's path. This program integrates it.

The model: material point at wafer-frame position (x0, y0) is rotated by the carrier angle omega_c * t, the relative velocity formula from our derivation is evaluated at its current position, and removal Kp * P * |v_rel| * dt accumulates over the polish. We sweep the head speed around the platen speed and report both the instantaneous speed nonuniformity and the end-of-polish removal nonuniformity (std/mean, the metric fabs call WIWNU when applied to thickness maps).

```python
"""CMP kinematics simulator: relative-speed field and Preston removal
for a rotary polisher. Platen center at origin, carrier center fixed
at (d, 0). All angles in radians, SI units throughout."""
import numpy as np

RPM = 2*np.pi/60.0

def wafer_grid(r_wafer=0.150, n=61):
    xs = np.linspace(-r_wafer, r_wafer, n)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, xs)
    mask = X**2 + Y**2 <= r_wafer**2
    return X[mask], Y[mask]        # coords of material points, wafer frame

def removal_map(omega_p, omega_c, d, polish_time=60.0, n_t=360,
                kp=1.0e-13, pressure=13.8e3):
    """Integrate Preston removal RR = kp*P*|v_rel| for each material
    point over polish_time, sampling the wafer rotation."""
    x0, y0 = wafer_grid()
    t = np.linspace(0.0, polish_time, n_t, endpoint=False)
    dt = polish_time / n_t
    removal = np.zeros_like(x0)
    dw = omega_p - omega_c
    for ti in t:
        # material point rotates with the carrier
        cs, sn = np.cos(omega_c*ti), np.sin(omega_c*ti)
        x = cs*x0 - sn*y0
        y = sn*x0 + cs*y0
        # v_rel = omega_p (z x d_vec) + dw (z x r), d_vec = (d, 0)
        vx = -dw*y
        vy = omega_p*d + dw*x
        removal += kp * pressure * np.sqrt(vx**2 + vy**2) * dt
    return removal

def instantaneous_speed(omega_p, omega_c, d):
    x0, y0 = wafer_grid()
    dw = omega_p - omega_c
    return np.sqrt((-dw*y0)**2 + (omega_p*d + dw*x0)**2)

def nonuniformity(vals):
    return 100.0 * np.std(vals) / np.mean(vals)

omega_p = 60 * RPM
d = 0.170

print("Sweep of head speed around platen speed (platen 60 rpm, d = 170 mm)")
print(f"{'head rpm':>9} | {'inst speed NU':>13} | {'mean nm':>8} | {'min nm':>7} "
      f"| {'max nm':>7} | {'removal NU':>10}")
for head_rpm in (48, 54, 57, 59, 60, 61, 63, 66, 72):
    inst = nonuniformity(instantaneous_speed(omega_p, head_rpm*RPM, d))
    rem = removal_map(omega_p, head_rpm*RPM, d) * 1e9   # meters -> nm
    print(f"{head_rpm:9.0f} | {inst:12.2f}% | {rem.mean():8.1f} | {rem.min():7.1f} "
          f"| {rem.max():7.1f} | {nonuniformity(rem):9.3f}%")

print()
print("Same 10 percent mismatch, three offsets d: bigger d dilutes the mismatch")
for d_try in (0.120, 0.170, 0.220):
    rem = removal_map(omega_p, 66*RPM, d_try) * 1e9
    print(f"d = {d_try*1000:3.0f} mm : NU = {nonuniformity(rem):5.2f}%  "
          f"(mean speed ~ {omega_p*d_try:.2f} m/s)")
```

```text
       48 |         8.76% |     88.8 |    88.4 |    89.1 |     0.224%

```

Now the subtlety pays off. Compare the two NU columns. A 10 percent speed mismatch produces a 4.4 percent instantaneous speed spread, but the time-integrated removal nonuniformity is only 0.06 percent. Rotation averaging cancels the mismatch term to first order: as a material point circles the carrier axis, the extra velocity (omega_p - omega_c) x r points every which way over a revolution and mostly cancels, leaving only a second-order residual that grows with the square of the mismatch (check the table: doubling the mismatch from 6 to 12 rpm quadruples removal NU from 0.056 to 0.224) and shrinks with larger d. The residual always polishes the edge slightly faster than the center, since the time-averaged speed grows with wafer radius when speeds are mismatched.

So is the matched-speed rule pointless? No, and knowing exactly why is what separates "I read the recipe" from "I understand the tool":

- The second-order residual is small but systematic, and modern uniformity budgets are themselves fractions of a percent; a free 0.1 percent is worth taking.
- Under mismatch, each wafer point sees its sliding speed oscillate over every revolution. Friction, and therefore heat input and motor torque, oscillate with it. Matched speeds give steady friction, quieter motor-current signals (better friction endpoint), and less periodic excitation of exactly the resonances we studied.
- The averaging argument assumed P and Kp constant. Real pressure distributions are not perfectly flat, and removal couples pressure and speed multiplicatively; a nonuniform instantaneous speed field multiplied against a nonuniform pressure field does not average away as politely.

And the small deliberate offsets (60/63) buy pad-texture averaging at a removal-NU cost of 0.014 percent, effectively free. The simulator has reproduced actual recipe practice from first principles. Extensions worth making before you put this on GitHub: add the slow head sweep (make d a function of time), add a radial pressure profile P(rho) and watch the NU columns stop agreeing, and add a pad-wear accumulator that integrates sliding distance per pad radius to predict the wear track.

## Mini-project: a vibration watchdog

Field service engineers increasingly ship with a vibration pen or a magnet-mount accelerometer, and big fabs stream spindle vibration into predictive-maintenance systems. The analysis under all of them is the same three steps: FFT the acceleration signal, integrate energy in frequency bands chosen from bearing arithmetic, and compare against a healthy baseline. Let's build the whole pipeline on synthetic data so you can see both what the code does and what the signatures look like.

The physical story from the bearings section, encoded: a healthy spindle shows a clean tone at the rotation frequency (1x, residual imbalance) plus a small 2x (slight misalignment) over a noise floor. A failing outer race adds tones at BPFO and its harmonics, and, as damage progresses, ball impacts start ringing the bearing's own high-frequency structural resonances, raising a broad band in the kilohertz range. That high band is the early-warning channel: it rises before the tool sounds different to a human.

```python
"""Vibration watchdog: synthetic accelerometer traces for a healthy
spindle and one with a failing rolling-element bearing, then an
FFT-based band-energy health score."""
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)

FS = 20000            # samples per second
N  = FS               # 1 second of data
t  = np.arange(N) / FS

F_SHAFT = 25.0        # 1500 rpm spindle
N_BALLS = 9
BPFO = 0.4 * N_BALLS * F_SHAFT      # outer-race defect frequency ~ 90 Hz

def healthy():
    sig  = 1.00*np.sin(2*np.pi*F_SHAFT*t)            # residual imbalance, 1x
    sig += 0.10*np.sin(2*np.pi*2*F_SHAFT*t)          # small 2x (misalignment)
    sig += 0.05*np.random.randn(N)                   # broadband floor
    return sig

def failing(severity=1.0):
    sig = healthy()
    for h in (1, 2, 3, 4):                           # defect tones + harmonics
        sig += severity*0.15*h**-0.5*np.sin(2*np.pi*h*BPFO*t + 0.3*h)
    # metal-on-metal impacts excite a high-frequency resonance band
    band = np.random.randn(N)
    spec = np.fft.rfft(band)
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(N, 1/FS)
    spec[(f < 2500) | (f > 6000)] = 0
    sig += severity*0.12*np.fft.irfft(spec, N)
    return sig

def band_energy(sig, f_lo, f_hi):
    spec = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(sig))**2 / N
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(N, 1/FS)
    sel = (f >= f_lo) & (f < f_hi)
    return spec[sel].sum()

BANDS = {"1x-2x    (20-60 Hz)": (20, 60),
         "bearing  (70-400 Hz)": (70, 400),
         "HF burst (2.5-6 kHz)": (2500, 6000)}

def health_report(name, sig, baseline):
    print(f"\n{name}")
    score = 0.0
    for label, (lo, hi) in BANDS.items():
        e = band_energy(sig, lo, hi)
        db = 10*np.log10(e / baseline[label])
        flag = " <-- ALERT" if (db > 6.0 and "1x" not in label) else ""
        print(f"  {label}: {e:10.3f}   {db:+6.1f} dB vs baseline{flag}")
        if "1x" not in label:
            score += max(0.0, db)
    verdict = "HEALTHY" if score < 6 else ("WATCH" if score < 35 else "REPLACE SOON")
    print(f"  health score: {score:5.1f} dB -> {verdict}")

h = healthy()
baseline = {label: band_energy(h, lo, hi) for label, (lo, hi) in BANDS.items()}

print(f"Shaft 1x = {F_SHAFT} Hz, estimated BPFO = {BPFO:.0f} Hz, fs = {FS} Hz")
health_report("healthy spindle", healthy(), baseline)
health_report("early bearing wear (severity 0.5)", failing(0.5), baseline)
health_report("failing bearing   (severity 2.0)", failing(2.0), baseline)
```

```text

healthy spindle
  1x-2x    (20-60 Hz):   5048.695     -0.0 dB vs baseline
  bearing  (70-400 Hz):      0.831     +0.2 dB vs baseline
  HF burst (2.5-6 kHz):      8.578     -0.2 dB vs baseline
  health score:   0.2 dB -> HEALTHY

  1x-2x    (20-60 Hz):   5049.719     -0.0 dB vs baseline
  bearing  (70-400 Hz):     59.339    +18.8 dB vs baseline <-- ALERT
  HF burst (2.5-6 kHz):     21.814     +3.9 dB vs baseline
  health score:  22.7 dB -> WATCH

failing bearing   (severity 2.0)
  1x-2x    (20-60 Hz):   5049.050     -0.0 dB vs baseline
  bearing  (70-400 Hz):    934.817    +30.7 dB vs baseline <-- ALERT
```

Points worth noticing in the design, because they carry over directly to real signals:

- The 1x band barely moves across all three cases. Imbalance and bearing damage are different diseases with different spectral signatures; a watchdog that just measured overall vibration level would blur them together and miss the early case entirely, since the early defect adds almost nothing to total energy (compare 59 to the 5000 sitting in the 1x band).
- Ratios are in decibels against a per-band baseline captured when the spindle was known healthy. Absolute thresholds do not transfer between machines or even between mounting spots on one machine; baselining is what makes the score portable.
- The band edges come from arithmetic you can do at the whiteboard: shaft frequency from the recipe rpm, defect frequencies from the bearing's ball count and geometry (real bearings ship with exact BPFO/BPFI factors on the datasheet).

To grow this into a repository-worthy project: wrap it as a CLI that reads a CSV of accelerometer samples, add the inner-race band (BPFI, roughly 0.6 * ball count * shaft frequency; the exercises start you off), track band energies over simulated weeks and fit a trend line to estimate time-to-threshold, and stream results into the SPC framework you built in [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md). That combination, physics-chosen features plus simple statistics plus honest baselining, is the working core of most industrial predictive maintenance, and describing it that way in an interview lands well.

## Common mistakes

1. **Confusing strength with stiffness.** "The bracket is rated for ten times the load" says nothing about whether it deflects 50 um and ruins your alignment. Check deflection against the error budget, not stress against yield, for anything that positions something.
2. **Treating psi and kPa casually.** A recipe zone at 2.0 psi is 13.8 kPa; typing 2.0 where the software expects kPa is a 7x downforce error in the gentle direction, and the reverse mistake scraps wafers. Write units next to every number, convert explicitly, and be suspicious of any interface that does not display units.
3. **Torquing in a circle, or skipping the staged passes.** Sequential tightening around a flange drives the part into a taco shape; each bolt disturbs its neighbors. Star pattern, multiple passes, calibrated wrench, every time, even when the schedule is screaming.
4. **Blaming the cylinder when the valve is sticking.** Intermittent pneumatic faults are far more often the solenoid valve, its exhaust muffler, or a kinked line than the cylinder itself. Swap or exercise the cheap suspect first and watch the pressure at the point of use, not just at the regulator.
5. **Ignoring a change in sound.** Bearings, belts, and pads narrate their condition acoustically, and "it sounds different this week" from an operator is data with a built-in baseline. The watchdog project is that intuition made quantitative; use both.
6. **Reassembling an o-ring joint with whatever ring fits.** Groove dimensions assume a specific cross-section, squeeze, and material. A near-miss ring seals during the leak check and fails during the weekend. Match the part number, check the material against the chemistry, and lubricate per procedure.
7. **Parking a rotation speed on a resonance.** If vibration spikes at one particular rpm and calms above and below it, you have found a natural frequency, not a bearing failure. Change speed a few percent and plan the real fix (stiffen, damp, or balance); do not chase phantom bearing defects at that one speed.
8. **Forgetting that temperature is a load.** A tool qualified at dawn drifts by lunch; a fixture machined in a 26 C shop will not measure true in a 21 C fab. Any micron-level puzzle should get "what moved thermally?" on its suspect list from the start.

## On the job / interview angle

For the CMP field service and applications path ([17.6](../17-Semiconductors/17.6-semiconductor-careers.md) maps the roles, [30.5](30.5-field-service-and-tool-install.md) covers install life in detail), this chapter is the daily toolkit:

- **Install and facilities work is applied statics.** You will read rigging plans that hinge on center-of-gravity and lever-arm arithmetic, and you will level a tool with laser levels or precision machinist levels against a spec of fractions of a millimeter per meter, because the platen's spin axis must be square to gravity and the frame must not be twisted by an uneven floor. Leveling feet are set in a pattern for the same reason bolts are torqued in one.
- **Qualification is applied vibration.** After install or a major rebuild, tools get vibration checks; you will recognize a 1x line as imbalance, harmonics and defect tones as bearing trouble, and a fixed-frequency peak that ignores rpm changes as a structural resonance or something coming through the floor.
- **The classic triage call, "the head is leaving a ring."** Now you own the suspect list, ordered by mechanism: retaining ring worn or seated wrong (edge pressure boundary changed), membrane edge-zone pressure or a leak (P wrong at the edge), gimbal not articulating freely (head cannot follow the pad, edge digs), pad conditioning profile (v and Kp fine, pad surface not), head-platen speed mismatch beyond intent (the second-order edge-fast residual you simulated). You can now attach a physical mechanism and a check to each line, which is exactly what the escalation report needs.
- **Interviews.** Equipment vendors love whiteboard questions that this chapter answers directly: "why do platen and head run at the same speed?" (derive v_rel = omega_p x d + (omega_p - omega_c) x r and discuss both the first-order identity and the averaging subtlety), "how much force does 2 psi put on a 300 mm wafer?" (do the unit conversion out loud; the answer near 1000 N surprises people), and "a customer says the tool got noisy after PM, walk me through it" (bearing preload, torque pattern, something touching that should not be, baseline comparison). Showing the kinematics simulator or the watchdog repo turns those from quiz answers into a portfolio conversation.

6. A torque spec says 25 N*m on M8 bolts (d = 8 mm), dry threads, K = 0.2. Estimate the preload per bolt. Six such bolts clamp a seal flange: what total clamping force holds the gasket?
7. Extend the vibration watchdog: the same spindle (25 Hz shaft, 9 balls) develops an inner-race defect at BPFI = 0.6 * 9 * 25 = 135 Hz. Generate a signal with BPFI tones at two harmonics and show that a band around 130 to 300 Hz catches it. Why do inner-race defects often show sidebands at the shaft frequency in real data? (Answer the last part in words.)

### Solutions

**1.** Area = pi * 0.150^2 = 0.07069 m^2. Pressure = 3.5 * 6894.76 = 24,132 Pa. Force = 24,132 * 0.07069 = 1706 N. For 200 mm: area = pi * 0.100^2 = 0.03142 m^2, force = 24,132 * 0.03142 = 758 N. Same psi gives 2.25x less force (area scales with radius squared), yet the local mechanics at every point of the wafer, pressure against the pad, is identical, and Preston depends on pressure, not total force. That is why pressure is the portable recipe quantity across wafer sizes.

**2.** Point at vector r from the carrier center; position from the platen center is d + r. Pad velocity: omega_p x (d + r). Wafer velocity: omega_c x r. Subtract: v_rel = omega_p x d + (omega_p - omega_c) x r. The r-term dies when omega_c = omega_p, leaving |v_rel| = omega_p * d everywhere. At 87 rpm: omega = 87 * 2*pi/60 = 9.111 rad/s, and v = 9.111 * 0.200 = 1.82 m/s.

**3.** Differential growth = (23e-6 - 11.7e-6) * 0.250 * 3 = 8.5e-6 m, about 8.5 um. Both pins fixed means the plate must bow, shear the pins, or shift unpredictably: an over-constraint, exactly the disease kinematic mounting avoids. Slotting one hole releases the redundant constraint along the line between pins, so the plate stays located (position by round pin, rotation by slot) while free to breathe thermally.

**4.** A = F/P = 600 / 500,000 = 1.2e-3 m^2. Bore d = sqrt(4A/pi) = sqrt(4 * 1.2e-3 / 3.1416) = 0.0391 m = 39.1 mm. Next standard bore up: 40 mm, giving A = 1.257e-3 m^2 and 628 N, a thin margin; if friction or a real safety factor matters (it does), choose 50 mm and regulate down.

**5.** Want T = 0.1, so 1/(r^2 - 1) = 0.1, r^2 = 11, r = 3.32. Mount natural frequency f_n = 25 / 3.32 = 7.5 Hz or lower. Note the direction of the rule: softer mounts (lower f_n) isolate better at a given disturbance frequency, which is the counterintuitive heart of isolation; making the mounts stiffer would make transmission worse.

**6.** F = T / (K * d) = 25 / (0.2 * 0.008) = 15,625 N, roughly 15.6 kN per bolt. Six bolts: about 94 kN total, the weight of a loaded truck, squeezing the gasket. This is why a gasket joint that leaks is usually a torque or sequence problem, not a "needs more gasket goo" problem.

**7.** Runnable check:

```python
import numpy as np
np.random.seed(7)

FS = 20000; N = FS
t = np.arange(N)/FS
F_SHAFT, BPFI = 25.0, 0.6*9*25.0     # 135 Hz

sig  = 1.0*np.sin(2*np.pi*F_SHAFT*t) + 0.05*np.random.randn(N)
bad  = sig + 0.20*np.sin(2*np.pi*BPFI*t) + 0.10*np.sin(2*np.pi*2*BPFI*t)

def band_energy(s, lo, hi):
    spec = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(s))**2/N
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(N, 1/FS)
    return spec[(f >= lo) & (f < hi)].sum()

for name, s in (("healthy", sig), ("inner-race defect", bad)):
    e = band_energy(s, 130, 300)
    print(f"{name:18s}: 130-300 Hz band energy = {e:8.3f}")
```

```text
healthy           : 130-300 Hz band energy =    0.429
inner-race defect : 130-300 Hz band energy =  250.932
```

The defect raises the band energy by over 20 dB, an easy detection. Sidebands in real data: an inner-race defect rides on the shaft, so it rotates into and out of the loaded zone once per revolution. The defect's impacts are therefore amplitude-modulated at the shaft frequency, and amplitude modulation in the time domain appears as sideband pairs spaced at the shaft frequency around BPFI and its harmonics in the spectrum. Outer-race defects sit still in the load zone, so BPFO tones are typically cleaner, which is one way analysts tell the two apart.
