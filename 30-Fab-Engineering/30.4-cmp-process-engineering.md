## Start here: the pilot, not the mechanic

Think of the CMP tool as an aircraft. The mechanical engineers of 30.3 built the airframe and the engines: the platen bearings, the carrier head gimbal, the drive motors. The chemical engineers of 30.2 supply the fuel and characterize the weather: slurry chemistry, pad material, the fluid film in the contact. The software engineers of 17.7 built the avionics: endpoint detectors, run-to-run controllers, SPC charts.

The process engineer is the pilot.

The pilot did not build any of it, but the pilot is responsible for the flight. The recipe is the flight plan: pressures, speeds, flows, times, filed in advance and followed unless something forces a change. Metrology is the instrument panel: you cannot see the film you are polishing any more than a pilot in cloud can see the ground, so you fly on instruments, and you learn to scan them in a fixed pattern rather than staring at one gauge. SPC charts are the flight recorder plus the instrument scan discipline: they tell you when a reading is a gust and when it is a fuel leak. A designed experiment is a flight test program: a deliberate, structured set of maneuvers to map how the aircraft actually responds, flown once so that every ordinary flight afterward can be routine. And qualification, which we reach at the end, is the preflight checklist: the fixed ritual that decides whether this aircraft flies today at all.

One more property of this aircraft matters, and it will return again and again: the instruments lag. Post-CMP thickness is often measured on a separate metrology tool, minutes to hours after the polish, and often on only a few wafers per lot. You are flying a plane whose altimeter tells you where you were 25 wafers ago. Every control decision in this chapter is shaped by that delay.

## The job on one page: own the transfer function

Strip the job to a block diagram and it is a transfer function: inputs go in, a physical process transforms them, outputs come out. The process engineer owns the arrow in the middle.

```text
  INPUTS                        PROCESS                    OUTPUTS
  ──────                        ───────                    ───────
  recipe:                                                  removal rate
    zone pressures      ┌─────────────────────┐            (A/min, per zone)
    platen/head speed   │                     │
    slurry flow         │   pad + slurry +    │            uniformity
    polish time         │   wafer contact     │            (WIWNU, WTWNU)
                        │                     │
  consumable state:     │   (Preston, plus    │            topography
    pad age/condition   │   everything hiding │            (step height,
    disk wear           │   inside Kp)        │            dishing, erosion)
    slurry health       │                     │
    ring wear           └─────────────────────┘            defects
                                                           (scratches,
  tool state:                                              residue, corrosion)
    temperature, warm-up,
    chamber-to-chamber match
```

Three things to notice, because they define the daily work:

- **Only the recipe column is directly settable.** Consumable state and tool state change on their own, continuously, whether or not you touch anything. A large fraction of process engineering is estimating those hidden states from the outputs.
- **The outputs trade against each other.** More pressure raises rate but worsens defects and dishing. A softer pad reduces scratches but planarizes worse. There is no single "best" setting, only a chosen operating point on a trade-off surface, and the spec limits define the box you must stay inside.
- **You never observe the outputs completely.** You measure a handful of sites on a handful of wafers. Everything else is inference. The pilot's instruments sample the world; they are not the world.

## Preston beyond the poster

Chapter [17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md) introduced **Preston's equation** as the first-order model of removal:

```text
   RR = Kp · P · V
```

Removal rate is proportional to pressure times relative velocity, and Kp absorbs everything else. For the tool software of 17.7 that was enough. For a process engineer, the interesting physics is exactly what the poster version hides, so let us open both boxes: what is inside Kp, and why P is not what you think it is.

### What hides inside Kp

Kp is not a constant of nature. It is a lumped coefficient that bundles at least:

- **Slurry chemistry**: oxidizer concentration, pH, abrasive type and loading, all covered in [30.2](30.2-chemical-engineering-for-cmp.md). Change the slurry batch and Kp moves.
- **Pad state**: a freshly conditioned pad presents sharp asperities that carry abrasive into the film; a glazed pad presents a smooth plateau that mostly slides. Same commanded pressure, very different Kp.
- **Temperature**: friction dissipates real power into the pad (see the power budget in [30.3](30.3-mechanical-engineering-for-fab-tools.md)), the pad warms up over consecutive wafers, and most CMP chemistries speed up with temperature. Kp on wafer 1 of the day is not Kp on wafer 10.
- **Film material and pattern**: oxide, copper, tungsten, and barrier metals each have their own Kp with a given slurry, which is the entire basis of polish selectivity, and the local pattern density modulates it further (more below).

So when your rate drifts and pressure and speed have not changed, the drift lives in Kp, and your job is to figure out which of Kp's tenants moved.

### The sublinear truth

Preston is linear in P·V; real CMP is not, especially for metal polishes. At low P·V, removal is limited by mechanics and the linear law holds well. Push P·V high enough and removal saturates, because the chemistry cannot regenerate the softened surface layer as fast as the mechanics wipes it away: the reaction step and the abrasion step run in series, and the slower one sets the pace. A **Langmuir-type saturation** model captures this in one line, `RR = a·P·V / (1 + b·P·V)`, which is linear with slope `a` when `b·P·V << 1` and flattens toward `a/b` when the chemical step is the bottleneck. The practical consequence: near the top of the tool's pressure range, adding pressure buys you less rate than Preston promises but the full extra dose of defects, so experienced engineers treat the last 20 percent of the pressure range with suspicion.

### Pressure is not uniform, even when you command it

The recipe says 4.0 psi. No point on the wafer necessarily sees 4.0 psi. Three reasons:

- **The edge discontinuity.** The wafer is a rigid disk pressed into a compliant pad. At the wafer's edge the pad rebounds, and contact mechanics concentrates stress there, classically producing a fast-polishing ring a few millimeters wide. The **retaining ring** that keeps the wafer from scooting out of the head (see [30.3](30.3-mechanical-engineering-for-fab-tools.md) for the force balance) is also a process knob: it presses the pad down just outside the wafer, pre-compressing it, and ring pressure and ring wear both reshape the edge profile.
- **Membrane zones blend.** Modern carrier heads press through a flexible membrane divided into concentric pressure zones, five to seven on current tools. But the membrane is one continuous sheet of rubber; it cannot hold a pressure step. Each zone's influence on the wafer is a smooth bump centered on the zone, and the pressure the wafer feels at any radius is a blend of neighboring zones.
- **The film stack fights back.** Incoming wafers arrive with their own bow and thickness profile, so even a perfectly uniform pressure would produce a nonuniform post-polish thickness.

The blending point deserves code, because it produces the least intuitive behavior a new engineer meets: zone knobs interact. Model each zone's influence as a Gaussian in radius, normalize so the influences sum to one everywhere, and compute the removal profile:

```python
"""Multi-zone carrier head: zone pressures shape the radial removal profile.
Run: python3 zone_profile.py
"""
import numpy as np

R_WAFER = 150.0                          # wafer radius, mm (300 mm wafer)
r = np.linspace(0.0, R_WAFER, 151)       # radial grid, 1 mm steps

# A 5-zone membrane: zone centers across the radius, in mm.
ZONE_CENTERS = np.array([0.0, 45.0, 85.0, 118.0, 143.0])
ZONE_WIDTH = 24.0                        # influence spread of each zone, mm

def pressure_profile(zone_psi):
    """Pressure the wafer actually sees at each radius.
    Each zone contributes a Gaussian influence function; the membrane
    blends them, so felt pressure is a weighted mix of zone setpoints."""
    g = np.exp(-0.5 * ((r[:, None] - ZONE_CENTERS[None, :]) / ZONE_WIDTH) ** 2)
    w = g / g.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)     # normalized blend at each radius
    return w @ zone_psi                      # psi felt at each radius

def removal_profile(zone_psi, V=1.2, Kp=520.0):
    """Preston per radius: RR(r) = Kp * P(r) * V, times a retaining-ring
    edge band where pad rebound raises contact pressure a few percent."""
    P = pressure_profile(zone_psi)
    ring_band = 1.0 + 0.05 * np.exp(-0.5 * ((R_WAFER - r) / 7.0) ** 2)
    return Kp * P * V * ring_band            # A/min at each radius

def wiwnu_sigma(profile):
    return 100.0 * profile.std() / profile.mean()

recipes = {
    "all zones 4.00 psi":       np.array([4.00, 4.00, 4.00, 4.00, 4.00]),
    "edge zone only, -0.22":    np.array([4.00, 4.00, 4.00, 4.00, 3.78]),
    "zones 4+5 tuned together": np.array([4.02, 4.00, 4.00, 4.14, 3.78]),
}
for name, recipe in recipes.items():
    rr = removal_profile(recipe)
    print(f"{name:>25}: mean {rr.mean():6.1f} A/min | "
          f"center {rr[0]:6.1f}  mid {rr[75]:6.1f}  edge {rr[-1]:6.1f} | "
          f"WIWNU(1sigma) {wiwnu_sigma(rr):.2f}%")
```

```text
       all zones 4.00 psi: mean 2503.7 A/min | center 2496.0  mid 2496.0  edge 2620.8 | WIWNU(1sigma) 1.00%
    edge zone only, -0.22: mean 2484.3 A/min | center 2496.0  mid 2494.5  edge 2521.8 | WIWNU(1sigma) 0.80%
 zones 4+5 tuned together: mean 2503.8 A/min | center 2506.6  mid 2505.5  edge 2548.8 | WIWNU(1sigma) 0.52%
```

Read the numbers. With uniform zone pressures, the ring edge band makes the outer 10 mm polish about 5 percent fast, and WIWNU sits at 1.00 percent. The obvious fix, trim only the edge zone, helps but disappoints: 0.80 percent, because the trim also drags down the region between zones 4 and 5 that the edge zone's influence tail reaches. The better fix trims zone 5 and raises zone 4 to backfill the tail, cutting WIWNU roughly in half. This is the daily texture of profile tuning: the knobs are not orthogonal, every zone move leaks into its neighbors through the membrane, and a good engineer tunes zones in pairs, thinking in influence functions rather than in zones. Keep this model; the Build it simulator reuses it.

## The uniformity zoo

"Uniform" is not one number. The trade uses a small zoo of metrics, and interviews for this job routinely ask you to define them precisely, so let us do exactly that.

- **WIWNU** (within-wafer nonuniformity) describes variation across a single wafer's measurement sites. Two flavors coexist and they are not interchangeable. The _range flavor_ is `(max - min) / (2 * mean) * 100%`, driven entirely by the two extreme sites. The _one-sigma flavor_ is `stddev / mean * 100%` (the coefficient of variation from [16.1](../16-Data/16.1-statistics-and-probability.md)), which uses all sites and is gentler on single outliers. For the same wafer the range flavor is typically 1.5 to 3 times larger. Reporting one when the customer expects the other is a classic and expensive miscommunication: always state the flavor and the site count.
- **WTWNU** (wafer-to-wafer nonuniformity) applies the same statistics to wafer means across a lot or a day: it measures the stability of the process, where WIWNU measures the flatness of the profile.
- **Step height reduction**: patterned wafers arrive with topography, steps of height `SH_pre` between high and low regions. After polish the step is `SH_post`, and the reduction is `1 - SH_post / SH_pre`.
- **Planarization efficiency (PE)** asks a sharper question: of the material you removed, did you take it from the peaks (good) or from everywhere (wasteful)? Define `PE = 1 - (removal in low areas / removal in high areas)`. PE = 1 means only peaks polished, ideal planarization. PE = 0 means blanket thinning: the step survives untouched while you burn film budget. Hard pads and long-range stiffness push PE up; soft conformable pads push it down.

### Dishing and erosion: uniformity's pattern-level cousins

Two topography defects live below the reach of any zone knob, at the scale of the chip layout itself. Both were introduced qualitatively in 17.7; here is the cross-section a process engineer draws on the whiteboard:

```text
  ideal damascene result          dishing                    erosion
  (flat top surface)              (wide line sags)           (dense array sinks)

  ────┬────┬────┬────         ────┐          ┌────       ─┐ ╷  ╷  ╷  ╷ ┌─
      │ Cu │    │ Cu              │ ~~~~~~~~ │            │_╵__╵__╵__╵_│
  oxide    oxide                  │____Cu____│              Cu Cu Cu Cu
                                  wide copper line          oxide between lines
                                  polishes below the        thinned; whole array
                                  oxide surface             sits low
```

**Dishing** is the sagging of a wide soft feature (a fat copper power rail) below the surrounding hard dielectric: the pad flexes into the wide opening and keeps polishing the metal after the field has cleared. **Erosion** is the thinning of the dielectric between densely packed lines: a dense array is mostly metal, polishes fast as a unit, and the thin oxide spacers get dragged down with it. Both are measured in angstroms by profilometry or high-resolution metrology on specific layout structures, both get worse with overpolish, and neither responds to zone pressure, because they are set by pattern geometry and consumable choice (pad hardness, slurry selectivity, per 30.2), not by the radial pressure profile.

This is the mechanism behind **pattern density effects**: the local removal rate depends on the local fraction of area that is actually up and touching the pad. A dense array presents lots of contact area, so pressure per unit contact is low and steps planarize slowly; an isolated feature in open field concentrates the entire local load on itself and planarizes fast. The same recipe therefore polishes a dense SRAM block and an open analog region differently on the same die, which is why layouts carry dummy fill (see 17.7) and why process engineers qualify recipes on patterned product-like test wafers, never only on blanket films.

### Metrology: the instrument panel

Post-CMP film thickness is measured optically (interferometry or ellipsometry) at a fixed pattern of sites: a common full map is **49 points** arranged as a center point plus concentric rings, and a quick in-line check might use 9 or 13. The radial arrangement is deliberate: CMP signatures are overwhelmingly radial (center-fast, edge-fast, W-shaped), so sites are placed to sample each radius. A single number like "mean thickness" throws away the profile; a good engineer always looks at thickness versus radius before touching a zone pressure.

Here are all the metrics as one small library, applied to a synthetic edge-fast 49-point map:

```python
"""Uniformity metrics every CMP process engineer computes daily.
Run: python3 uniformity_metrics.py
"""
import numpy as np
np.random.seed(3)

def wiwnu_range(vals):
    """Range flavor, percent: (max - min) / (2 * mean) * 100."""
    return 100.0 * (vals.max() - vals.min()) / (2.0 * vals.mean())

def wiwnu_sigma(vals):
    """One-sigma flavor, percent: stddev / mean * 100."""
    return 100.0 * vals.std(ddof=1) / vals.mean()

def planarization_efficiency(removed_up, removed_down):
    """PE = 1 - (removal in low areas / removal in high areas).
    PE = 1: only peaks polish (ideal). PE = 0: blanket thinning."""
    return 1.0 - removed_down / removed_up

def step_height_reduction(sh_pre, sh_post):
    """Fraction of the incoming step that the polish erased."""
    return 1.0 - sh_post / sh_pre

# --- a 49-point post-polish oxide thickness map (Angstroms) --------------
# Sites live on rings: 1 center, then 8/16/24 sites at r = 50/100/145 mm.
ring_r  = np.array([0.0] + [50.0]*8 + [100.0]*16 + [145.0]*24)
true_thk = 3000.0 - 240.0*(ring_r/145.0)**2          # edge polished ~240 A more
meas = true_thk + np.random.normal(0.0, 8.0, ring_r.size)  # 8 A gauge noise

print(f"49-pt mean thickness : {meas.mean():8.1f} A")
print(f"WIWNU (range flavor) : {wiwnu_range(meas):8.2f} %")
print(f"WIWNU (1-sigma)      : {wiwnu_sigma(meas):8.2f} %")

# --- step-height numbers from a patterned test wafer ---------------------
sh_pre, sh_post = 5000.0, 400.0        # A, before and after polish
removed_up, removed_down = 6200.0, 1600.0
print(f"step height reduction: {step_height_reduction(sh_pre, sh_post):8.2%}")
print(f"planarization eff.   : "
      f"{planarization_efficiency(removed_up, removed_down):8.2%}")

# --- wafer-to-wafer on a 25-wafer lot (WTWNU uses wafer means) -----------
lot_means = 3000.0 + np.random.normal(0.0, 12.0, 25)
print(f"WTWNU (1-sigma)      : {100.0*lot_means.std(ddof=1)/lot_means.mean():8.2f} %")
```

```text
49-pt mean thickness :   2838.2 A
WIWNU (range flavor) :     4.82 %
WIWNU (1-sigma)      :     3.08 %
step height reduction:   92.00%
planarization eff.   :   74.19%
WTWNU (1-sigma)      :     0.45 %
```

Note the ratio between the two WIWNU flavors (4.82 versus 3.08 percent) even on this smooth synthetic profile, and note that the "same wafer" would score very differently on a 9-point map that undersamples the edge roll-off. Metrics are only comparable when the flavor, the site count, and the edge exclusion (how close to the wafer edge you allow sites, commonly 2 to 3 mm) all match.

## Consumables are process state

A new engineer's model of the tool is: settings in, wafers out. The working engineer's model adds a third category, and it dominates troubleshooting: **consumable state**. The polisher is partly made of parts that are being consumed by the process itself, and their decay is the default explanation for drift. When someone says "nothing changed but the process shifted", the correct translation is "something I do not chart changed." The usual suspects:

- **The pad** wears and glazes. A fresh pad has open pores and sharp asperities; hundreds of wafers of polishing flatten the asperities and pack the pores with debris (_glazing_, introduced in 17.7), and the removal rate slides down. The **pad life curve** is the standard picture: a short break-in rise over the first tens of wafers as conditioning opens the fresh surface, a long slow decline through mid-life, and an accelerating fall near end of life. Fabs set a pad change interval (order of several hundred to a couple thousand wafers, process dependent) well before the cliff.
- **The conditioner disk** is the consumable that maintains a consumable. Its diamonds dull with use, so late-life conditioning cuts less texture per sweep, the pad glazes faster, and the rate decay steepens even though the pad itself is mid-life. Disk age is charted separately from pad age for exactly this reason.
- **The slurry** is a live colloidal suspension: it settles, agglomerates, ages, and varies batch to batch. [30.2](30.2-chemical-engineering-for-cmp.md) owns that story; the process engineer's summary is that slurry health (particle size distribution, chemistry titration, loop filtration state) is process state, and a scratch excursion sends you to the slurry loop first.
- **The retaining ring** wears down against the pad. Ring thickness sets how the pad is pre-compressed at the wafer edge, so the edge removal profile migrates over ring life: a recipe tuned on a new ring polishes the edge differently on a worn one. Edge-profile drift with no recipe change is a ring-life question before it is anything else.

The professional habit this teaches: **log consumable age alongside every measurement.** Rate, WIWNU, and defect counts mean little without pad wafer count, disk age, ring hours, and slurry batch attached. In the aircraft analogy, this is the maintenance log riding along with the flight log, and an investigator reads them together or not at all.

## Knowing when to stop: endpoint, time, and drift

[17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md) covered endpoint detection as a software problem: motor-current friction signatures for metal clears, optical interference for dielectrics, filtering, and overpolish tuning. That chapter owns the algorithms, and a paragraph of recap is all we need: when the film change is detectable in real time, the tool stops on the signal, and the process engineer's job reduces to tuning the detector's window, threshold, and overpolish percentage against scrap risk on both sides.

The more common situation for oxide CMP is that there is nothing to detect: you must remove a set amount and stop, so polish time is computed from an assumed rate, `t = target_removal / rate_estimate`, and the entire problem becomes tracking the rate. And the rate drifts, for reasons you can now name precisely:

- **Pad wear and glazing** push the rate down over tens to hundreds of wafers, the dominant slow drift.
- **Temperature** pushes it around on a scale of single wafers. Friction dissipates hundreds of watts into the pad (30.3 computed the power budget), the pad and slurry warm toward a steady state over the first several wafers, and warm chemistry polishes faster.
- **The first-wafer effect** is the temperature story's sharp edge: after an idle period the pad is cold, and wafer 1 polishes measurably slower (a few percent) than wafer 5. Fabs blunt this edge by running **warm-up wafers** (dummies polished purely to heat the pad) before product after any long idle, trading a few minutes of throughput for a predictable rate.

Rate tracking is exactly the run-to-run control loop you built in 17.7: estimate the rate with an EWMA of measured removals, invert the model to set the next time. We will return to how to run that loop responsibly in the SPC section, because a controller in the wrong hands is a noise amplifier.

## Defects: the daily Pareto

Uniformity failures waste angstroms; defects kill dies. Post-CMP inspection (patterned-wafer scanners plus review, per the metrology section of 17.7) classifies what it finds, and the process engineer's morning ritual is the **defect Pareto**: a bar chart of defect counts by category, sorted descending, because the top bar is where the day goes. The recurring cast:

- **Scratches** are the signature CMP defect: an oversized particle or agglomerate dragged across the surface gouges a line through many dies. The particle source is usually the slurry loop (agglomeration, settling, a dead filter, all 30.2 territory) or a shedding, worn conditioner disk. A sudden scratch excursion means "audit the slurry path" before touching the recipe.
- **Chatter marks** are periodic curved scratch trains, mechanical in origin: a vibrating or stick-slipping contact (worn head gimbal, wrong ring pressure, pad-glazing-induced friction oscillation) leaves stitches instead of a smooth track. The periodicity is the clue; spacing times relative velocity gives the vibration frequency, which points to a rotating component per 30.3.
- **Residue and particles**: slurry left on the wafer, pad debris, dried droplets. These implicate the **post-CMP clean** (brush condition, chemistry concentration, drying) more often than the polish itself. Since the clean is integrated into the tool (dry-in, dry-out per 17.7), the process engineer owns it too, and a particle map that rings the wafer or spirals implicates the clean-and-dry path, while a straight-line signature implicates the polish.
- **Corrosion** on copper: the aggressive oxidizing chemistry keeps attacking after the mechanics stops if the queue time to the clean stretches, or if the inhibitor chemistry is off. Pits and stains on wide copper are the fingerprint.
- **Dishing and erosion**, already defined, are counted here too: they are topography defects with electrical consequences (thinner wires, higher resistance) rather than particles.
- **Delamination**: modern low-k dielectrics are mechanically weak (porous, by design, for capacitance), and CMP shear can peel film stacks apart at their weakest interface. The fix is almost always lower downforce, which is why low-k drove the industry toward gentler polishes and better slurries rather than more pressure.

The habit to build: defects are diagnosed by their **spatial signature and their history**, not by their name. Scratches aligned with the polish track point to the slurry; a ring of particles points to the dryer; a step change on the Pareto the day after a pad change points to break-in. The Pareto tells you what; the wafer map and the consumable log tell you why.

## DOE: the process engineer's power tool

Suppose rate is low and WIWNU is high, and you have three knobs: pressure, speed, slurry flow. The instinct everyone arrives with is **one-factor-at-a-time** (OFAT): hold two knobs, sweep the third, pick the best value, move on. OFAT fails for a specific, provable reason: it assumes the knobs do not interact, and in CMP they do. The effect of slurry flow depends on speed (a fast pad flings slurry off and starves the contact at low flow). The effect of edge-zone trim depends on speed (the edge band it must cancel grows with speed, as we will engineer into the mini-project). OFAT explores an L-shaped path through the corner of the design space and never visits the combinations where interactions live.

The fix, from the statistics you built in [16.1](../16-Data/16.1-statistics-and-probability.md), is the **factorial designed experiment**: vary all factors together, in a pattern chosen so their effects can be separated afterward.

### A 2^2 example, by hand

Two factors, each at a low (-) and high (+) level: pressure P at 3 or 5 psi, speed S at 60 or 100 rpm. Run all four corners and measure removal rate in A/min:

```text
   run    P    S    rate
    1     -    -    1800
    2     +    -    2600
    3     -    +    2200
    4     +    +    3400
```

The **main effect** of a factor is the average response at its high level minus the average at its low level:

```text
  effect(P) = (2600 + 3400)/2 - (1800 + 2200)/2 = 3000 - 2000 = 1000 A/min
  effect(S) = (2200 + 3400)/2 - (1800 + 2600)/2 = 2800 - 2200 =  600 A/min
```

The **interaction effect** asks: does P's effect depend on S's level? Compute P's effect at each S level and take half the difference:

```text
  P effect at S high : 3400 - 2200 = 1200
  P effect at S low  : 2600 - 1800 =  800
  interaction PS     = (1200 - 800)/2 = 200 A/min
```

Equivalently, and this is the form that generalizes: multiply the sign columns, and the interaction is the average of runs where the product P·S is + minus the average where it is -: `(1800 + 3400)/2 - (2600 + 2200)/2 = 2600 - 2400 = 200`. Same answer.

These effects assemble into a prediction model with coded units (x = -1 at low, +1 at high), where each coefficient is half its effect because moving -1 to +1 is two units:

```text
  rate = 2500 + 500·xP + 300·xS + 100·xP·xS
```

Check it against run 4: `2500 + 500 + 300 + 100 = 3400`. It reproduces all four corners exactly (with four runs and four coefficients it must; replicates are what let you also estimate noise). Now see what OFAT would have done from corner 1: sweep P alone, gain 800; then sweep S alone at low P, gain 400; conclude the best case is `1800 + 800 + 400 = 3000`. The true best corner is 3400. The missing 400 is the interaction, twice over, and OFAT is structurally unable to see it.

### A 2^3 factorial against a hidden process

Scale to three factors and let the computer keep the books. The design is all 2^3 = 8 corners, replicated twice for a noise estimate, in randomized run order (randomization protects you from time drift masquerading as a factor effect, and on a CMP tool there is always time drift). The "hidden process" plays the role of the real tool: it has an interaction and noise, and the analysis has to recover both.

```python
"""2^3 factorial DOE against a hidden process with an interaction.
Factors (coded -1/+1): x1 = pressure (3 -> 5 psi), x2 = platen speed
(0.9 -> 1.5 m/s), x3 = slurry flow (150 -> 250 ml/min).
Run: python3 doe_2cubed.py
"""
import numpy as np
np.random.seed(42)

# --- the hidden truth (the real tool; the engineer never sees this) ------
def hidden_process(x1, x2, x3):
    return (2500.0 + 550.0*x1 + 320.0*x2 + 140.0*x3
            + 90.0*x2*x3                     # speed*flow interaction
            + np.random.normal(0.0, 40.0))   # run noise, 40 A/min 1-sigma

# --- the design: all 8 corners, 2 replicates, randomized run order -------
corners = [(a, b, c) for a in (-1, 1) for b in (-1, 1) for c in (-1, 1)]
runs = corners * 2
y = np.zeros(len(runs))
for k in np.random.permutation(len(runs)):   # always randomize run order
    y[k] = hidden_process(*runs[k])

# --- effects: contrast column for every term ------------------------------
X = np.array(runs, dtype=float)
cols = {"pressure": X[:, 0], "speed": X[:, 1], "flow": X[:, 2],
        "P*S": X[:, 0]*X[:, 1], "P*F": X[:, 0]*X[:, 2],
        "S*F": X[:, 1]*X[:, 2], "P*S*F": X[:, 0]*X[:, 1]*X[:, 2]}
effects = {n: y[c > 0].mean() - y[c < 0].mean() for n, c in cols.items()}

truth = {"pressure": 1100, "speed": 640, "flow": 280, "S*F": 180}
print(f"grand mean rate: {y.mean():7.1f} A/min   ({len(runs)} runs)")
print(f"{'term':>9} {'effect':>8}   (truth)")
for name, e in effects.items():
    print(f"{name:>9} {e:8.1f}   ({truth.get(name, 0)})")

# replicate noise -> standard error of an effect: SE = 2*s/sqrt(N)
pairs = np.array([[y[i], y[i+8]] for i in range(8)])
s = np.sqrt(np.mean(pairs.var(axis=1, ddof=1)))
se = 2.0*s/np.sqrt(len(runs))
print(f"\nreplicate sigma ~ {s:.1f} A/min -> effect standard error ~ {se:.1f}")
print("rule of thumb: an effect under 2-3 standard errors is noise")

# --- pick the winner and confirm ------------------------------------------
# Model with the real terms only; predict the (+,+,+) corner.
keep = ("pressure", "speed", "flow", "S*F")
pred = y.mean() + 0.5*sum(effects[n] for n in keep)   # all x = +1
conf = np.array([hidden_process(1, 1, 1) for _ in range(3)])
print(f"\npredicted rate at (+,+,+): {pred:7.1f} A/min")
print(f"3 confirmation runs      : {conf.mean():7.1f} A/min "
      f"(each: {conf.round(0)})")
```

Sixteen wafers bought the whole map: three main effects, the one real interaction cleanly separated from the four null terms (which all land within a couple of standard errors of zero), a noise estimate, and a validated prediction. Notice the last step: **confirmation runs**. A DOE ends with a bet placed at the predicted best setting and checked against fresh wafers; skipping confirmation is how analysis errors reach production.

Two extensions you should know by name. **Fractional factorials** handle more factors on a budget: a 2^(5-1) design runs half of the 32 corners of a five-factor experiment, at the cost of *aliasing* (some high-order interactions become indistinguishable from main effects; you choose the fraction so that only implausible ones are sacrificed).

**Center points**, a few runs at the middle of all ranges, test for curvature: if the center response is far from the corner average, the linear-plus-interactions model is bending and you graduate to **response surface methods**, which fit a quadratic and walk you to the optimum along its gradient. The workflow, and the reason DOE is the trade's power tool, is always the same shape: screen many factors with a cheap fraction, model the survivors with a full factorial, then map the region around the best point with a response surface and place the operating point with margin.

## SPC and capability: control is not capability

DOE finds the operating point; **SPC** guards it. The mechanics of control charts were built in 17.7 (Shewhart's ±3σ logic, limits from the process's own stable history, OCAPs); here is how a process engineer runs them day to day, plus the one distinction that separates engineers from chart-watchers.

The workhorse is the **Xbar-R chart** pair on the daily rate monitor: measure a subgroup (say the rate on 5 monitor sites or wafers), plot the subgroup mean (Xbar) to watch for shifts, and the subgroup range (R) to watch for spread changes. For subgroup size n = 5 the classical constants are `UCL_X = Xbar + 0.577·Rbar`, `LCL_X = Xbar - 0.577·Rbar`, and `UCL_R = 2.114·Rbar`. On top of the basic beyond-3σ rule sit the **Western Electric rules**, sensitized patterns like 2 of 3 points beyond 2σ, 4 of 5 beyond 1σ on the same side, and 8 in a row on one side of the center line; each is an event rare enough under stability (16.1 probability again) that its occurrence is evidence of a real change, and together they catch slow drifts long before a single point escapes the limits.

Then, capability. **Control** means the process is stable: only random variation, charts quiet. **Capability** means the stable process fits inside the spec. They are independent, and the capability indices quantify the second:

```text
  Cp  = (USL - LSL) / (6·sigma)          potential: does the spread fit?
  Cpk = min(mean - LSL, USL - mean) / (3·sigma)    reality: is it centered?
```

Worked numbers, the kind an interviewer will ask you to do on a whiteboard. Post-polish thickness spec is 2900 to 3100 A; the process runs at mean 2980 A with sigma 25 A. Then `Cp = 200/150 = 1.333`: the spread would fit comfortably if centered. But `Cpk = min(80, 120)/75 = 80/75 = 1.067`: the 20 A off-center gives away a third of the margin on the low side. The distance to the nearer limit is `3·Cpk = 3.2` sigmas, roughly 700 ppm outside on that side under a normal model. A fab asking for Cpk ≥ 1.33 (4 sigma to the nearer limit, order of 30 ppm) is telling you to either re-center or shrink sigma. Code it once and keep it:

```python
"""Xbar-R chart on removal rate plus Cp/Cpk on post thickness.
```

### Using the run-to-run controller responsibly

[17.7](../17-Semiconductors/17.7-fab-equipment-and-tool-software.md) built the R2R controller: an EWMA rate estimate feeding a model inversion for polish time. The software is the autopilot; the process engineer decides how it is engaged, and three settings carry most of that responsibility:

- **EWMA weight (lambda).** Small lambda (0.1 to 0.3) filters hard: smooth estimates, slow response to real shifts. Large lambda chases every measurement. The steady-state noise on the estimate scales as `sigma_meas · sqrt(lambda/(2-lambda))`, so lambda 0.7 passes roughly three times more metrology noise into your polish times than lambda 0.1. With gauge noise a sizeable fraction of true run-to-run variation, heavy filtering is usually right, and the exercise section makes you compute the trade.
- **Deadband.** Do not act on deviations smaller than the metrology can resolve. If gauge sigma is 20 A, a 15 A "error" is a coin flip, and correcting it injects the correction as fresh variation. A deadband (no adjustment inside, say, ±1 gauge sigma) makes the controller ignore what it cannot see. Chasing noise makes output variance worse than doing nothing; Deming's funnel experiment is the classic demonstration, and CMP fabs relive it whenever someone cranks lambda up after one bad lot.
- **Metrology delay.** The measurement that reaches the controller describes wafers polished minutes to hours ago; meanwhile the pad wore further. This is the lagging altimeter from the start of the chapter: with a 25-wafer delay, an aggressive controller responds to where the process was, overshoots where it is, then corrects the overshoot a lap later, a self-inflicted oscillation. The mitigation is structural, not clever tuning: filter heavily, correct gently, and let SPC (which is judging stability, not steering) catch anything the slow controller misses.

## Qualification: the preflight checklist

One more idea completes the process engineer's vocabulary and bridges to [30.5](30.5-field-service-and-tool-install.md): **qualification**. A tool is "qualified" when it has demonstrated, with data, that it produces results statistically indistinguishable from the reference process, and it holds that status only until something changes (a pad change, a repair, an idle weekend, a new slurry lot), after which some level of requalification is owed. The standard evidence package:

- **Rate and uniformity qual**: polish monitor wafers, verify mean rate and WIWNU inside their control limits.
- **Particle qual**: polish and clean bare or blanket monitors, count adders (particles present after that were not present before) on an inspection tool, verify below the adder limit.
- **Marathon runs**: for a new tool, chamber, or consumable set, a long continuous run (hundreds of wafers) to prove stability over consumable life, not just a good first hour. Short quals catch offsets; only marathons catch drift.
- **Golden-wafer correlation**: metrology itself must be qualified, so a **golden wafer** (a stable, well-characterized reference) is measured periodically on each metrology tool; if the metrology drifts, every process chart it feeds lies. Instruments get calibrated before the aircraft flies.

Field service engineers execute much of this after installs and repairs, which is exactly where 30.5 picks up the thread.

## Build it: a 100-wafer marathon on a virtual tool

Time to assemble everything into one simulator: the multi-zone radial model from this chapter, pad-wear drift, the first-wafer temperature transient, run noise, and honest 9-point metrology with gauge noise. Then we run a 100-wafer marathon and judge it the way a fab would: trend table, SPC limits from the stable early history, Western Electric flags.

```python
"""Wafer-scale CMP process simulator: 100-wafer marathon with drift.
Radial grid + multi-zone pressure + pad wear + warm-up + 9-pt metrology.
Run: python3 cmp_marathon.py
"""
import numpy as np
np.random.seed(2024)

R = np.linspace(0.0, 150.0, 151)                # radius grid, mm
ZC = np.array([0.0, 45.0, 85.0, 118.0, 143.0])  # zone centers, mm
ZW = 24.0                                       # zone influence width, mm
KP0 = 520.0                     # fresh-pad Preston coeff, A/min/(psi*m/s)
MET_R = np.array([0., 30., 55., 75., 95., 110., 125., 138., 148.])  # 9 sites

def pressure(zone_psi):
    g = np.exp(-0.5*((R[:, None]-ZC[None, :])/ZW)**2)
    return (g/g.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)) @ zone_psi

class CMPTool:
    """Holds hidden state the process engineer cannot see directly."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.pad_wafers = 0        # wafers since pad change
        self.idle = True           # tool has been idle: pad starts cold

    def polish(self, zone_psi, v, t_polish):
        # pad wear: Kp decays ~0.12%/wafer as the pad glazes despite conditioning
        kp = KP0 * (1.0 - 0.0012*self.pad_wafers)
        # first-wafer effect: cold pad polishes ~5% slow, recovers over ~4 wafers
        if self.idle:
            self.warm = 0
            self.idle = False
        warm_mult = 1.0 - 0.05*np.exp(-self.warm/1.8)
        self.warm += 1
        self.pad_wafers += 1
        # edge band from retaining ring, plus run noise and local texture noise
        ring = 1.0 + 0.05*np.exp(-0.5*((150.0-R)/7.0)**2)
        run_noise = np.random.normal(1.0, 0.008)
        prof_noise = np.random.normal(0.0, 8.0, R.size)   # A/min local texture
        rr = kp*warm_mult*run_noise * pressure(zone_psi) * v * ring + prof_noise
        removed = rr * t_polish                          # Angstroms
        return removed

def measure(removed, t_polish):
    """9-point metrology with 8 A gauge noise -> rate and WIWNU estimates."""
    idx = np.searchsorted(R, MET_R)
    thk = removed[idx] + np.random.normal(0.0, 8.0, MET_R.size)
    rates = thk / t_polish
    return rates.mean(), 100.0*rates.std(ddof=1)/rates.mean()

# --- the marathon ---------------------------------------------------------
tool = CMPTool()
recipe = np.array([4.02, 4.00, 4.00, 4.14, 3.78])   # tuned zones from earlier
rates, wiwnus = [], []
for w in range(100):
    removed = tool.polish(recipe, v=1.2, t_polish=1.0)
    r_mean, wiwnu = measure(removed, 1.0)
    rates.append(r_mean); wiwnus.append(wiwnu)
rates = np.array(rates); wiwnus = np.array(wiwnus)

print("wafer   rate A/min   WIWNU%     (every 10th wafer)")
for w in range(0, 100, 10):
    print(f"{w+1:5d}   {rates[w]:9.1f}   {wiwnus[w]:6.2f}")

# --- SPC-style summary: limits from wafers 6-30, judge the rest ----------
base = rates[5:30]
cl, s = base.mean(), base.std(ddof=1)
ucl, lcl = cl + 3*s, cl - 3*s
print(f"\nSPC on rate: CL {cl:.0f}, UCL {ucl:.0f}, LCL {lcl:.0f} "
      f"(from wafers 6-30)")
beyond = np.where((rates < lcl) | (rates > ucl))[0]
print(f"rule 1 (beyond 3 sigma): first hit at wafer "
      f"{beyond[0]+1 if beyond.size else 'none'}")
# Western Electric rule 4: 8 in a row on one side of the center line
run_len, first8 = 0, None
for w in range(30, 100):
    run_len = run_len + 1 if rates[w] < cl else 0
    if run_len >= 8 and first8 is None:
        first8 = w + 1
print(f"rule 4 (8 in a row below CL): first hit at wafer {first8}")
print(f"first-wafer effect: wafer 1 rate {rates[0]:.0f} vs wafer 6 {rates[5]:.0f}")
print(f"pad-wear drift: mean rate wafers 1-10 {rates[:10].mean():.0f} "
      f"vs wafers 91-100 {rates[90:].mean():.0f}")
```

Read it like the engineer who owns this chamber:

- **Wafer 1 is 74 A/min slow**: the cold-pad first-wafer effect, exactly why warm-up wafers exist and why the SPC baseline starts at wafer 6, not wafer 1. Baselining on a transient poisons the limits.
- **The rate loses roughly 230 A/min over 100 wafers**: the pad-wear drift, about 0.1 percent per wafer, invisible wafer to wafer and unmistakable over the marathon. A time-based polish with a fixed time would be removing 9 percent too little by the end; this is the drift the run-to-run controller from 17.7 exists to absorb.
- **SPC catches the drift around wafer 35**, first by a 3σ breach, then confirmed by the 8-in-a-row rule shortly after. In a real fab this triggers the OCAP: hold, check consumable ages, requalify or compensate.

The deeper lesson is about observability: the simulator's hidden state (pad_wafers, warm) is exactly the kind of state a real engineer cannot read directly and must infer from the outputs. You now have a tool where you know the truth and can test whether your charts and controllers actually find it. That is worth more than it sounds; almost nothing in a real fab comes with ground truth attached.

## Mini-project: optimize the virtual tool with a designed experiment

Now use the simulator as a **virtual fab** and do the actual job: a customer needs mean rate at or above 2400 A/min with the best WIWNU you can deliver. You have three knobs: an edge-zone pressure trim (0 or -0.3 psi), platen speed (1.0 or 1.4 m/s), and slurry flow (150 or 250 ml/min). Run a 2^3 factorial with replicates, build the effects table for both responses, pick the winner under the constraint, and confirm. The hidden physics contains a trap that OFAT would fall straight into.

```python
"""Mini-project: DOE on the virtual CMP tool.
Factors: x1 = edge-zone trim (0.0 -> -0.3 psi), x2 = platen speed
(1.0 -> 1.4 m/s), x3 = slurry flow (150 -> 250 ml/min).
Goal: minimize WIWNU subject to mean rate >= 2400 A/min.
Run: python3 doe_virtual_tool.py
"""
import numpy as np
np.random.seed(7)

R = np.linspace(0.0, 150.0, 151)
ZC = np.array([0.0, 45.0, 85.0, 118.0, 143.0])
ZW = 24.0
MET_R = np.array([0., 30., 55., 75., 95., 110., 125., 138., 148.])
IDX = np.searchsorted(R, MET_R)

def polish_and_measure(edge_trim, v, flow):
    """The virtual tool. Hidden physics the DOE has to discover:
    - flow feeds Kp with saturation (Langmuir-flavored),
    - the ring edge band grows with speed,
    - so edge trim and speed interact on WIWNU."""
    zones = np.array([4.0, 4.0, 4.0, 4.0, 4.0 + edge_trim])
    g = np.exp(-0.5*((R[:, None]-ZC[None, :])/ZW)**2)
    P = (g/g.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)) @ zones
    kp = 650.0 * flow/(flow + 60.0)               # saturating flow response
    ring_amp = 0.02 + 0.06*(v - 1.0)/0.4          # edge band grows with speed
    ring = 1.0 + ring_amp*np.exp(-0.5*((150.0-R)/7.0)**2)
    rr = kp*P*v*ring * np.random.normal(1.0, 0.008)
    meas = rr[IDX] + np.random.normal(0.0, 8.0, IDX.size)
    return meas.mean(), 100.0*meas.std(ddof=1)/meas.mean()

def run_doe():
    lo = {"trim": 0.0, "v": 1.0, "flow": 150.0}
    hi = {"trim": -0.3, "v": 1.4, "flow": 250.0}   # "+" = trimmed, fast, wet
    corners = [(a, b, c) for a in (-1, 1) for b in (-1, 1) for c in (-1, 1)]
    runs = corners*2
    order = np.random.permutation(len(runs))
    rate = np.zeros(len(runs)); wiw = np.zeros(len(runs))
    for k in order:
        x1, x2, x3 = runs[k]
        args = (hi["trim"] if x1 > 0 else lo["trim"],
                hi["v"] if x2 > 0 else lo["v"],
                hi["flow"] if x3 > 0 else lo["flow"])
        rate[k], wiw[k] = polish_and_measure(*args)
    X = np.array(runs, dtype=float)
    terms = {"trim": X[:, 0], "speed": X[:, 1], "flow": X[:, 2],
             "trim*speed": X[:, 0]*X[:, 1], "trim*flow": X[:, 0]*X[:, 2],
             "speed*flow": X[:, 1]*X[:, 2]}
    print(f"{'term':>11} | {'rate effect':>11} | {'WIWNU effect':>12}")
    for name, c in terms.items():
        er = rate[c > 0].mean() - rate[c < 0].mean()
        ew = wiw[c > 0].mean() - wiw[c < 0].mean()
        print(f"{name:>11} | {er:11.1f} | {ew:12.2f}")
    # score each corner: WIWNU, with the rate floor as a constraint
    print(f"\n{'corner (t,s,f)':>15} | {'rate':>7} | {'WIWNU%':>6} | meets floor?")
    best = None
    for i, c in enumerate(corners):
        rmean = 0.5*(rate[i] + rate[i+8]); wmean = 0.5*(wiw[i] + wiw[i+8])
        ok = rmean >= 2400.0
        tag = "yes" if ok else "no"
        print(f"{str(c):>15} | {rmean:7.0f} | {wmean:6.2f} | {tag}")
        if ok and (best is None or wmean < best[1]):
            best = (c, wmean, rmean)
    c = best[0]
    print(f"\nwinner: trim={'on' if c[0]>0 else 'off'}, "
          f"speed={'1.4' if c[1]>0 else '1.0'} m/s, "
          f"flow={'250' if c[2]>0 else '150'} ml/min")
    args = (hi["trim"] if c[0] > 0 else lo["trim"],
            hi["v"] if c[1] > 0 else lo["v"],
            hi["flow"] if c[2] > 0 else lo["flow"])
    conf = np.array([polish_and_measure(*args) for _ in range(5)])
    print(f"confirmation (5 runs): rate {conf[:,0].mean():.0f} A/min, "
          f"WIWNU {conf[:,1].mean():.2f}% +/- {conf[:,1].std(ddof=1):.2f}")

run_doe()
```


Now the trap, and why this experiment is the portfolio piece. Look at the trim row of the effects table: main effect on WIWNU is -0.02, essentially zero. An OFAT experimenter starting at low speed would test the trim, watch WIWNU get *worse* (0.79 to 1.55 percent at the (-1,-1,-1) versus (1,-1,-1) corners: at low speed the edge band is small and the trim over-corrects, digging an edge droop), and conclude the trim is harmful. The factorial sees the truth in the **trim*speed interaction, -0.90**: at high speed, where the ring band is large, the same trim cuts WIWNU from 2.55 to 1.57 percent. The trim is neither good nor bad; it is a compensator whose correct magnitude depends on speed. Meanwhile the rate constraint eliminates all low-speed corners (the rate floor forces 1.4 m/s), so the trim is exactly what makes the required speed livable, and the speed*flow interaction on rate (67 A/min) is the multiplicative Preston physics showing itself, not an added fudge term.

Also notice what the winner does not achieve: 1.63 percent WIWNU is better than 2.55 but far from the 0.68 available at low speed. The DOE has told you the box you were given is too small, and the professional next step is a follow-up experiment outside it: a deeper trim, or trimming zones 4 and 5 together as in the first code block. Real optimization is iterative, and a DOE that ends by recommending the next DOE has done its job.

For the GitHub version, wrap it properly: the simulator as a module, the DOE runner taking factor definitions from a config, an effects table with standard errors, and a short README framed exactly as: "I built a virtual CMP tool with realistic drift and metrology noise, then optimized it with a replicated 2^3 factorial designed experiment, including a constraint, an interaction the design had to discover, and confirmation runs." An interviewer at any equipment vendor will recognize every word of that sentence as their daily work.

## Common mistakes

1. **Chasing noise.** One low reading, one knob tweak, then a "correction" of the correction. Any adjustment smaller than the metrology noise adds variance instead of removing it. Fix: know your gauge sigma, use a deadband, and demand an SPC rule violation (not a bad-looking point) before acting.
2. **Changing two things at once.** The pad was changed and the recipe was tweaked on the same shift; the rate moved; now nobody knows why, and the next move is a guess. Fix: one change per evaluation window, or a designed experiment where multiple changes are deliberately structured for separation. "Together by design or one at a time, never two by accident."
3. **Ignoring consumable age.** Comparing today's WIWNU (pad wafer 700) against the qual report (pad wafer 50) and concluding the tool broke. Fix: every comparison at matched consumable state, every data point logged with pad, disk, and ring age attached.
4. **Trusting a single metrology site.** Center-point-only monitoring while an edge excursion eats the outer 10 mm of every wafer, where a large share of the dies live. Fix: radial maps for anything involving profile, and never tune a zone pressure from a measurement that cannot see that zone.
5. **Confusing control with capability.** "The charts are quiet, we are fine" while Cpk is 0.8 and the process quietly ships hundreds of ppm out of spec; or its mirror image, panicking over an out-of-control point that is still deep inside spec. Fix: keep both numbers in view; control limits describe the process, spec limits describe the product, and they are allowed to disagree in both directions.
6. **Comparing WIWNU numbers with mismatched definitions.** Range flavor versus sigma flavor, 49 points versus 9, 3 mm edge exclusion versus 5. The same wafer can score 1 percent or 5 percent depending on the bookkeeping. Fix: state flavor, site count, and edge exclusion with every number, and convert before comparing.
7. **Baselining on a transient.** Setting SPC limits from data that includes the first wafers after an idle, a pad change, or a PM, which inflates sigma and blinds the chart. Fix: baseline on the stable region only, and treat post-change wafers as their own population until they rejoin it.

## On the job / interview angle

**How the FSA-to-process path actually runs.** Most vendor process engineers were not hired as process engineers. The common on-ramp, and likely yours, is field service or applications: you install tools ([30.5](30.5-field-service-and-tool-install.md)), run the qual packages, and collect the metrology that someone else interprets. The transition happens the day you start interpreting it yourself: you notice the edge profile migrated over ring life before the process engineer does, you attach consumable ages to your qual data unprompted, you propose the DOE instead of the knob-twiddle. Vendor **process applications engineers** sit exactly at that seam: they own polish results on customer tools, run demos and buy-off quals, and translate between the customer's process problems and the vendor's hardware, which makes the role a proven bridge from field work into process or product engineering at companies like Ebara and Applied Materials.

**What interviews probe.** CMP process interviews are refreshingly concrete, and this chapter is most of the syllabus:

- Write Preston's equation and then discuss what is inside Kp and where the linearity fails. The follow-up "your rate dropped 5 percent overnight, walk me through your investigation" is a consumable-state question (pad age, disk age, slurry batch, idle time, first-wafer effect) before it is anything else, and interviewers listen for whether you ask about what changed in the logs before proposing recipe changes.
- Define WIWNU, dishing, and erosion precisely, including which knobs do and do not affect each. Zone pressure fixes radial profile; it does not fix dishing. Knowing that boundary is a strong signal.
- Compute a Cpk on the whiteboard (the 2980/25/spec-200 example above is exactly the right rehearsal) and explain control versus capability in one breath.
- Design an experiment: "you have three factors and twenty wafers, go." The expected answer is a replicated 2^3 with randomized order and confirmation runs, plus the sentence "OFAT would miss the interactions." If you have the mini-project on GitHub, this question becomes a demo instead of a quiz.
- The judgment question: "metrology says trim 0.2 psi, the last three lots were fine, what do you do?" They are testing whether you chase noise, and the answer is the deadband and the SPC discipline, delivered with the reason, not just the rule.

## Exercises

1. **Kp bookkeeping.** A polish removes at 2400 A/min at P = 4.0 psi and V = 1.2 m/s. Compute Kp in A/min per (psi·m/s), then predict the rate at P = 3.0 psi, V = 1.5 m/s. State one physical reason the prediction could be off by more than the metrology noise.
2. **Two flavors of WIWNU.** Five thickness sites: 2980, 3005, 3010, 2960, 2995 A. Compute the mean, the range-flavor WIWNU, and the one-sigma WIWNU (sample standard deviation, ddof = 1). Which flavor moved more because of the 2960 site?
3. **Effects by hand.** A 2^2 experiment gives: (-,-) 1500, (+,-) 1900, (-,+) 1700, (+,+) 2500 A/min. Compute both main effects and the interaction, write the coded prediction model, and verify it reproduces the (+,+) corner.
4. **Capability after a drift.** Spec 2900 to 3100 A, sigma 25 A. Compute Cp and Cpk at mean 3000 A, then at mean 2950 A after an uncorrected drift. For the drifted case, estimate the out-of-spec fraction on the near side using the normal model (z = 3·Cpk).
5. **A dying conditioner disk.** In the marathon simulator, a worn disk shows up as faster Kp decay: change the pad-wear coefficient from 0.0012 to 0.0025 per wafer. Without running the code, predict roughly which wafer first breaches the LCL, given the baseline (wafers 6 to 30) has CL near 2450 and sigma near 23 A/min. Then check by running it.
6. **Choosing lambda.** An EWMA rate estimator sees gauge noise of sigma = 20 A/min. For lambda = 0.1 and lambda = 0.7, compute (a) the steady-state sigma of the estimate, `sigma·sqrt(lambda/(2-lambda))`, and (b) the number of runs to absorb 90 percent of a sudden step change, the smallest n with `(1-lambda)^n <= 0.1`. State which lambda you would choose for a pad-wear-dominated process with metrology delay, and why.

### Solutions

**1.** Kp = RR / (P·V) = 2400 / (4.0 × 1.2) = 2400 / 4.8 = 500 A/min per (psi·m/s). Prediction: RR = 500 × 3.0 × 1.5 = 500 × 4.5 = 2250 A/min. One physical reason it could miss: Kp is not constant across conditions; for example, the lower pressure changes pad contact and the higher speed changes pad temperature and slurry transport, all of which live inside Kp. (Also acceptable: sublinear saturation means the linear extrapolation overestimates high-P·V rates.)

**2.** Mean = (2980 + 3005 + 3010 + 2960 + 2995) / 5 = 14950 / 5 = 2990 A. Range = 3010 - 2960 = 50 A, so range flavor = 50 / (2 × 2990) × 100 = 0.84 percent. Deviations: -10, +15, +20, -30, +5; squares sum to 100 + 225 + 400 + 900 + 25 = 1650; sample variance = 1650 / 4 = 412.5; s = 20.31 A; one-sigma flavor = 20.31 / 2990 × 100 = 0.68 percent. The 2960 site is one of the two range-defining extremes, so it moves the range flavor much more; in the sigma flavor its 900 contribution is diluted by the other four sites.

**3.** Effect(A) = (1900 + 2500)/2 - (1500 + 1700)/2 = 2200 - 1600 = 600. Effect(B) = (1700 + 2500)/2 - (1500 + 1900)/2 = 2100 - 1700 = 400. Interaction: product-column form, (1500 + 2500)/2 - (1900 + 1700)/2 = 2000 - 1800 = 200. Grand mean = (1500 + 1900 + 1700 + 2500)/4 = 1900, so the model is `y = 1900 + 300·xA + 200·xB + 100·xA·xB`. Check (+,+): 1900 + 300 + 200 + 100 = 2500, matching.

**4.** Centered: Cp = 200 / (6 × 25) = 1.333; Cpk = min(100, 100) / 75 = 1.333 (equal because centered). Drifted to 2950: Cp is unchanged at 1.333 (spread did not change), Cpk = min(2950 - 2900, 3100 - 2950) / 75 = 50 / 75 = 0.667. Near-side z = 3 × 0.667 = 2.0; the normal tail beyond z = 2 is about 2.3 percent, so roughly 23,000 ppm out of spec on the low side, versus order of 30 ppm per side when centered. The same machine, the same sigma, several hundred times more scrap: centering is capability.

**5.** The drift rate is 0.0025 × 2500 ≈ 6.3 A/min per wafer, versus 3 A/min at the original coefficient. The LCL sits 3σ ≈ 69 A/min below the CL, and the CL reflects the baseline midpoint near wafer 18, so the mean crosses the LCL roughly 69 / 6.3 ≈ 11 wafers later, around wafer 29 to 31; with noise, the first individual breach lands within a few wafers of that. Verification snippet (drop-in replacement for the wear line, then rerun):

```python
import numpy as np
np.random.seed(2024)
# analytic estimate of the first-LCL-breach wafer for two wear coefficients
for coef in (0.0012, 0.0025):
    drift_per_wafer = coef * 2500.0     # A/min lost per wafer
    sigma = 23.0                        # baseline noise, A/min
    wafers_past_midbase = 3.0 * sigma / drift_per_wafer
    print(f"coef {coef}: mean crosses LCL ~{18 + wafers_past_midbase:.0f} "
          f"wafers (baseline midpoint 18 + {wafers_past_midbase:.0f})")
```

```text
coef 0.0012: mean crosses LCL ~41 wafers (baseline midpoint 18 + 23)
coef 0.0025: mean crosses LCL ~29 wafers (baseline midpoint 18 + 11)
```

The original run flagged at wafer 35 (noise brought the breach earlier than the mean crossing at 41, as it will about half the time); the doubled wear roughly halves the post-baseline distance to detection. The teaching point: SPC detection latency scales inversely with drift rate, so a dying disk is caught sooner but has also done proportionally more mischief per wafer by then.

**6.** (a) Steady-state estimate noise: lambda = 0.1 gives 20 × sqrt(0.1/1.9) = 20 × 0.229 = 4.6 A/min; lambda = 0.7 gives 20 × sqrt(0.7/1.3) = 20 × 0.734 = 14.7 A/min. (b) Step absorption: (0.9)^n ≤ 0.1 gives n = ln(0.1)/ln(0.9) = 21.9, so 22 runs; (0.3)^n ≤ 0.1 gives n = 1.91, so 2 runs. Choice: pad wear is a slow, smooth drift (a few A/min per wafer), so tracking it does not require fast response, while the metrology delay means aggressive reactions arrive late and destabilize the loop. Pick lambda near 0.1 to 0.3: the 4.6 A/min estimate noise is far below the drift you care about across a pad life, and the 22-run response time is fast relative to a several-hundred-wafer pad life. Reserve large lambda for processes with genuine step changes and same-run metrology.
