## Start here - the on-ramp (read this even if you're brand new)

Before any math, here's the whole field in one analogy. Suppose you want to predict a house's price from its size. You have a notebook of past sales: "1500 sq ft → \$300k, 2000 sq ft → \$380k, …". **Machine learning is just drawing the best line (or curve) through those dots so that when a new house shows up, you can read its price off the line.**

That's it. Everything in this chapter is a more careful version of that sentence:

- **"The dots"** are your *training data*, examples you learn from.
- **"The line"** is your *model*, a rule with adjustable knobs (the *parameters* `w`).
- **"Best"** means you define a number that says how wrong the line is (the *loss*), and you twist the knobs to make that number as small as possible.
- **"A new house"** is the *test point*, and the entire reason ML is hard is that you care about being right on houses you've *never seen*, not just memorizing the ones you have. That's **generalization**, and it's the difference between a useful model and an expensive party trick.

**Why does this matter?** Because almost every "smart" product is this loop in disguise. Spam filter: dots are emails labeled spam/not-spam, the line separates them. Netflix recommendations, fraud alerts, demand forecasts, medical risk scores, the autocomplete finishing this sentence, all the same skeleton: *data in, find the rule, predict on new data, measure if it generalized.* Learn the skeleton once and the whole field stops looking like a pile of acronyms.

Two words you'll hear constantly, demystified up front:
- **Overfitting** = memorizing the training dots instead of learning the trend (great on data you've seen, terrible on new data). Like a student who memorizes the practice exam answers and bombs the real test.
- **Underfitting** = the line is too simple to capture the trend at all (a straight line through data that clearly curves). Like answering every exam question with "42."

The rest of this chapter makes those two ideas precise and shows you exactly which knob fixes each. Keep the house-price picture in your head; we'll keep coming back to it.

## The learning problem

### The setup

Supervised learning assumes there is some unknown function `f: X → Y` and a joint distribution `P(X, Y)` over inputs and outputs. You get a finite sample drawn from it:

$$\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(1)}, y^{(1)}), \dots, (x^{(m)}, y^{(m)})\} \sim P(X, Y)^m$$

You pick a **hypothesis class** `H` (linear functions, trees, nets…) and search for the hypothesis `h ∈ H` that best approximates `f`. "Best" is defined by a **loss** `L(h(x), y)`. The thing you actually care about is the **expected risk**, the loss over the whole distribution, not just your sample:

$$R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim P}\big[L(h(x), y)\big]$$

You can't compute that (you don't have `P`), so you minimize the **empirical risk**, the average loss over your data:

$$\hat{R}(h) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m L(h(x^{(i)}), y^{(i)})$$

Everything in this chapter is a strategy for making `\hat{R}` small *while keeping `R` small too*. That gap, training error vs. true error, is **generalization**, and it's the whole game.

### Three flavors

- **Supervised**, you have labels `y`. Regression (`y` continuous) or classification (`y` categorical). This is most real-world work: classifiers, forecasters.
- **Unsupervised**, no labels. You model the structure of `X` itself: clustering (k-means), density estimation, dimensionality reduction (PCA). High-dimensional data work lives here.
- **Reinforcement learning**, no fixed dataset; an agent takes actions in an environment and learns from a scalar reward signal. Different beast; we'll only sketch it elsewhere.

### Generalization and the bias - variance tradeoff (derived)

Here's the decomposition everyone hand-waves. Let's actually do it.

Assume the true label is a deterministic function plus noise:

$$y = f(x) + \varepsilon, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0, \quad \text{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$$

Your model `\hat{f}` is trained on a random dataset `D`, so `\hat{f}` is itself a random variable (different `D` → different fit). Fix a test point `x` and look at the **expected squared error** over both the noise and the randomness in `D`:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\big]$$

Let's write `\hat{f} = \hat{f}(x)` and `\bar{f} = \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{f}(x)]` (the average prediction across all possible training sets). Expand:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f})^2\big]
= \mathbb{E}\big[(f + \varepsilon - \hat{f})^2\big]
$$

Insert `+\bar f - \bar f`:

$$
= \mathbb{E}\big[((f - \bar f) + (\bar f - \hat f) + \varepsilon)^2\big]
$$

Expand the square. The cross terms vanish: `ε` is independent of `\hat f` and has mean 0, and `(\bar f - \hat f)` has mean 0 over `D` while `(f - \bar f)` is a constant. What survives:

$$
\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f})^2\big]
= \underbrace{(f - \bar f)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2}
+ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f - \bar f)^2\big]}_{\text{Variance}}
+ \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible}}\;}
$$

That's it, the cleanest result in ML.

- **Bias²**, how far your *average* model is from the truth. High when the model is too simple (linear fit to a curve). This is **underfitting**.
- **Variance**, how much your model jiggles when you resample the data. High when the model is too flexible (deep tree memorizing noise). This is **overfitting**.
- **Irreducible noise** `σ²`, the floor. No model beats it.

Total error is a U-shape in model complexity: bias dominates on the left, variance on the right. The sweet spot is the minimum of the sum, not of either term. Every regularization knob, every "make the model smaller/bigger" decision, is you sliding along this curve.

```
error
  |   \                         /
  |    \  bias²                / variance
  |     \                     /
  |      \___           _____/
  |          \_______ /          <- total = bias² + var + σ²
  |  - - - - - - - - - - - - σ²   (floor)
  +--------------------------------> model complexity
       underfit        overfit
```

## The math you actually need

We won't re-teach linear algebra. Let's pin down the four objects that show up in every gradient you're about to derive.

**Vectors.** A weight vector `w ∈ ℝⁿ`, a feature vector `x ∈ ℝⁿ`. The **dot product** is the linear predictor:

$$w^\top x = \sum_{j=1}^n w_j x_j$$

Geometrically `w^\top x = \|w\|\,\|x\|\cos\theta`. It measures alignment. A linear model is "score how aligned the input is with your learned direction `w`."

**Matrices.** Stack `m` examples as rows of a design matrix `X ∈ ℝ^{m×n}`. Then `Xw ∈ ℝ^m` is all `m` predictions at once. Batched linear algebra is why NumPy is fast, no Python loop over examples.

**Gradients.** For a scalar function `J(w)` of a vector `w`, the gradient is the vector of partials:

$$\nabla_w J = \left[\frac{\partial J}{\partial w_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_n}\right]^\top$$

It points in the direction of steepest *increase*. So to *minimize*, you step the opposite way. That single fact is gradient descent.

**Two identities we'll lean on** (derive once, reuse forever):

$$\nabla_w (a^\top w) = a, \qquad \nabla_w (w^\top A w) = (A + A^\top)w$$

The second, for symmetric `A`, is `2Aw`, the matrix analog of `d/dx (ax²) = 2ax`.

**The chain rule.** If `J` depends on `z` which depends on `w`, then `\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}`. This is *all* of backprop, applied repeatedly. We'll use it constantly.

## Linear regression from scratch

### The model

$$\hat{y} = w^\top x + b$$

We'll fold the bias into `w` by appending a constant `1` feature to every `x`, so `\hat{y} = w^\top x` and `w_0 = b`. Cleaner derivations.

### The loss: mean squared error

$$J(w) = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m \big(w^\top x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\big)^2 = \frac{1}{2m}\|Xw - y\|^2$$

The `1/2` is a convenience, it cancels the `2` from the derivative. MSE isn't arbitrary: if `y = w^\top x + ε` with Gaussian noise `ε ∼ N(0, σ²)`, then maximizing the likelihood of the data is *exactly* minimizing MSE (the log of a Gaussian is a negative square). So least squares = maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise.

### Derive the gradient

Let `r = Xw - y` (the residual vector). Then `J = \frac{1}{2m} r^\top r`. Using the chain rule with `\partial r / \partial w = X`:

$$\nabla_w J = \frac{1}{m} X^\top r = \frac{1}{m} X^\top (Xw - y)$$

Sanity check the shapes: `Xᵀ` is `n×m`, `(Xw − y)` is `m×1`, product is `n×1`, same shape as `w`. 

### Gradient descent

Repeat until convergence:

$$w \leftarrow w - \alpha \cdot \frac{1}{m} X^\top(Xw - y)$$

`α` is the **learning rate**. Too small → crawls. Too large → overshoots and diverges (the loss explodes). For a convex quadratic like MSE there's a single global minimum, so GD always gets there with a reasonable `α`.

### The closed-form normal equation

MSE is convex and differentiable, so you can just set the gradient to zero:

$$X^\top(Xw - y) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; X^\top X w = X^\top y \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{w = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y}$$

That's the **normal equation**, an exact solution, no iteration. So why ever use gradient descent? Because `(XᵀX)⁻¹` is `O(n³)` to invert and `XᵀX` is `n×n`. With millions of features (or examples streaming in), the matrix is huge or singular. GD scales; the normal equation doesn't. Knowing both, and *when* each wins, is the point.

### Learning rate, scaling, convergence

- **Feature scaling.** If feature A ranges 0-1 and feature B ranges 0-10000, the loss surface is a long thin valley; GD zig-zags. Standardize (`(x − μ)/σ`) so the valley is round and GD goes straight to the bottom. The normal equation doesn't care about scale, but GD desperately does.
- **Convergence.** Plot `J` vs. iteration. It should decrease monotonically and flatten. If it oscillates or grows, `α` is too big. If it's still dropping at the last step, train longer or raise `α`.

### Worked example - two gradient-descent steps by hand

Let's make GD concrete with numbers you can check on paper. Tiny dataset, one feature plus bias:

| `x` | `y` |
|-----|-----|
| 1   | 2   |
| 2   | 3   |

So with the bias-folded design matrix `X = [[1,1],[1,2]]` (first column = the constant), `y = [2, 3]`, and `m = 2`. Start at `w = [0, 0]` (so `w_0 = b = 0`, `w_1 = 0`), learning rate `α = 0.1`.

**Step 0, predictions and residuals.** `Xw = [0, 0]`, so residual `r = Xw - y = [-2, -3]`.

**Gradient:** `∇J = (1/m) Xᵀ r = (1/2) · [[1,1],[1,2]]ᵀ · [-2,-3]`.

Compute `Xᵀ r`: row 1 is `1·(-2) + 1·(-3) = -5`; row 2 is `1·(-2) + 2·(-3) = -8`. So `Xᵀr = [-5, -8]`, and `∇J = (1/2)[-5,-8] = [-2.5, -4.0]`.

**Update:** `w ← w - α∇J = [0,0] - 0.1·[-2.5,-4.0] = [0.25, 0.40]`.

**Step 1, recompute.** `Xw = [0.25 + 0.40·1, 0.25 + 0.40·2] = [0.65, 1.05]`. Residual `r = [0.65-2, 1.05-3] = [-1.35, -1.95]`.

`Xᵀr`: row 1 `= -1.35 + -1.95 = -3.30`; row 2 `= -1.35 + 2·(-1.95) = -5.25`. `∇J = (1/2)[-3.30,-5.25] = [-1.65, -2.625]`.

**Update:** `w ← [0.25,0.40] - 0.1·[-1.65,-2.625] = [0.415, 0.6625]`.

Notice the gradient magnitudes are *shrinking* (`[-2.5,-4]` → `[-1.65,-2.6]`) and `w` is climbing toward the true least-squares solution. Sanity check with the normal equation: `XᵀX = [[2,3],[3,5]]`, `Xᵀy = [5, 8]`, so `w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy`. The inverse of `[[2,3],[3,5]]` is `[[5,-3],[-3,2]]` (determinant `= 10-9 = 1`), giving `w = [5·5 + -3·8, -3·5 + 2·8] = [1, 1]`. So GD is marching toward `w = [1, 1]`, meaning `y = 1 + 1·x`, which indeed passes through `(1,2)` and `(2,3)` exactly. Run a few more steps and you'll watch it land on `[1, 1]`. *That's* gradient descent, no library, fully auditable.

## Logistic regression from scratch

Now classification. You want a probability `P(y=1 | x) ∈ (0,1)`, but `w^\top x` is unbounded. Squash it.

### The sigmoid

$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad z = w^\top x$$

It maps `ℝ → (0,1)`, is monotonic, and `σ(0) = 0.5`. Its derivative is gorgeous and you'll need it:

$$\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big)$$

Quick proof: `σ = (1+e^{-z})^{-1}`, so `σ' = -(1+e^{-z})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-z}) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} = σ \cdot \frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} = σ(1-σ)`. 

### The loss: cross-entropy, derived from MLE

Model each label as Bernoulli with parameter `\hat{p} = σ(w^\top x)`:

$$P(y \mid x) = \hat{p}^{\,y}(1 - \hat{p})^{1-y}$$

(Check: `y=1` gives `\hat p`, `y=0` gives `1-\hat p`.) The likelihood over the dataset is the product; the **log**-likelihood is the sum:

$$\ell(w) = \sum_i \Big[ y^{(i)} \log \hat{p}^{(i)} + (1 - y^{(i)})\log(1 - \hat{p}^{(i)})\Big]$$

Maximizing log-likelihood = minimizing its negative, averaged:

$$J(w) = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_i \Big[ y^{(i)} \log \hat{p}^{(i)} + (1 - y^{(i)})\log(1 - \hat{p}^{(i)})\Big]$$

That's **binary cross-entropy / log-loss**. It's not a heuristic, it's the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli model. That's *why* it's the right loss for classification, and why MSE-on-probabilities is wrong (non-convex in `w`, weak gradients when very confident-and-wrong).

### Derive the gradient

Here's the small miracle. Let `\hat p = σ(z)`, `z = w^\top x`. For one example, by the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial \hat p}\cdot\frac{\partial \hat p}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}$$

Compute each:

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial \hat p} = -\Big(\frac{y}{\hat p} - \frac{1-y}{1-\hat p}\Big) = \frac{\hat p - y}{\hat p(1-\hat p)}, \quad \frac{\partial \hat p}{\partial z} = \hat p(1-\hat p), \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial w} = x$$

The `\hat p(1-\hat p)` terms **cancel**:

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = (\hat p - y)\,x$$

Averaged over the batch, in matrix form:

$$\boxed{\nabla_w J = \frac{1}{m}X^\top(\hat p - y)}$$

This is the *identical shape* to linear regression's gradient, `\frac{1}{m}X^\top(\text{prediction} - \text{target})`. That's not a coincidence: both are generalized linear models with their "natural" loss, and the error-times-input form is universal. No closed form here (the sigmoid makes it transcendental), so gradient descent it is.

### Worked example - one logistic-regression forward + gradient step

One example, two features: `x = [1, 2]` (no bias for brevity), label `y = 1`. Current weights `w = [0.5, -0.5]`.

**Forward.** `z = wᵀx = 0.5·1 + (-0.5)·2 = 0.5 - 1.0 = -0.5`. Then `p̂ = σ(-0.5) = 1/(1+e^{0.5})`. With `e^{0.5} ≈ 1.6487`: `p̂ ≈ 1/2.6487 ≈ 0.3775`.

**Loss (this one example).** `J = -[y log p̂ + (1-y) log(1-p̂)] = -log(0.3775) ≈ 0.974`. The model said "probably class 0" (`p̂ < 0.5`) but the truth is class 1, so the loss is large, good, that's what loss should do.

**Gradient.** Using the clean form `∇ = (p̂ - y)x = (0.3775 - 1)·[1, 2] = -0.6225·[1,2] = [-0.6225, -1.245]`.

**Update** with `α = 0.1`: `w ← [0.5, -0.5] - 0.1·[-0.6225, -1.245] = [0.5623, -0.3755]`.

**Did it help?** New `z = 0.5623·1 + (-0.3755)·2 = 0.5623 - 0.751 = -0.1887`, so new `p̂ = σ(-0.1887) ≈ 0.453`. It climbed from `0.3775` toward `1`, the step pushed the probability of the *correct* class up, exactly as intended. One step, fully by hand, and you can see the mechanism working.

### Regularization: L1 and L2

Add a penalty on weight size to the loss:

- **L2 (Ridge):** `J + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|_2^2`. Gradient gains `+\lambda w`, so the update becomes `w \leftarrow (1 - \alpha\lambda)w - \alpha\nabla J`, every step *shrinks* `w` toward zero (weight decay). Penalizes large weights smoothly; weights get small but rarely exactly zero.
- **L1 (Lasso):** `J + \lambda\|w\|_1`. Subgradient adds `+\lambda\,\text{sign}(w)`, a constant push toward zero regardless of magnitude. This drives weights *exactly* to zero → **sparse** models / feature selection.

**Geometric intuition.** Picture the loss contours (ellipses) meeting the constraint region `\|w\| \le t`. L2's region is a circle, the contour tends to touch it on a smooth edge, both weights small but nonzero. L1's region is a diamond with corners *on the axes*, the contour usually hits a corner, where one coordinate is zero. Corners cause sparsity. That single picture explains everything.

```
   L2 (circle): touches edge        L1 (diamond): touches corner
        w2                                 w2
        |   ((o))   <- contour              |  /\
        | (  *  )                           | /  \   <- contour kisses corner
   -----+--*-----  w1                  -----+/----\---- w1   (w1 ≠ 0, w2 = 0)
        | (     )                           |\    /
        |                                   | \  /
                                            |  \/
```

## Gradient descent variants

Same update, different amount of data per step:

- **Batch GD**, full dataset per step. Smooth, exact gradient, but one step needs a full pass; slow per update.
- **Stochastic GD (SGD)**, one example per step. Noisy gradient, but tons of updates; the noise can even help escape shallow minima. Needs a decaying learning rate to settle.
- **Mini-batch**, `b` examples per step (32, 64, 256…). The default everywhere. Vectorizes well on hardware, noise is a useful regularizer, and it's the bridge to deep learning. An **epoch** = one full pass = `m/b` mini-batch steps.

## Model evaluation done right

This is where you'll see the most real-world damage, so we'll be pedantic.

### The split

- **Train**, fit parameters.
- **Validation**, tune hyperparameters (`α`, `λ`, tree depth) and pick models.
- **Test**, touched *once*, at the very end, to estimate true performance. If you tune on the test set, you've leaked it and your estimate is optimistic garbage.

**k-fold cross-validation**, when data is scarce: split into `k` folds, train on `k−1`, validate on the held-out one, rotate, average. Uses all data for both training and validation and gives a variance estimate. For classification use **stratified** k-fold so each fold preserves class balance.

### The confusion matrix and friends

For binary classification:

|                | Predicted + | Predicted − |
|----------------|-------------|-------------|
| **Actual +**   | TP          | FN          |
| **Actual −**   | FP          | TN          |

- **Accuracy** = `(TP+TN)/total`.
- **Precision** = `TP/(TP+FP)`, of what you flagged positive, how much was right? (Cost of false alarms.)
- **Recall (sensitivity)** = `TP/(TP+FN)`, of all real positives, how many did you catch? (Cost of misses.)
- **F1** = harmonic mean `2PR/(P+R)`, balances the two, punishes lopsidedness.
- **ROC-AUC**, sweep the decision threshold, plot TPR vs. FPR, take the area. Threshold-independent ranking quality; 0.5 is random, 1.0 is perfect.

### Why accuracy lies on imbalanced data

This is the one worth telling everyone. Fraud detection: 99% legitimate, 1% fraud. A model that predicts "legit, always" scores **99% accuracy** and catches *zero* fraud, precision and recall on the positive class are both 0. Accuracy rewarded it for ignoring the only class that matters. On any imbalanced classification problem (which is most of them) report precision/recall/F1 and AUC, look at the confusion matrix, and consider class weights, resampling (SMOTE), or threshold tuning. Accuracy is the headline number that hides the failure.

### Worked example - read a confusion matrix end to end

A disease screen on 1,000 patients. 100 actually have the disease (positive). Your model:

|                | Predicted + | Predicted − |
|----------------|-------------|-------------|
| **Actual +**   | TP = 80     | FN = 20     |
| **Actual −**   | FP = 50     | TN = 850    |

- **Accuracy** `= (80+850)/1000 = 0.93`, looks great.
- **Precision** `= TP/(TP+FP) = 80/130 ≈ 0.615`, of everyone you flagged sick, only 62% really were. 50 healthy people got a scary false positive.
- **Recall** `= TP/(TP+FN) = 80/100 = 0.80`, you caught 80% of the truly sick, but **missed 20** (the FNs, the dangerous ones in a medical setting).
- **F1** `= 2·(0.615·0.80)/(0.615+0.80) = 2·0.492/1.415 ≈ 0.696`, the harmonic mean lands below the arithmetic mean, punishing the gap between precision and recall.

The takeaway that 0.93 accuracy hid: you're letting 1 in 5 sick patients walk out untreated. **Which error matters more decides the threshold.** For a disease screen, missing a case (FN) is far worse than a false alarm (FP), so you'd lower the decision threshold to raise recall, accepting more FPs to catch more real cases. For a spam filter it's the reverse (a deleted real email is worse than one spam slipping through), so you'd raise the threshold for precision. *There is no universally correct metric; there's the metric that matches the cost of each mistake.*

### Common mistakes

These are the bugs that quietly inflate your scores and then detonate in production. Most people make them at least once.

- **Data leakage via preprocessing.** Fitting your scaler / imputer / PCA / target-encoder on the *whole* dataset before splitting. The test set's statistics bleed into training and your CV score becomes optimistic fiction. Fix: fit every transformer **inside** a `Pipeline` so each CV fold only sees its own training portion. (This is *the* most common interview "spot the bug.")
- **Evaluating on the training set.** "99% accuracy!" on the same data you fit on tells you nothing about generalization, a 1-NN gets 100% by definition. Always report on held-out data.
- **Touching the test set more than once.** Every time you peek at test performance and then change something, you've leaked it a little. Tune on validation; test is a one-shot final exam.
- **Not scaling for distance/gradient-based methods.** k-NN, SVM (RBF), logistic-regression-with-GD, and PCA all care about feature scale. A feature in dollars (0-1,000,000) will steamroll one in fractions (0-1). Trees don't care; everything else does.
- **Target leakage from the future / from the label.** Including a feature that's only known *after* the outcome (e.g., "was_refunded" when predicting fraud) or that's a near-copy of the label. Your model looks magical, then fails on day one because that feature isn't available at prediction time.
- **Judging imbalanced problems by accuracy.** Covered above, report precision/recall/F1/AUC and look at the confusion matrix.
- **Shuffling time-series data.** For anything with a time order, a random split lets the model "see the future." Split chronologically.
- **Comparing models on different splits.** Use the same `random_state` / same CV folds so the comparison is apples-to-apples.

### On the job / interview angle

These come up in nearly every data/ML screen. Short, defensible answers:

> **Q: Explain the bias-variance tradeoff.**
> A: Expected test error decomposes into bias² + variance + irreducible noise (derived above). Bias is error from the model being too simple (underfitting); variance is error from the model being too sensitive to the particular training set (overfitting). More complexity lowers bias but raises variance, you tune to the minimum of the *sum*, not either term. Regularization, more data, and ensembling are the main levers.

> **Q: How do you know if your model is overfitting, and what do you do?**
> A: Training error much lower than validation error, or validation error rising while training error keeps falling. Fixes: more data, regularization (L1/L2), a simpler model, feature selection, early stopping, or cross-validation to detect it reliably.

> **Q: Precision vs. recall, when do you optimize which?**
> A: Optimize **recall** when missing a positive is costly (disease screening, fraud, churn). Optimize **precision** when a false positive is costly (spam filter, recommending content, automated actions). Use F1 when you want balance, and AUC when you care about ranking quality independent of threshold.

> **Q: When would you use L1 vs. L2 regularization?**
> A: L1 (Lasso) when you want sparsity / automatic feature selection (many irrelevant features). L2 (Ridge) when features are correlated and you want to shrink them smoothly without zeroing them out. Elastic Net blends both.

> **Q: Your model has 99% accuracy. Are you happy?**
> A: Not until I see the class balance and the confusion matrix. On a 99%-negative dataset, 99% accuracy can mean "predicts negative always." I'd report precision/recall/F1/AUC on the minority class.

> **Q: Tree-based model vs. linear model, how do you choose?**
> A: Linear/logistic for interpretable, mostly-linear relationships and when you need calibrated coefficients; gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost/LightGBM) for messy tabular data with nonlinear interactions and mixed feature types, usually the strongest tabular baseline. Neural nets when you have lots of data and unstructured inputs (images, text, audio).

> **Q: What's the difference between generative and discriminative models?**
> A: Discriminative models learn `P(y|x)` directly (logistic regression, SVMs, most nets). Generative models learn `P(x,y)` (or `P(x|y)` and `P(y)`) and apply Bayes' rule, e.g., Naive Bayes, GDA, GANs/diffusion for `P(x)`. Generative can sample new data and works with less data sometimes; discriminative usually wins on raw predictive accuracy.

## Feature engineering & the curse of dimensionality

Models are only as good as their features. Standard moves: scaling, one-hot encoding categoricals, interaction/polynomial terms, log-transforming skewed variables, binning, domain ratios, date decomposition. Garbage features → garbage model, regardless of algorithm.

**The curse of dimensionality**, as `n` grows, volume explodes and data gets sparse. In high `d`, almost all the volume of a hypercube is in its corners, and pairwise distances concentrate (everything is roughly equidistant), which guts distance-based methods like k-NN. You also need exponentially more data to densely cover the space. Defenses: dimensionality reduction (PCA), feature selection (L1), regularization, and trees/embeddings that don't rely on raw Euclidean distance. High-dimensional work succeeds when you *reduce* effective dimension, not when you throw every column at the model.

---

## A tour of key algorithms (with intuition)

- **k-NN**, predict by the majority/average of the `k` nearest training points. No training; all the work at inference. Dead simple, but distance-based → murdered by high dimensions and needs scaling.
- **Decision trees**, recursively split features to make regions pure. The split is chosen to maximize purity gain. **Gini impurity** `= 1 - \sum_c p_c^2` and **entropy** `= -\sum_c p_c \log p_c` both measure how mixed a node is; the tree greedily picks the (feature, threshold) that most reduces the weighted child impurity (**information gain**). Interpretable, no scaling needed, but a single deep tree overfits (high variance).
- **Random forests**, many trees on bootstrapped samples with random feature subsets, then average/vote. **Bagging** averages away the variance of individual trees. Robust, strong default, low tuning.
- **Gradient boosting** (XGBoost/LightGBM), build trees *sequentially*, each fitting the residual errors (negative gradient) of the ensemble so far. Reduces *bias* by additively correcting. Usually the top performer on tabular data; needs careful tuning (learning rate, depth, regularization) to avoid overfitting.
- **SVMs**, find the hyperplane with the **maximum margin** between classes; only the **support vectors** (boundary points) matter. The **kernel trick** maps to high-dimensional spaces implicitly to handle nonlinearity. Strong on medium-sized, clean data.
- **k-means** (unsupervised), partition into `k` clusters; alternate assigning points to the nearest centroid and recomputing centroids. Minimizes within-cluster variance. Fast, but assumes round, similar-size clusters and you must pick `k`.

### PCA derived as eigen-decomposition of the covariance

PCA finds the orthogonal directions of maximum variance. Center the data (`X` mean-zero per column). You want a unit vector `u` maximizing the variance of the projections `Xu`:

$$\text{Var}(Xu) = \frac{1}{m}(Xu)^\top(Xu) = u^\top \Big(\tfrac{1}{m}X^\top X\Big) u = u^\top \Sigma u$$

where `Σ` is the covariance matrix. Maximize `u^\top \Sigma u` subject to `\|u\|=1`. Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal{L} = u^\top \Sigma u - \lambda(u^\top u - 1), \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u} = 2\Sigma u - 2\lambda u = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\Sigma u = \lambda u}$$

So the optimal directions are the **eigenvectors of the covariance matrix**, and the variance captured by each is its **eigenvalue** `λ` (since `u^\top \Sigma u = \lambda u^\top u = \lambda`). The top-`k` eigenvectors (largest eigenvalues) are the principal components. PCA is *literally* eigen-decomposition of `Σ`, that's the whole algorithm.

## Build it

### Linear regression in NumPy (GD + normal equation), vs. sklearn

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Synthetic data: y = 4 + 3x + noise
m = 200
X_raw = 2 * rng.random((m, 1))
y = 4 + 3 * X_raw[:, 0] + rng.normal(0, 0.5, m)

def add_bias(X):
    return np.c_[np.ones((X.shape[0], 1)), X]   # prepend constant feature

Xb = add_bias(X_raw)                              # (m, 2): [1, x]

# ---- Closed form: normal equation w = (XᵀX)⁻¹ Xᵀy ----
w_normal = np.linalg.inv(Xb.T @ Xb) @ Xb.T @ y
print("Normal equation w:", w_normal)             # ~ [4, 3]

# ---- Gradient descent ----
def gd_linear(Xb, y, alpha=0.1, n_iters=2000):
    m, n = Xb.shape
    w = np.zeros(n)
    history = []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        grad = (1/m) * Xb.T @ (Xb @ w - y)        # derived gradient
        w -= alpha * grad
        history.append(np.mean((Xb @ w - y) ** 2))
    return w, history

w_gd, hist = gd_linear(Xb, y)
print("Gradient descent w:", w_gd)                # ~ same as normal eqn

# ---- sklearn confirms ----
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
lr = LinearRegression().fit(X_raw, y)
print("sklearn intercept, coef:", lr.intercept_, lr.coef_)  # ~ [4], [3]

# ---- Plots ----
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4))
ax1.scatter(X_raw, y, s=10, alpha=0.5)
xs = np.linspace(0, 2, 100)
ax1.plot(xs, w_gd[0] + w_gd[1]*xs, 'r-', lw=2, label="GD fit")
ax1.set_title("Linear regression fit"); ax1.legend()
ax2.plot(hist); ax2.set_xlabel("iteration"); ax2.set_ylabel("MSE")
ax2.set_title("Loss converges (monotone ↓)")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

All three agree on `w ≈ [4, 3]`. The from-scratch GD, the from-scratch normal equation, and sklearn land in the same place, exactly what you want before trusting any black box.

### Logistic regression in NumPy, vs. sklearn

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=400, n_features=2, n_redundant=0,
                           n_informative=2, n_clusters_per_class=1, random_state=1)
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)   # scaling matters for GD
Xb = np.c_[np.ones((X.shape[0], 1)), X]

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(z, -500, 500)))   # clip avoids overflow

def gd_logistic(Xb, y, alpha=0.3, n_iters=3000, lam=0.0):
    m, n = Xb.shape
    w = np.zeros(n)
    loss_hist = []
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        p = sigmoid(Xb @ w)
        grad = (1/m) * Xb.T @ (p - y)        # derived: Xᵀ(p − y)/m
        grad[1:] += (lam/m) * w[1:]          # L2, skip bias term
        w -= alpha * grad
        eps = 1e-12
        loss = -np.mean(y*np.log(p+eps) + (1-y)*np.log(1-p+eps))
        loss_hist.append(loss + (lam/(2*m))*np.sum(w[1:]**2))
    return w, loss_hist

w_log, loss_hist = gd_logistic(Xb, y)
preds = (sigmoid(Xb @ w_log) >= 0.5).astype(int)
print("From-scratch accuracy:", (preds == y).mean())

# ---- sklearn confirms ----
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
clf = LogisticRegression(C=1e9).fit(X, y)   # C huge ≈ no regularization
print("sklearn accuracy:", clf.score(X, y))
print("our w:", w_log)
print("sklearn w:", clf.intercept_, clf.coef_)   # ~ matches (sign/scale)
```

The hand-coded log-loss gradient `Xᵀ(p − y)/m`, the one whose `\hat p(1-\hat p)` terms canceled in the derivation, trains a classifier that matches sklearn. You can now read sklearn's source and know exactly what `LogisticRegression` is doing under the hood.

## Mini-project: end-to-end classification on a real dataset

Full pipeline on the Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset: EDA → preprocessing → model selection with cross-validation → proper evaluation → interpretation. This is the shape of every real classification task you'll do.

```python
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import (classification_report, confusion_matrix,
                             roc_auc_score, RocCurveDisplay)

# ---- 1. Load + EDA ----
data = load_breast_cancer(as_frame=True)
df = data.frame
print(df.shape)                       # (569, 31)
print(df["target"].value_counts(normalize=True))   # ~63% / 37% - mild imbalance
print(df.isna().sum().sum(), "missing values")
# Correlation snapshot - many features are collinear (radius/perimeter/area)
corr = df.corr()["target"].sort_values()
print(corr.head(5)); print(corr.tail(5))

X = data.data; y = data.target

# ---- 2. Train/test split (stratified, test touched ONCE) ----
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=42)

# ---- 3. Pipelines (scaling lives INSIDE the pipeline → no leakage in CV) ----
logreg = Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()),
                   ("clf", LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000))])
rf = Pipeline([("clf", RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=42))])

# ---- 4. Model selection via stratified cross-validation ----
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
for name, model in [("LogReg", logreg), ("RandomForest", rf)]:
    scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc")
    print(f"{name}: AUC {scores.mean():.4f} ± {scores.std():.4f}")

# LogReg typically edges out RF here (clean, mostly-linear separable data).

# ---- 5. Fit best model, evaluate on the held-out TEST set ----
best = logreg.fit(X_train, y_train)
y_pred = best.predict(X_test)
y_proba = best.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]

print(confusion_matrix(y_test, y_pred))
print(classification_report(y_test, y_pred, digits=3))   # precision/recall/F1
print("Test AUC:", roc_auc_score(y_test, y_proba))
RocCurveDisplay.from_predictions(y_test, y_proba); plt.show()

# ---- 6. Interpretation: which features drive the decision? ----
scaler = best.named_steps["scale"]
coefs = best.named_steps["clf"].coef_[0]   # on standardized features → comparable
importance = (pd.Series(coefs, index=X.columns)
              .sort_values(key=np.abs, ascending=False).head(10))
print(importance)
# Because features are standardized, |coef| ranks influence directly.
# Sign tells direction: negative coef → pushes toward malignant class, etc.
```

**Decisions and why:**
- Scaling *inside* the Pipeline so each CV fold fits its scaler only on its own training portion, otherwise test statistics leak into training and CV scores are optimistic.
- Stratified folds so class ratios are preserved (mild imbalance here, critical when severe).
- `roc_auc` as the CV metric, threshold-independent and robust to the imbalance, unlike raw accuracy.
- Report the full classification report + confusion matrix on test, not a single accuracy number.
- Interpret via standardized coefficients so magnitudes are comparable across features.

This is the discipline that separates a demo from a deployable result: no leakage, honest evaluation, interpretable conclusion.

## Mini-project 2: build k-means and PCA from scratch, then watch the bias - variance curve

Two from-scratch builds that cement the unsupervised half of this chapter, plus a learning-curve experiment that makes bias-variance visible instead of theoretical.

### Part A - k-means by hand (Lloyd's algorithm)

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Three blobs
centers = np.array([[0,0],[5,5],[0,6]])
X = np.vstack([c + rng.normal(0, 0.8, (100,2)) for c in centers])

def kmeans(X, k, n_iters=20, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    # init: pick k random points as centroids
    cent = X[rng.choice(len(X), k, replace=False)]
    for _ in range(n_iters):
        # ASSIGN: each point to nearest centroid (broadcasted distances)
        d = np.linalg.norm(X[:, None, :] - cent[None, :, :], axis=2)  # (m, k)
        labels = d.argmin(axis=1)
        # UPDATE: centroid = mean of its assigned points
        new_cent = np.array([X[labels==j].mean(axis=0) if (labels==j).any()
                             else cent[j] for j in range(k)])
        if np.allclose(new_cent, cent):   # converged
            break
        cent = new_cent
    inertia = sum(((X[labels==j] - cent[j])**2).sum() for j in range(k))
    return labels, cent, inertia

labels, cent, inertia = kmeans(X, k=3)
print("within-cluster SSE (inertia):", round(inertia, 1))

# Elbow: inertia drops sharply until k = true clusters, then flattens
ks = range(1, 8)
inertias = [kmeans(X, k)[2] for k in ks]
plt.plot(list(ks), inertias, 'o-'); plt.xlabel("k"); plt.ylabel("inertia")
plt.title("Elbow at k=3 - the true number of clusters"); plt.show()
```

The two-step `ASSIGN → UPDATE` loop *is* k-means; it minimizes within-cluster variance by coordinate descent. The elbow plot is how you pick `k` without knowing the answer.

### Part B - PCA from scratch, confirmed against sklearn

```python
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

X = load_iris().data
Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)                       # center (PCA requires it)
cov = (Xc.T @ Xc) / (len(Xc) - 1)            # covariance matrix Σ
eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(cov)        # symmetric → eigh
order = np.argsort(eigvals)[::-1]            # largest variance first
eigvals, eigvecs = eigvals[order], eigvecs[:, order]

Z = Xc @ eigvecs[:, :2]                        # project onto top-2 PCs
explained = eigvals / eigvals.sum()
print("variance explained by PC1, PC2:", explained[:2].round(3))  # ~[0.92, 0.05]

# sklearn confirms (signs may flip - eigenvectors are unique up to sign)
Z_sk = PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(X)
print("match (abs):", np.allclose(np.abs(Z), np.abs(Z_sk), atol=1e-6))  # True
```

This is the eigen-decomposition derivation made real: covariance → `eigh` → project. Two components capture ~97% of Iris's variance, exactly why PCA is the go-to for compressing high-dimensional data.

### Part C - make bias - variance visible with a learning curve

```python
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

# True function is a cubic; fit polynomials of increasing degree
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 60); y = 0.5*x**3 - x + rng.normal(0, 2, 60)
Xc = x.reshape(-1, 1)

for deg in [1, 3, 12]:
    model = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(deg), LinearRegression())
    cv = -cross_val_score(model, Xc, y, cv=5,
                          scoring="neg_mean_squared_error").mean()
    print(f"degree {deg:2d}:  CV MSE {cv:7.2f}")
# degree 1  -> high error (underfit / high bias)
# degree 3  -> lowest error (matches truth)
# degree 12 -> high error again (overfit / high variance)
```

Degree 1 underfits (high bias), degree 12 overfits (high variance), degree 3, the true complexity, wins. You just *watched* the U-shaped curve from the start of the chapter, on real numbers. **This experiment is the whole chapter in ten lines.**

Work these by hand or in a few lines of NumPy. Solutions follow.

3. **(Easy)** A classifier has TP=40, FP=10, FN=20, TN=130. Compute accuracy, precision, recall, and F1.
4. **(Medium)** Given `X=[[1,1],[1,3]]` (bias-folded), `y=[2,4]`, solve the normal equation `w=(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy` by hand.
5. **(Medium)** Do one gradient-descent step for logistic regression: `x=[2,1]`, `y=0`, `w=[0.3,0.1]`, `α=0.5`. Report `z`, `p̂`, the gradient, and the new `w`.
6. **(Medium)** You standardize your features using the mean and std of the *entire* dataset, then split into train/test and run cross-validation. Name the bug and explain how it inflates your score.
7. **(Hard)** Show that adding L2 regularization `(λ/2)‖w‖²` to MSE changes the normal equation to `w = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy`. Why does this also fix the case where `XᵀX` is singular?
8. **(Hard)** A fraud dataset is 2% positive. Model A: accuracy 98%, recall 0%. Model B: accuracy 95%, recall 70%, precision 30%. Which would you ship and why? What single number best summarizes B's tradeoff?
9. **(Hard)** Show that for a symmetric matrix `A`, `∇_w(wᵀAw) = 2Aw`. (Hint: write `wᵀAw = Σᵢⱼ wᵢAᵢⱼwⱼ` and differentiate w.r.t. `w_k`.)
10. **(Hard / conceptual)** You have 50,000 features and 500 training examples. Which algorithms are likely to fail, and what three techniques would you reach for first? Tie your answer to the curse of dimensionality.

### Solutions

1. The points are collinear: slope `w = (5-1)/(2-0) = 2`, intercept `b = 1`. Line `y = 1 + 2x` hits all three exactly, so **MSE = 0**.

2. `σ(0) = 0.50`. `σ(2) = 1/(1+e^{-2}) = 1/(1+0.1353) ≈ 0.88`. `σ(-2) = 1 - σ(2) ≈ 0.12`. `σ'(2) = σ(2)(1-σ(2)) ≈ 0.88·0.12 ≈ 0.105`.

3. Accuracy `= (40+130)/200 = 0.85`. Precision `= 40/(40+10) = 0.80`. Recall `= 40/(40+20) ≈ 0.667`. F1 `= 2·(0.80·0.667)/(0.80+0.667) = 2·0.533/1.467 ≈ 0.727`.

4. `XᵀX = [[2,4],[4,10]]`, `Xᵀy = [6, 14]`. `det = 2·10 - 4·4 = 4`, inverse `= (1/4)[[10,-4],[-4,2]]`. `w = (1/4)[[10,-4],[-4,2]]·[6,14] = (1/4)[60-56, -24+28] = (1/4)[4,4] = [1,1]`. So `y = 1 + 1·x`, which passes through `(1,2)` and `(3,4)` exactly. ✓

5. `z = wᵀx = 0.3·2 + 0.1·1 = 0.7`. `p̂ = σ(0.7) = 1/(1+e^{-0.7}) ≈ 1/1.4966 ≈ 0.668`. Gradient `= (p̂-y)x = (0.668-0)·[2,1] = [1.336, 0.668]`. New `w = [0.3,0.1] - 0.5·[1.336,0.668] = [-0.368, -0.234]`. (The model was too confident in class 1 for a class-0 example, so the step pushed `w` down.)

6. **Data leakage** (preprocessing leakage). The scaler's mean/std were computed using the test rows too, so test information bled into the training transform. Every CV fold's "training" data was already informed by the held-out fold, so the CV score is optimistically biased, the model looks better than it will be on truly unseen data. Fix: put the scaler in a `Pipeline` so it's refit on each fold's training portion only.

7. With penalty, `J = (1/2)‖Xw-y‖² + (λ/2)‖w‖²`. Gradient: `Xᵀ(Xw-y) + λw`. Set to zero: `XᵀXw + λw = Xᵀy ⇒ (XᵀX + λI)w = Xᵀy ⇒ w = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy`. Adding `λI` (with `λ>0`) lifts every eigenvalue of `XᵀX` by `λ`, so even when `XᵀX` is singular (eigenvalue 0, e.g. more features than examples or collinear features), `XᵀX + λI` is invertible. Ridge is numerically *and* statistically stabilizing.

8. Model A is useless, 0% recall means it never catches fraud; the 98% accuracy is just the base rate of legitimate transactions. Ship **Model B**: it catches 70% of fraud. Its low precision (30%) means lots of false alarms, acceptable if a flagged transaction goes to cheap human review. The single best summary is the **F1 score** (or, if you can tune the threshold, **AUC**); raw accuracy is actively misleading here.

9. `wᵀAw = Σᵢⱼ wᵢ Aᵢⱼ wⱼ`. Differentiate w.r.t. `w_k`: term contributes when `i=k` (giving `Σⱼ A_{kj} w_j`) or `j=k` (giving `Σᵢ w_i A_{ik}`). So `∂/∂w_k = Σⱼ A_{kj}w_j + Σᵢ A_{ik}w_i = (Aw)_k + (Aᵀw)_k`. Stacking over `k`: `∇ = (A + Aᵀ)w`. For symmetric `A`, `Aᵀ = A`, so `∇ = 2Aw`. ✓

10. With `n ≫ m` (50k features, 500 examples), distance-based methods (**k-NN**) and unregularized linear models fail, distances concentrate (curse of dimensionality), and `XᵀX` is singular so the normal equation has no unique solution; the model can perfectly memorize (huge variance). Reach for: **(1) L1/Lasso or Elastic Net** to select a sparse subset of features; **(2) dimensionality reduction (PCA)** to compress to a few dozen informative directions; **(3) heavy regularization** (Ridge with large `λ`) plus cross-validation. Trees/gradient boosting with regularization are also reasonable since they don't rely on Euclidean distance. The unifying idea: *reduce the effective dimension* before modeling.

## Teach it back

1. **Decompose expected squared error into bias², variance, and irreducible noise. Which term does adding regularization reduce, and what does it cost?** (Walk through inserting `±\bar f` and killing the cross terms. Regularization lowers variance by constraining the model, at the price of more bias, you slide left on the complexity axis.)

2. **Derive the gradient of logistic regression's cross-entropy loss. Why does the final form look identical to linear regression's gradient?** (Chain rule through `J → \hat p → z → w`; the `\hat p(1-\hat p)` terms cancel, leaving `\frac{1}{m}X^\top(\hat p - y)`. Both are GLMs with their natural loss, so the "error × input" form is shared.)

3. **You have a closed-form solution for linear regression. When would you still use gradient descent, and why?** (Normal equation is `O(n³)` to invert `XᵀX` and breaks when `n` is huge or `XᵀX` is singular/ill-conditioned. GD scales, handles streaming data, and generalizes to non-convex models.)

4. **A fraud model reports 99% accuracy. Why might that be worthless, and what would you report instead?** (Base rate is ~99% negatives; predicting "always negative" hits 99% accuracy with zero recall. Report precision/recall/F1 on the positive class, AUC, and the confusion matrix; consider class weights / resampling / threshold tuning.)

5. **Explain geometrically why L1 produces sparse weights but L2 doesn't.** (L1's constraint region is a diamond with corners on the axes; loss contours tend to touch a corner → a coordinate is exactly zero. L2's region is a smooth circle; contours touch an edge → all weights shrink but stay nonzero.)

6. **Derive why PCA's principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.** (Maximize `u^\top \Sigma u` s.t. `\|u\|=1`; the Lagrangian's stationarity condition gives `\Sigma u = \lambda u`. Variance captured equals the eigenvalue, so top eigenvectors = top components.)
