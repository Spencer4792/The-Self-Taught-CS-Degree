## Why structure matters: from idea to company

An idea is not a company. A company is the *container* that holds the idea: it decides who owns what, who is liable when something goes wrong, how money comes in and goes out, who can be hired, and who can invest. Get the container wrong and the best idea in the world leaks.

Three things structure protects:

1. **Liability**, keeping a business problem (a lawsuit, a debt, a breach) from reaching your personal house and bank account.
2. **Ownership**, who owns how much of the company, and what happens when that changes (co-founders, employees, investors).
3. **Optionality**, keeping the door open to the futures you might want (raise money, hire, sell, or just run it profitably forever) without painting yourself into a corner.

The mistake to avoid is treating "the legal stuff" as paperwork to rush through. The structure *is* the strategy made concrete. A two-founder handshake with no vesting and no written equity split is a lawsuit waiting to happen. A sole proprietorship that takes on real customer risk is your personal net worth on the line. The structure is cheap to get right at the start and expensive, sometimes fatal, to fix later.

> **On the job.** Even as an employee, understanding this matters. Your equity grant, your option pool, your vesting schedule, the company's cap table and runway, these decide whether your offer is worth what it looks like. The same literacy that lets you *build* a company lets you *evaluate* one you're joining. Cross-link **15.3** (negotiation), equity is half the negotiation and most people negotiate it blind.

## Legal entity structures

When you "form a company," you're choosing a legal *entity type*. Each is a different trade-off across liability, taxes, and the ability to raise money. Here are the four that matter for software founders in the US.

### Sole proprietorship

The default if you just start doing business as yourself. No formation needed; you *are* the business.

- **Liability:** None. Your personal assets are fully exposed. If a customer sues the "business," they're suing you.
- **Taxes:** Simplest. Profit flows onto your personal tax return (Schedule C in the US). You pay self-employment tax.
- **Raising money:** Effectively impossible. There are no shares to sell.
- **When it fits:** A side project earning a little, with near-zero risk, that you haven't decided to take seriously yet.

### LLC (Limited Liability Company)

A flexible middle ground. A formal entity, but lighter than a corporation.

- **Liability:** Limited, the main reason to form one. Properly maintained, the LLC is a shield between business problems and your personal assets. ("Properly maintained" matters: mixing personal and business money, "commingling", can let a court "pierce the veil" and remove the shield.)
- **Taxes:** Pass-through by default, profits flow to your personal return, avoiding the "double taxation" a C-corp can face. Flexible: an LLC can *elect* to be taxed as an S-corp or C-corp.
- **Raising money:** Workable for revenue/debt financing and small-scale investment, but **awkward for venture capital.** VCs and their funds generally don't want LLC membership interests (pass-through income creates tax headaches for their investors).
- **When it fits:** A bootstrapped product business, a consultancy, a holding company, or a real-estate-style venture, anything not chasing institutional VC. Many profitable software businesses are perfectly happy as LLCs forever.

### C-corporation

A fully separate legal "person." Owned by shareholders, run by a board, managed by officers.

- **Liability:** Limited, like an LLC.
- **Taxes:** **Double taxation** in the classic case, the corporation pays tax on profits, then shareholders pay tax again on dividends. In practice early startups rarely pay dividends (they reinvest), so this hurts less than it sounds, and certain qualified-small-business-stock rules can be very favorable to founders/early investors (ask the accountant; this is a real, specific benefit worth understanding).
- **Raising money:** **Ideal.** Clean shares, preferred stock for investors, a standard stock-option pool for employees. This is the structure the entire VC ecosystem is built around.
- **When it fits:** Any company planning to raise venture capital or eventually go public.

### S-corporation

Not a separate *entity* so much as a *tax election* available to an LLC or corporation that meets the rules.

- **Liability:** Same as the underlying entity (limited).
- **Taxes:** Pass-through (avoids C-corp double taxation) and can reduce self-employment tax for an owner who also takes a salary.
- **Raising money:** **Restrictive.** Hard caps on number/type of shareholders (e.g., must be US persons, limited count, one class of stock). These rules collide head-on with how VC investing works.
- **When it fits:** A profitable small business with a few US owners optimizing their own tax bill, *not* a company about to raise a venture round.

### The comparison at a glance

```
                  Liability     Taxes              Raise VC?     Complexity
Sole prop         NONE          pass-through       no            lowest
LLC               limited       pass-through*      awkward       low
C-corp            limited       double-taxed**     YES           higher
S-corp (election) limited       pass-through       very limited  medium
  * LLC can elect other tax treatment
  ** mitigated in practice; QSBS can be a big founder/investor benefit - ask an accountant
```

### Why VC-backed startups are almost always **Delaware C-corps**

If you're raising venture capital, the answer converges hard on one thing: a **Delaware C-corporation**. Two parts to that.

**Why C-corp:** VCs invest through funds with many limited partners. They need clean **preferred stock** (with the liquidation preferences, protective provisions, and pro-rata rights they negotiate), a standard **option pool** for hiring, and pass-through-free tax treatment for their fund. The C-corp delivers all of that off the shelf. An LLC or S-corp does not.

**Why Delaware:** Most US public companies and VC-backed startups incorporate in Delaware even if they operate elsewhere, because Delaware has (a) a body of corporate case law so deep that outcomes are predictable, (b) the Court of Chancery, judges, not juries, who specialize in corporate disputes, and (c) statutes and norms every VC lawyer already knows. When an investor's term sheet says "Company shall be a Delaware C-corp," they're asking for a structure their lawyers can paper in their sleep. Fighting it just adds legal cost.

> **Common mistakes**
> - **Forming an LLC, getting traction, then trying to raise VC**, now you're paying lawyers to *convert* to a Delaware C-corp mid-stride. If VC is even plausibly the plan, many founders incorporate as a Delaware C-corp from the start. (Talk to a lawyer about *your* odds before deciding, converting later is doable but not free.)
> - **Optimizing the entity for today's tax bill when the goal is a big raise tomorrow.** Different goals, different structures.
> - **Treating "Delaware" as automatically correct.** It's the *default for fundraising*, not automatically right for a bootstrapped product business that will never raise. NYX, as a paid product, could be perfectly fine as an LLC if it never intended to raise, the entity should follow the *plan*.

## Founder equity, vesting, and IP

Before any investor shows up, the founders have to settle ownership among themselves. This is where friendships die if it's done badly.

### Splitting founder equity

There's no formula, but the honest questions are: who had the idea (worth less than people think), who is doing the work *going forward* (worth more than people think), who is taking the risk (quitting a job, putting in money), and who brings what the company can't function without. **Equal-ish splits are common and often healthy**, but the dangerous instinct is to avoid the conversation. A 50/50 split with no decision-making tiebreaker can deadlock a company.

Whatever the split, it gets **vested**.

### Vesting (the single most important founder term)

Vesting means you *earn* your shares over time instead of owning them all on day one.

- **Standard schedule:** 4-year vest with a **1-year cliff.** Nothing vests for the first year; at the 1-year mark, 25% vests at once ("the cliff"); the remaining 75% vests monthly over the next three years.
- **Why it exists:** It protects the company from a co-founder who quits after three months and walks away with 50% of the company forever. With vesting, they leave with 0% (before the cliff) or a small earned slice. Investors *require* founder vesting, but you want it even without investors: it's the mechanism that makes "we're in this together" enforceable.

> **On the job.** Employee option grants use the *same* 4-year / 1-year-cliff shape. When you read an offer, "vesting" tells you how long you must stay to actually earn the equity. Leaving at month 11 = zero equity. This is by design.

### The 83(b) election (US, ask a tax pro)

If you get restricted stock subject to vesting, there's a time-sensitive tax election (the "83(b) election") that generally must be filed with the IRS **within 30 days** of the grant. Filing it can have large tax consequences down the line; *missing* the window can be very costly and is irreversible. Don't wing this: file on time and confirm with your accountant. It's worth flagging here because the deadline is short and the stakes are real.

### IP assignment and founder agreements

The company must actually *own* the code and IP. That sounds obvious and is constantly botched.

- **IP assignment:** Every founder (and later, every employee and contractor) signs an agreement assigning to the company the IP they create for it. Without this, an early contributor could later claim they personally own a chunk of the product. Investors *will* check this in diligence; missing assignments can kill or delay a deal.
- **Founder agreement:** Puts the equity split, vesting, roles, decision-making, and what happens if a founder leaves *in writing, before there's anything to fight over.* The goal is to make the decisions while everyone is still friends.

> Anneava and NYX both live or die on this being clean. A healthcare orchestration platform whose *core IP ownership* is ambiguous is unfundable and unsellable. Clean IP assignment is table stakes.

## Build it: a cap table + dilution model

The **cap table** (capitalization table) is the source of truth for who owns what. Let's build one in plain Python so the mechanics are undeniable, then run a funding round through it and watch dilution happen.

The core idea: **ownership % = your shares ÷ total shares outstanding.** When the company *issues new shares* (to investors or an option pool), the denominator grows, so everyone who didn't get new shares owns a smaller slice. That's dilution. Dilution lowers your *percentage* but, in a good round, raises the *value* of your slice, a smaller piece of a much bigger pie.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

@dataclass
class CapTable:
    # name -> shares
    holders: dict = field(default_factory=dict)

    def total_shares(self):
        return sum(self.holders.values())

    def issue(self, name, shares):
        """Issue new shares to a holder (creates them if new)."""
        self.holders[name] = self.holders.get(name, 0) + shares

    def ownership(self):
        total = self.total_shares()
        return {name: shares / total for name, shares in self.holders.items()}

    def show(self, label=""):
        total = self.total_shares()
        print(f"\n=== {label}  (total shares: {total:,}) ===")
        for name, shares in self.holders.items():
            print(f"  {name:18s} {shares:>12,}   {shares/total:6.2%}")


# --- 1) Founding: two founders split 60/40, 10,000,000 shares ---
cap = CapTable()
cap.issue("Spencer (founder)", 6_000_000)
cap.issue("Co-founder",        4_000_000)
cap.show("At founding")
# Spencer 60.00%, Co-founder 40.00%


# --- 2) Create a 15% (post) option pool for hiring ---
# We want the pool to be 15% of the company AFTER it's added.
# If current shares = S and we add P so that P / (S + P) = 0.15:
#   P = 0.15/0.85 * S
S = cap.total_shares()
pool = round(0.15 / 0.85 * S)
cap.issue("Option pool (unallocated)", pool)
cap.show("After 15% option pool")
# Founders are now diluted to make room for future hires.


# --- 3) Seed round: investor puts in $2,000,000 at a $8,000,000 pre-money valuation ---
pre_money   = 8_000_000
investment  = 2_000_000
post_money  = pre_money + investment          # $10,000,000
investor_pct = investment / post_money        # 20%

# Price per share is set by the PRE-money valuation over EXISTING shares:
existing = cap.total_shares()
price_per_share = pre_money / existing
new_shares = round(investment / price_per_share)
cap.issue("Seed investor", new_shares)
cap.show(f"After seed (${investment:,} at ${pre_money:,} pre)")
print(f"  -> price/share ${price_per_share:.4f}, investor target {investor_pct:.0%}")
```

Running it produces (rounded):

```
=== At founding  (total shares: 10,000,000) ===
  Spencer (founder)     6,000,000   60.00%
  Co-founder            4,000,000   40.00%

=== After 15% option pool  (total shares: 11,764,706) ===
  Spencer (founder)     6,000,000   51.00%
  Co-founder            4,000,000   34.00%
  Option pool           1,764,706   15.00%

=== After seed ($2,000,000 at $8,000,000 pre)  (total: 14,705,882) ===
  Spencer (founder)     6,000,000   40.80%
  Co-founder            4,000,000   27.20%
  Option pool           1,764,706   12.00%
  Seed investor         2,941,176   20.00%
  -> price/share $0.6800, investor target 20%
```

Read the story in the numbers:

- The founder started at **60%**. After carving out a 15% pool and selling 20% to a seed investor, they're at **40.8%**, diluted twice, both on purpose.
- The seed investor gets exactly **20%** because $2M is 20% of the $10M *post-money* valuation. Memorize this: **investor % = investment ÷ post-money.**
- Here's the part that matters: that 40.8% is of a company now worth **$10M**, so the founder's stake is "worth" ~$4.08M on paper. Before the round the 51% was of an ~$8M-pre company → ~$4.08M. The round didn't make them poorer; it kept the paper value roughly flat *and* put $2M in the bank to grow the pie. A *good* raise trades percentage for a bigger absolute pie.

> **The option-pool shuffle.** Notice the pool was created *before* the investor came in, so the founders absorbed that dilution. In real term sheets, investors often require the pool be created (or topped up) from the **pre-money**, meaning founders eat it. Watch where the pool sits relative to the round: it changes who pays for it. (And note the difference between pre-money and **post-money** SAFEs/pools; that boundary is exactly where founders get surprised. Lawyer/accountant for the real doc.)

## Product & customers: finding a real problem

Now the other half, the thing the company is *for*. The number-one cause of startup death isn't competition or running out of money directly; it's **building something nobody wants.** Everything below is machinery to avoid that.

### Customer discovery and validation

Before building, go find the problem and confirm it's real and painful.

- **Talk to potential customers, before building.** The skill is asking about their *current behavior and past pain*, not pitching your idea. "Tell me about the last time you tried to do X. What did you use? What was annoying? What did it cost you?" People will lie to be nice about a hypothetical product; they can't lie about what they did last Tuesday.
- **The "Mom Test" idea:** ask questions even someone's mom couldn't lie about. Past facts, not future hypotheticals. "Would you buy this?" is nearly worthless. "What do you spend on this today, and why?" is gold.
- **Look for pull, not politeness.** Signals of a real problem: people already paying for a bad workaround, hacking spreadsheets together, asking *when can I have it*, or offering to pay before it exists. NYX exists because analytics pain is concrete and people already pay to relieve it.

### MVP (Minimum Viable Product)

The MVP is the *smallest* thing that lets you learn whether the problem and solution are real, not a tiny version of the final product, but the cheapest valid experiment. Sometimes it isn't even code (a landing page, a concierge "we do it manually behind the scenes" service, a Figma clickthrough). The goal is **maximum learning per unit of build.**

### Product-market fit (PMF) - how you'd actually know

PMF is the moment the market starts *pulling* the product out of you instead of you pushing it. It's famously hard to define, but here's how it actually shows up:

- **Retention curves that flatten** instead of decaying to zero, people keep coming back. (This is the single most honest signal.)
- **The "very disappointed" test (Sean Ellis):** survey users, "How would you feel if you could no longer use this?" If **~40%+ say "very disappointed,"** that's a strong PMF signal.
- **Organic pull:** word-of-mouth growth, inbound demand, usage growing faster than your sales effort.
- **It feels different.** Servers fall over, support is overwhelmed, money comes in faster than expected. Pre-PMF, you're dragging the rock uphill; post-PMF, you're running to keep up.

> **Common mistakes**
> - **Building for months before talking to a single customer.** The most expensive way to learn.
> - **Confusing being busy with PMF.** Lots of meetings and a few pilots ≠ a market pulling.
> - **Vanity metrics.** Signups and pageviews feel good; *retention and revenue* tell the truth.

## Pricing models and unit economics

### Pricing models

- **Subscription / SaaS:** Recurring fee (monthly/annual) for access. Predictable revenue (MRR/ARR), the backbone of most software businesses. **NYX as a paid analytics product fits here**, a tiered monthly/annual subscription.
- **Usage-based:** Pay per unit consumed (API calls, GB, compute). Aligns price with value and scales with the customer; revenue is less predictable. (Many infra/AI products do this, cross-link the MLOps/serving cost discussion in **Part 14**.)
- **Freemium:** A free tier to drive adoption, paid tiers for power features. Great for top-of-funnel, dangerous if the free tier is *too* good (you carry cost for users who never convert). Conversion rates are often low single-digit percentages.
- **Per-seat:** Price per user. Simple; aligns with team size. Common in B2B.
- **Hybrid:** e.g., a base subscription plus usage overages, common and powerful.

The strategic move is to **price on value, not cost.** The question isn't "what did it cost to run" but "how much value does this create for the customer, and what fraction can you capture?" Most early founders price *way* too low out of fear.

### Unit economics - the numbers that decide if the business works

Unit economics ask: does *one customer* make money? If a single customer is unprofitable, scaling just loses money faster. The core metrics, with worked numbers for a hypothetical SaaS:

**Gross margin**, revenue left after the *direct* cost of delivering the service (hosting, third-party APIs, support directly tied to serving). Software targets **70-90%** gross margin; that high margin is *why* software is a great business.

```
Customer pays:                 $100 / month
Cost to serve (hosting, etc.):  $15 / month
Gross profit:                   $85 / month
Gross margin:                   85%   ← strong, software-like
```

**CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)**, fully loaded cost to acquire one customer (ads + sales salaries + marketing) divided by customers acquired.

```
Spent on sales & marketing in Q:  $50,000
New customers acquired:                100
CAC:                              $500 / customer
```

**Churn**, the rate customers leave per period. If 5% of customers cancel each month, monthly churn = 5%. Its inverse is **average lifetime ≈ 1 / churn**.

```
Monthly churn:        5%
Avg customer lifetime: 1 / 0.05 = 20 months
```

**LTV (Lifetime Value)**, total *gross profit* one customer generates over their lifetime.

```
LTV = monthly gross profit × lifetime
    = $85 × 20 months
    = $1,700 per customer
```

**The two ratios that matter:**

```
LTV : CAC  =  $1,700 : $500  =  3.4 : 1     ← target ≥ 3:1 (healthy)
CAC payback =  CAC / monthly gross profit
            =  $500 / $85  ≈  5.9 months      ← target < 12 months
```

- **LTV:CAC ≥ 3:1** means each customer returns at least 3× what they cost to acquire. Below 1:1 means you literally lose money on every customer, scaling is suicide. Way above 3:1 can mean you're *under*-investing in growth.
- **CAC payback < 12 months** means you get your acquisition money back within a year, so you can recycle it into more growth without bleeding cash. ~6 months here is excellent.

> **On the job.** These five numbers, gross margin, CAC, LTV, churn, payback, are the vocabulary of every board meeting and investor update. If you can recite your company's actual figures and explain why they're moving, you sound like an operator, not a hobbyist. For NYX specifically, the lever to watch hardest is **churn**, because in subscription software churn quietly destroys LTV and nothing scales on top of a leaky bucket.

> Compute LTV on **gross profit**, not revenue. LTV on revenue overstates the truth by ignoring cost-to-serve, a classic way founders fool themselves.

### The Business Model Canvas

Use it to pressure-test coherence: do the pieces *fit*? A high-touch enterprise sales channel paired with a $10/month price doesn't add up, the channel costs more than the revenue. The canvas makes those contradictions visible.

### Moats (defensibility)

A moat is *why a competitor with money can't just copy you.* Common ones:

- **Network effects**, the product gets more valuable as more people use it (marketplaces, social, payment networks). The strongest moat in software.
- **Switching costs**, once a customer's data, workflows, and integrations live in your product, leaving is painful. (Anneava, as an *orchestration* layer wired into a hospital's workflow, accrues exactly this.)
- **Economies of scale**, costs drop as you grow, so you can price below new entrants.
- **Brand / trust**, especially in regulated spaces (healthcare, finance) where trust *is* the product.
- **Proprietary data**, a data flywheel competitors can't replicate (ties to ML moats in **Part 14**).
- **Regulatory / licensing**, being on the right side of compliance is itself a barrier (more below).

"First mover" alone is *not* a moat. Being early only matters if you convert it into one of the above before fast followers catch up.

### Market sizing: TAM / SAM / SOM (worked example)

Investors and founders both need to know *how big this could get.* Three nested circles:

- **TAM (Total Addressable Market):** total spend if *everyone* who could ever use this bought it.
- **SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market):** the slice your product/geography/segment can actually serve.
- **SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market):** the slice you can realistically capture in the medium term.

Worked example, a SaaS analytics product like NYX, sold to mid-market finance teams in the US:

```
TAM: 200,000 US companies with a finance team that could use analytics
     × $6,000/yr average contract
     = $1.2 BILLION    ← total addressable

SAM: of those, 40,000 are mid-market (my actual sweet spot)
     × $6,000/yr
     = $240 MILLION    ← serviceable addressable

SOM: realistically capture 3% of SAM in ~3 years
     = 1,200 customers × $6,000
     = $7.2 MILLION ARR ← serviceable obtainable (the near-term prize)
```

**Always build market size bottom-up** (count of buyers × price), never the lazy top-down "it's a $50B market and you just need 1%." The "just 1%" pitch screams that you haven't thought about *how* you'd actually reach customers. The bottom-up number is defensible because every input is a number you can argue.

### Competition

"No competitors" is a **red flag**, not a selling point; it usually means either no market or you haven't looked. Map competitors (including the *status quo*: spreadsheets, doing nothing, an internal tool) and articulate why you win for a *specific* segment. You don't need to be better at everything; you need to be clearly better for *someone*.

## Funding: bootstrapping vs venture, and the ladder

### Two fundamentally different games

- **Bootstrapping**, fund the company from revenue and savings. You keep control and all the equity; you grow only as fast as cash allows; success can mean a profitable, self-owned business. Many great software companies never raised a dime. A profitable bootstrapped NYX is a completely legitimate, often *better*, outcome than a venture rocket.
- **Venture capital**, sell equity to investors for cash to grow *faster than revenue allows.* You trade ownership and control for fuel and acceleration. VC math demands **outsized outcomes**, funds need a few huge wins to return the whole fund, so a VC-backed company that becomes a stable $5M/yr business is, to the VC, a *failure*. That misalignment is the thing to understand before taking the money. VC is a tool for a *specific* kind of company (big market, winner-take-most dynamics), not a trophy.

### The funding ladder

```
Pre-seed   $100k-$1M     idea + early team; friends/family, angels, accelerators
Seed       $1M-$5M       early traction / early PMF signals; seed funds, angels
Series A   $5M-$20M      proven PMF + a repeatable growth engine; institutional VCs
Series B   $20M-$50M     scaling what works; bigger funds
Series C+  $50M+         dominate the market; growth equity, late-stage funds
(Exit)     IPO or acquisition (M&A) - investors and founders get liquidity
```

Each rung answers a different question: pre-seed buys belief, seed buys *evidence the dog will eat the dog food*, Series A buys *proof you can pour money in and get growth out repeatably.* Raising the next round means hitting the milestones that de-risk the *next* question.

### SAFEs vs priced rounds

- **Priced round (equity round):** investors buy shares at an agreed valuation *now*. Sets a price per share, issues preferred stock, full legal docs. More expensive and slower; standard from Series A on.
- **SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity):** an investor gives money now in exchange for the right to shares *later* (at the next priced round), without setting a valuation today. Faster, cheaper, founder-friendly for early rounds, the standard for pre-seed/seed.
  - **Valuation cap:** the maximum valuation at which the SAFE converts, protects the early investor's upside if the next round is priced high.
  - **Discount:** a percentage discount on the next round's price, rewards the early investor for going first.
  - SAFEs feel "free" because there's no dilution *today*, but they all convert at the next round at once. Stack several SAFEs with low caps and you can wake up far more diluted than you expected. **Post-money** vs **pre-money** SAFEs allocate dilution very differently. Model the conversion *before* signing. (Lawyer for the actual instrument.)

### What investors look for

Roughly, in order: **team** (can these people execute?), **market** (is it big enough to matter?), **traction** (is something already working?), **product**, and a credible **why now.** At pre-seed it's mostly team + market + insight; by Series A it's traction and unit economics carrying the weight.

### Alternatives (especially relevant for Anneava)

- **Revenue-based financing:** repay from a percentage of revenue; non-dilutive, good for businesses with steady revenue, costs more than a bank loan.
- **Grants:** non-dilutive money (e.g., research/health-focused grants like SBIR/STTR in the US). Slow and paperwork-heavy, but free equity-wise, and **healthcare ventures like Anneava** often have grant avenues that pure-software consumer apps don't. Worth investigating before defaulting to VC.
- **Venture debt:** loans for already-VC-backed companies to extend runway without selling more equity.
- **Customer/contract financing:** in B2B/healthcare, a large customer pre-paying an annual contract is the cheapest capital there is, non-dilutive and it *validates* the product simultaneously.

> The healthcare context for Anneava actually *widens* the funding menu, grants, strategic health-system investors, and contract pre-payment, beyond the consumer-app default of "raise from VCs." The regulated nature that makes it harder to build also makes it more defensible and opens funding doors.

## Build it: a runway/burn calculator

**Runway** is the single most important number for a startup's survival: *how many months until the money runs out.* The formula is brutally simple, and not knowing it is how founders get blindsided.

```
Net burn   = cash going out − cash coming in   (per month)
Runway     = cash in the bank / net burn       (months)
```

Let's build a calculator that also projects the bank balance month by month, since burn and revenue usually *change*.

```python
def runway(starting_cash, monthly_revenue, monthly_costs,
           revenue_growth=0.0, cost_growth=0.0, max_months=60):
    """
    Project cash month by month until it runs out.
    revenue_growth / cost_growth are monthly growth rates (e.g. 0.10 = +10%/mo).
    Returns (runway_months, schedule).
    """
    cash = starting_cash
    rev, cost = monthly_revenue, monthly_costs
    schedule = []
    for month in range(1, max_months + 1):
        net_burn = cost - rev                      # >0 means losing money
        cash -= net_burn
        schedule.append((month, round(rev), round(cost),
                         round(net_burn), round(cash)))
        if cash <= 0:
            return month, schedule                 # ran out THIS month
        rev  *= (1 + revenue_growth)
        cost *= (1 + cost_growth)
    return None, schedule                          # survived the horizon (default-alive)


# Scenario: $1,000,000 in the bank. Revenue $40k/mo growing 8%/mo.
# Costs $140k/mo growing 3%/mo (hiring).
months, sched = runway(1_000_000, 40_000, 140_000,
                       revenue_growth=0.08, cost_growth=0.03)

print("Mo |   Revenue |     Costs |  Net burn |       Cash")
for m, rev, cost, burn, cash in sched[:12]:
    print(f"{m:2d} | {rev:>9,} | {cost:>9,} | {burn:>9,} | {cash:>10,}")
print("Runway:", "default-alive (survived horizon)" if months is None
      else f"{months} months")
```

Output (first 12 months):

```

 1 |    40,000 |   140,000 |   100,000 |    900,000
 2 |    43,200 |   144,200 |   101,000 |    799,000
 3 |    46,656 |   148,526 |   101,870 |    697,130
 4 |    50,388 |   152,982 |   102,593 |    594,537
 5 |    54,420 |   157,571 |   103,152 |    491,385
 6 |    58,773 |   162,298 |   103,525 |    387,860
 7 |    63,475 |   167,167 |   103,692 |    284,167
 8 |    68,553 |   172,182 |   103,629 |    180,538
 9 |    74,037 |   177,348 |   103,311 |     77,227
10 |    79,960 |   182,668 |   102,708 |    -25,481
Runway: 10 months
```

What this tells you, and how you'd *act* on it:

- **~10 months of runway.** The fundraising rule of thumb is to start raising with **6+ months left**, because a raise takes months and a cash-out-while-raising is fatal. So you'd start the next raise around month 4, not month 9.
- **Net burn barely shrinks** even though revenue grows 8%/mo, because costs grow too. This is the trap: hiring "to keep up with growth" can keep burn flat. Slowing cost growth is the fastest lever on runway.
- **"Default-alive vs default-dead"** (Paul Graham's framing): if current growth makes you profitable *before* the cash runs out, you're default-alive and in control. Default-dead means you *must* raise. Knowing which one you are changes every decision. Run this model both ways: "what cost-growth rate makes you default-alive?"

> **Common mistakes**
> - **Not knowing the runway number cold.** You should be able to say it to the day.
> - **Treating a signed contract as cash.** Revenue ≠ cash collected. A 12-month deal billed annually is cash *now*; billed monthly, it's cash *later*. Runway is about **cash in the bank**, not bookings.
> - **Forgetting that raising takes months.** Runway must cover the *raise itself.*

## Reading a basic financial model

A startup financial model is just three connected views. You don't need to be a CFO, but you must read these fluently.

- **P&L (Profit & Loss / Income Statement):** Revenue − Costs = Profit/Loss, over a period. Top line (revenue) down to bottom line (net income). Shows *profitability*.
- **Balance Sheet:** A snapshot at a point in time. **Assets = Liabilities + Equity.** What you own vs. what you owe. Shows *financial position.*
- **Cash Flow Statement:** Actual cash in and out over a period (operating, investing, financing). Shows *liquidity*, and is what runway is really about. **A profitable company can still die if it runs out of cash** (e.g., money tied up in receivables), which is exactly why cash flow is its own statement.

The trap a software person must internalize: **profit ≠ cash.** A company can "book" $1M in annual revenue, recognize it monthly on the P&L, but only have collected $200k in cash, and runway only cares about the cash. Keep the P&L story and the cash story separate in your head.

## Organization & people

### Org structures as the company grows

```
Stage 0  (1-10): FOUNDER-LED. Everyone does everything. No org chart needed; the
                 org chart is "us, in a room." Speed over structure.

Stage 1  (10-30): FUNCTIONAL TEAMS. People specialize: Engineering, Product,
                 Sales/GTM, Operations. First managers appear. Process starts to matter.

Stage 2  (30-150): DEPARTMENTS + LAYERS. Functions get sub-teams and directors;
                 communication overhead becomes a real cost; you formalize culture,
                 levels, and process because you can't rely on everyone knowing everyone.

Stage 3  (150+):  DIVISIONS / business units, multiple product lines. (Beyond the
                 scope of a founder's first few years, but the trajectory.)
```

The mistake is adding structure too *early* (bureaucracy kills a 6-person startup) or too *late* (chaos at 40 people with no managers). Structure should *follow* pain, not precede it.

### Roles (and what each actually does)

- **Engineering**, builds and runs the product. (The whole rest of this book.)
- **Product (PM)**, decides *what* to build and *why*; owns the roadmap and prioritization; the voice of the customer inside the building.
- **Design (UX/UI)**, how the product looks and *feels*; often the difference between adopted and abandoned.
- **Sales**, in B2B, turns interest into signed revenue (less central in pure self-serve).
- **Marketing / Growth**, creates awareness and demand; feeds the top of the funnel.
- **Operations / Finance / People**, keeps the machine running: payroll, legal, hiring, books, compliance.
- **Customer Success / Support**, keeps customers alive and *retained* (directly fights churn → protects LTV).

### Hiring: when, who first, and the cost of a bad hire

- **Hire to relieve the bottleneck**, not to look like a "real company." The first hire should be whatever is *most constraining*: if you're drowning in sales, it's a salesperson; if the product can't keep up, it's an engineer.
- **First hires are generalists.** Early employees wear many hats; specialists come later when the role is full-time's worth of one thing.
- **The cost of a bad hire is brutal** at small scale: salary, the time to hire and onboard, the morale hit, the customers/code they touch, *and* the opportunity cost of the right person you didn't hire. At a 5-person company, one bad hire is 20% of the company. Hire slow, fire fast (humanely) is a cliché because it's true.

> **Common mistake:** hiring to *delegate a problem you don't understand yet.* If you can't define what good looks like for a role, you'll hire badly and won't be able to tell. Founders should understand a function before delegating it.

### Culture and values

Culture is **what actually gets rewarded and tolerated**, not a poster. It's set by what founders *do*, who gets promoted, what behavior is praised, what's let slide. Writing down 3-5 real values early helps *only* if they're enforced in decisions (especially hiring and firing). In a regulated business like Anneava, a culture that takes compliance and trust seriously isn't decoration: it's existential.

### Equity comp for employees (the option pool)

To attract talent without burning cash, startups grant **stock options** from the **option pool** (the ~10-20% carved out in the cap table). An option is the *right to buy* shares later at a fixed "strike" price; if the company grows, that's valuable. Same **4-year vest / 1-year cliff** as founders. Equity stretches early salaries *and* aligns the team to the company's success, but it's only motivating if employees understand what they have, so transparency about the grant (and a refresher on vesting) matters.

### Remote vs in-person

A real strategic choice. **In-person** maximizes bandwidth, mentorship, and serendipity, valuable for a tiny founding team finding its footing. **Remote** widens the talent pool enormously and cuts cost, but demands deliberate written communication and async process. **Hybrid** tries to split the difference. There's no universally right answer; the wrong move is *defaulting* without deciding, then being surprised the culture didn't form.

## Execution: how software teams actually run

### Agile, Scrum, Kanban

**Agile** is the *philosophy*: ship small increments, get feedback, adapt, rather than a giant up-front plan. (Cross-link **Part 4** on iterative development and **Part 10** on CI/CD, which is the technical enabler of shipping continuously.) Two common flavors:

**Scrum**, work in fixed **sprints** (usually 1-2 weeks):
- **Backlog**, the prioritized list of everything that *could* be built. The PM owns its order.
- **Sprint planning**, pick what the team commits to this sprint from the top of the backlog.
- **Daily standup**, short (~15 min) sync: what you did, what you're doing, what's blocking you. *Surfacing blockers* is the real point, not status theater.
- **Sprint review / demo**, show what shipped to stakeholders.
- **Retrospective ("retro")**, the team reflects: what went well, what didn't, what we'll change. **The single highest-use meeting**: it's how the team improves itself.

**Kanban**, continuous flow instead of sprints. A board (`To Do → In Progress → Done`) with **WIP limits** (work-in-progress caps) to stop everyone half-finishing ten things. Great for support/ops and interrupt-driven work where fixed sprints don't fit.

There's no holy version. Small teams should take the useful parts (a prioritized backlog, a quick daily sync, a regular retro) and skip the ceremony that doesn't earn its time. *Process should serve shipping, not the reverse.*

### Product management & prioritization (RICE)

A PM's hardest job is **saying no**, there are always 100 things to build and capacity for 5. **RICE** scores each idea to prioritize objectively:

```
RICE = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort

Reach      - how many users/events affected per period
Impact     - how much it moves the goal (e.g., 3 = massive, 1 = medium, 0.25 = tiny)
Confidence - how sure we are (100% / 80% / 50%)
Effort     - person-months (the denominator: cheap things float up)
```

Worked example, ranking three features:

```
Feature            Reach  Impact  Conf   Effort   RICE = (R×I×C)/E
A) SSO login        500    1.0     80%    2        (500×1×0.8)/2   = 200
B) New dashboard   2000    2.0     50%    4        (2000×2×0.5)/4  = 500   ← top
C) Dark mode       1000    0.25    100%   1        (1000×0.25×1)/1 = 250
```

So you'd build **B, then C, then A**, even though dark mode "felt small," its cheap effort and certainty rank it above an expensive, uncertain SSO project. RICE doesn't make the decision *for* you; it makes the *trade-offs explicit and arguable*, which is what makes it useful. Other frameworks (MoSCoW, value-vs-effort 2×2, the Kano model) do the same job at different resolutions.

### Roadmaps

A roadmap communicates *direction and sequence*, not a dated promise carved in stone. The useful form is **Now / Next / Later** (what we're building now, what's queued, what's on the horizon), it sets direction without lying about precise dates the future can't honor.

### Engineering management basics

When you manage engineers, the job stops being "write the most code" and becomes "make the team productive": unblock people, set clear priorities, run good code-review culture, protect focus time, grow people, and translate between business goals and technical work. The classic first-time-manager trap is staying the best individual contributor and starving the actual management work. (Cross-link **15.3/15.4**, the IC→manager transition is a real career fork.)

### OKRs and goal-setting

**OKRs (Objectives & Key Results)** turn strategy into measurable goals:
- **Objective**, a qualitative, inspiring goal ("Make NYX the obvious choice for mid-market finance teams").
- **Key Results**, 3-5 *measurable* outcomes that prove it ("Grow paying customers 100→300," "Cut churn from 5%→3%," "Reach $1M ARR").

KRs must be **outcomes, not tasks** ("reduce churn to 3%," not "ship a churn dashboard"). Set quarterly, reviewed regularly. They keep a growing team rowing the same direction without you micromanaging.

### Metrics / KPIs and decision-making

Pick a small set of metrics that actually reflect the business (MRR/ARR, churn, CAC, payback, active users, gross margin) and watch *trends*, not vanity spikes. A useful idea is a **"North Star metric"**, the one number that best captures delivered customer value (e.g., for NYX maybe "weekly active analysts running reports"). For decisions: distinguish **reversible "two-way doors"** (decide fast, you can undo it) from **irreversible "one-way doors"** (entity choice, a key hire, a fundraise, slow down, get advice). Matching decision *speed* to *reversibility* is one of the highest-use founder habits.

## Operations & risk

### Accounting literacy (usable level)

Revisit the three statements above (P&L, balance sheet, cash flow). At a founder's working level you need to: keep clean books from day one (a bookkeeper/tool early is cheap insurance), know your **monthly burn and runway** cold, separate **bookings vs revenue vs cash**, and read a P&L well enough to see where money actually goes. You do *not* need to do your own taxes; that's what the accountant is for, but you need to understand the outputs enough to make decisions and not get surprised.

### Contracts & compliance overview

- **Customer contracts / Terms of Service / SLAs**, define what you're promising and what happens if you don't deliver. (Have a lawyer template these; don't copy-paste a random ToS.)
- **Privacy law**, depends entirely on *what data* you touch and *where your users are*:
  - **GDPR** (EU), strict rules on personal data: consent, data subject rights, breach notification, real penalties. Applies based on where *users* are, not where you are.
  - **CCPA/CPRA** (California) and a growing patchwork of US state laws, similar themes.
  - **HIPAA** (US healthcare), governs protected health information (PHI). **This is squarely Anneava's world.** Touching PHI triggers serious obligations (safeguards, Business Associate Agreements with partners, breach rules). Never wing HIPAA: it's specialized legal/compliance territory and the penalties are severe.
- **SOC 2**, not a law but a widely-expected **security attestation**. A SOC 2 report (an auditor verifies your security controls) is often the price of entry to sell to enterprises, *especially* in healthcare/finance. Buyers ask "are you SOC 2?" the way they ask "do you have an API?" Building toward it early (access controls, logging, change management, cross-link **Part 12 Security** and **Part 13 Observability**) makes the eventual audit far cheaper.

### Insurance

Boring and necessary. **General liability**, **professional liability / E&O** (errors & omissions, if your software causes a customer loss), **cyber insurance** (breach response), and **D&O** (directors & officers, investors often require it). A lawyer/broker right-sizes this; the point is it exists and you shouldn't operate naked on risk.

### The regulatory-awareness mindset (why Anneava routes payments through a licensed facilitator)

This is the most important mental model in the whole "risk" section, and Anneava is the perfect case study.

Moving money on behalf of others is heavily regulated. In the US, a business that transmits money for others can be classified as a **Money Services Business / money transmitter**, which triggers federal registration *and* a punishing patchwork of state-by-state money transmitter licenses, expensive, slow, and ongoing. Becoming a money transmitter accidentally is a genuine existential risk: it can mean operating illegally.

So Anneava **routes payments through a licensed payment facilitator** rather than touching the money flow as a transmitter itself. The facilitator already holds the licenses and bears that regulatory load; Anneava *orchestrates* the healthcare workflow and lets the licensed party move the funds. The result: you get the functionality (payments happen) without taking on the money-transmitter classification and its licensing burden. The takeaway: architect the business so the regulated activity sits with the party licensed to do it.

The transferable mindset, which you apply everywhere:

1. **Ask "what activity am I actually doing, legally?"**, not what you *call* it. Moving money, storing health data, giving financial advice, handling securities, each is a *regulated activity* with its own rulebook, regardless of your marketing language.
2. **Find out which classification you'd fall into** *before* building, with a lawyer who knows that domain.
3. **Architect to avoid or properly handle the burden**, partner with a licensed entity (facilitator, bank, broker-dealer), get the license, or design the product so the regulated activity sits elsewhere. This is a *design decision*, made early, exactly like a systems-architecture decision.
4. **Compliance is a feature and a moat**, not just a cost. In regulated markets, being demonstrably compliant is *why* serious customers (hospitals, banks) will trust you, and *why* a careless competitor can't just copy you.

> **Common mistake:** building the product first and asking "are we allowed to do this?" second. In regulated domains (health, payments, finance) that order can be fatal, you might have to rebuild or shut down. **Regulatory questions are architecture questions; ask them in the design phase.** And, saying it once more, this paragraph is *awareness*, not legal advice. The actual classification and structure for Anneava is a question for qualified counsel.

## Go-to-market (GTM)

Building the product is half the company; *getting it to customers* is the other half, and the half engineers neglect.

### Positioning

Positioning is the *one clear sentence* a customer can repeat: **for [target customer] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit], unlike [alternative].** If you can't fill that in crisply, the product isn't positioned. It's just features. Positioning is a *choice*, and choosing a narrow, specific target beats trying to be for everyone.

### Channels & motion: sales vs self-serve

The **GTM motion** must match the price and complexity of the product:

```
Self-serve / PLG     Low price, simple product. User signs up, tries, pays - no human.
(product-led growth) Needs a frictionless product + free trial/freemium. Scales cheaply.
                     (Fits a lower-priced NYX tier.)

Inside sales         Mid price ($5k-$50k/yr). A rep demos, follows up, closes remotely.
                     Needs marketing to feed leads.

Field / enterprise   High price ($50k+/yr), complex/regulated buyers. Long cycles,
sales                multiple stakeholders, custom contracts, security reviews (SOC 2!).
                     (Fits Anneava selling into hospitals.)
```

The classic error is a **mismatch**, trying to sell a $20/month product with an expensive field sales team (the economics can't work), or trying to sell a $200k regulated platform purely self-serve (the buyer needs a human and a security review). The motion is dictated by the price and the buyer.

### Content & growth basics

- **Content/inbound**, be useful publicly (writing, docs, tools, SEO) so customers find you. Compounds slowly but cheaply; great for developer/technical products.
- **Paid acquisition**, buy attention (ads). Fast and measurable, but only sane if **LTV > CAC** with room to spare (back to unit economics).
- **Referrals / word-of-mouth**, the cheapest and best channel; a real signal of PMF; can be nudged with referral mechanics.
- **Partnerships / channel**, sell *through* others who already reach your customers (huge in healthcare, health systems, EHR vendors, existing platforms).

You don't need every channel. You need **one or two that work** and to double down. Most companies have a single dominant channel; finding it is the early GTM job.

A startup has 8,000,000 shares outstanding. An investor invests **$1,500,000** at a **$6,000,000 pre-money** valuation. (a) What % does the investor own? (b) What's the price per share? (c) How many new shares are issued? (d) If a founder held 50% before, what do they hold after?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

- Post-money = pre + investment = $6M + $1.5M = **$7.5M**.
- (a) Investor % = investment / post = 1.5 / 7.5 = **20%**.
- (b) Price per share = pre-money / existing shares = $6,000,000 / 8,000,000 = **$0.75/share**.
- (c) New shares = investment / price = $1,500,000 / $0.75 = **2,000,000 shares**.
- (d) New total = 8,000,000 + 2,000,000 = 10,000,000. Founder had 50% = 4,000,000 shares (unchanged), now 4,000,000 / 10,000,000 = **40%**. They were diluted from 50% → 40%, exactly the 20% they "gave up" applied to their stake (50% × 0.8 = 40%). Every existing holder is diluted by the same 20% factor.
</details>

**Exercise 2, Unit economics health check.**
A SaaS product charges **$50/mo**, costs **$10/mo** to serve, has **4% monthly churn**, and CAC is **$600**. Compute gross margin, lifetime, LTV, LTV:CAC, and CAC payback. Is this business healthy? What's the *biggest* lever to improve it?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

- Gross profit/mo = $50 − $10 = **$40**. Gross margin = 40/50 = **80%** (strong).
- Lifetime = 1 / churn = 1 / 0.04 = **25 months**.
- LTV = gross profit × lifetime = $40 × 25 = **$1,000**.
- LTV:CAC = 1,000 / 600 = **1.67:1**, *below the 3:1 target.* Not healthy yet.
- CAC payback = CAC / gross profit/mo = 600 / 40 = **15 months**, *above the 12-month target.*
- Biggest lever: **churn.** Cutting churn from 4% → 2% doubles lifetime to 50 months → LTV = $2,000 → LTV:CAC = 3.3:1 (healthy) with no change in price or CAC. Retention is the highest-use number in subscription software, fix the leaky bucket before pouring in more acquisition spend.
</details>

**Exercise 3, Runway and the fundraise trigger.**
You have **$600,000** in the bank, burning a *constant* **net** $75,000/month (assume flat for simplicity). (a) What's your runway? (b) If raising takes ~4 months and you want a 2-month safety buffer, in which month must you start? (c) If you cut net burn to $50,000/month, what's the new runway?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

- (a) Runway = cash / net burn = 600,000 / 75,000 = **8 months**.
- (b) You need to *close* a raise with ≥2 months left → by month 6. A raise takes ~4 months → start by month **6 − 4 = month 2.** Practically: you'd start almost immediately. (This is why "raise with 6+ months left" is the rule, 8 months of runway is *already tight* once you subtract the raise duration.)
- (c) New runway = 600,000 / 50,000 = **12 months.** Cutting burn by a third bought 4 extra months, often cheaper and faster than raising, and it strengthens the eventual raise.
</details>

**Exercise 4, RICE prioritization.**
Score and rank these three features. Reach in users/quarter, Impact on the {3, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25} scale, Confidence as a %, Effort in person-months.

```
Feature             Reach   Impact   Conf    Effort
API v2              800     2.0      80%     5
Onboarding redesign 3000    1.0      100%    2
Enterprise SSO      200     3.0      50%     3
```

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

RICE = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort:

- **API v2:** (800 × 2.0 × 0.8) / 5 = 1280 / 5 = **256**
- **Onboarding redesign:** (3000 × 1.0 × 1.0) / 2 = 3000 / 2 = **1500**
- **Enterprise SSO:** (200 × 3.0 × 0.5) / 3 = 300 / 3 = **100**

Rank: **1) Onboarding redesign (1500) → 2) API v2 (256) → 3) Enterprise SSO (100).** The onboarding redesign wins decisively, huge reach, full confidence, low effort, even though its per-user impact is "only" medium. SSO, despite high impact, ranks last because it reaches few users, you're only 50% confident, and it's expensive. RICE made the trade-off explicit instead of letting the loudest customer's SSO request jump the queue.
</details>

**Exercise 5, Entity choice.**
For each, pick the most likely entity and one sentence of reasoning: (a) a solo side project earning $300/mo with near-zero risk; (b) a bootstrapped, profitable consultancy with two US partners who want to minimize taxes and never raise; (c) a startup planning to raise a seed round from VCs next year.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

- (a) **Sole proprietorship**, simplest, near-zero risk, not worth formation overhead *yet* (revisit if it grows or risk rises).
- (b) **LLC, possibly with an S-corp tax election**, limited liability + pass-through taxation, and the S-corp election can reduce self-employment tax for active owners. Fits a small, profitable, non-fundraising business with a couple of US owners. (Confirm with an accountant, the S-corp math is fact-specific.)
- (c) **Delaware C-corp**, what VCs require: clean preferred stock, a standard option pool, and Delaware's predictable corporate law. Incorporating this way *from the start* avoids a costly conversion later.

(As always: these are general-education answers; the right choice for a *real* situation needs a lawyer/accountant.)
</details>

**Exercise 6, The regulatory mindset (Anneava-style).**
A new fintech idea lets users send each other money inside an app, with the startup holding balances and moving funds between users. (a) What regulatory classification is the danger here? (b) Name two architectural ways to avoid carrying that burden directly. (c) Why is this an *architecture* decision rather than a "deal with it later" problem?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

- (a) Holding balances and moving money for others risks being classified as a **Money Services Business / money transmitter** in the US, triggering federal registration and **state-by-state money-transmitter licensing**, which is expensive, slow, and ongoing. Operating without required licenses can be illegal.
- (b) Two patterns: (1) **partner with a licensed entity**, route funds through a licensed payment facilitator or a bank-as-a-service / sponsor bank so the regulated party holds the licenses and custody (this is exactly Anneava's approach); (2) **redesign so you never take custody / never transmit**, e.g., direct bank-to-bank rails where the regulated processor moves the money and you only orchestrate.
- (c) Because *unwinding* it later can mean rebuilding the product or shutting down, the classification depends on how money *flows through the architecture*, which is baked in early. In regulated domains, the legal question *is* a design question, so you ask it during design, with counsel, not after launch.

(Education only, the actual classification and structure require qualified legal counsel and vary by jurisdiction.)
</details>
