## Start here

Before any formalism, here's the whole subject as one stubborn truth and three everyday pictures. Hold onto these, every theorem below collapses back into them.

**The stubborn truth: in a single program, you have a god's-eye view; in a distributed system, nobody does.** When your code runs on one machine, you can read any variable, you know exactly what time it is, and when a function call fails you *know* it failed. Across machines, all three of those disappear. You can only learn about another machine by *sending it a message and waiting*, and the wait might never end, because you cannot tell "it's slow" apart from "it's dead." That single inability, **you cannot distinguish a slow process from a crashed one**, is the seed from which nearly every hard problem in this field grows. Tattoo it on your brain.

**Picture one: the long-distance relationship.** You and a friend on opposite coasts can only communicate by mailing letters. Letters take an unpredictable number of days, sometimes arrive out of order, and occasionally get lost. Now try to agree on *exactly* what restaurant to meet at, when neither of you can see the other and a letter you sent might be sitting in a truck. You write "let's do Italian"; before it arrives, they write "let's do sushi." Whose choice wins? You can't even tell whether your letter *crossed* theirs in transit. That's distributed agreement, and it's hard for a reason that has nothing to do with bad code: **information takes time to travel, and you can't observe the other side directly.**

**Picture two: the office with no clock on the wall.** Imagine an office where everyone has their own wristwatch, and every watch drifts, some run fast, some slow, none agree to the second. Now someone asks, "Did the bug report come in *before* or *after* the fix was deployed?" You can't just compare two wristwatch readings, because the watches lie. What you *can* do is track *cause and effect*: if the deploy was a reply to an email that quoted the bug report, then the bug report definitely came first, not because of any clock, but because of the *chain of messages*. That shift, from "what time did it happen?" to "what caused what?", is the single most important conceptual move in this chapter, and it's exactly what **logical clocks** capture.

**Picture three: the photocopied ledger across branches (callback to 7.4).** Five bank branches each keep a copy of the same ledger. A correction made at one branch takes time to courier to the others. In that gap the copies *disagree*. Do you (a) refuse all reads until every branch is in sync, correct but sometimes unavailable, or (b) answer from whatever branch is nearest and risk showing stale data, always available but sometimes wrong? You cannot have both when the couriers are stuck (a network partition). **That forced choice is the CAP theorem**, and it's not a design flaw you can engineer away, it's a law.

**Why this matters for a tech job.** This is the deep end, and it's where senior and staff engineers are *made*. Anyone can wire two services together; the engineer who can answer "what happens when the network between them partitions?" or "could these two events have happened concurrently, and does that cause a bug?" is the one trusted with the architecture. In system-design interviews, the follow-up questions are *always* distributed-systems questions: "how do you keep replicas consistent?", "what's your consistency model?", "how do you order events?" For NYX, ordering trades correctly and not double-counting a fill is *the* correctness property. For Anneava, where a stale read could show a clinician the wrong patient state, the consistency model isn't an optimization, it's safety. Learn this once, deeply, and it pays out for the rest of your career.

## Theory

### What makes distributed systems hard - four root causes

Strip away the buzzwords and *every* difficulty traces to one of four root causes:

1. **Partial failure.** On one machine, a crash takes everything down, clean, all-or-nothing. In a distributed system, *some* nodes fail while others keep running, and the survivors often *can't tell* which is which. A node that doesn't reply might be dead, slow, or perfectly fine behind a broken network link. Partial failure means you must constantly handle "I don't know."
2. **No global clock.** There is no single authoritative "now" that all nodes share. Each has its own drifting clock. So "which event happened first?" has no cheap, reliable answer, and many bugs are really ordering bugs in disguise.
3. **Concurrency.** Multiple nodes act *at the same time* without coordination, producing events that have no inherent order, they are genuinely *concurrent*. Code that assumes a single global sequence of events is wrong.
4. **Unreliable networks.** Messages can be dropped, delayed, duplicated, reordered, or corrupted, and the network can split into islands (partition). The network is not a function call; it's a hostile postal service.

Hold these four up against any distributed bug you ever hit and you'll find at least one of them underneath.

### The 8 fallacies of distributed computing

In 1994-97, engineers at Sun (L. Peter Deutsch and others) catalogued the false assumptions that programmers *keep* making when they move from one machine to many. Memorize them; each is a trap you'll be tempted by:

1. **The network is reliable.** It isn't, packets drop, links die. *Consequence:* every remote call can fail; design for it.
2. **Latency is zero.** It isn't, a cross-continent round trip is ~100-300 ms versus nanoseconds for a local call. *Consequence:* chatty designs (N small calls) are death; batch.
3. **Bandwidth is infinite.** It isn't. *Consequence:* don't ship huge payloads casually; paginate and compress.
4. **The network is secure.** It isn't. *Consequence:* encrypt and authenticate (ties to 06.2 TLS, 12.2 network security).
5. **Topology doesn't change.** It does, nodes come and go, routes shift, deploys move things. *Consequence:* don't hard-code addresses; use discovery.
6. **There is one administrator.** There isn't, different teams/clouds own different pieces. *Consequence:* you can't assume coordinated config or upgrades.
7. **Transport cost is zero.** It isn't, serialization, bandwidth, and infrastructure all cost. *Consequence:* the "free" remote call has real CPU and dollar cost.
8. **The network is homogeneous.** It isn't, different OSes, versions, byte orders, protocols. *Consequence:* agree on explicit wire formats and be liberal in what you accept.

The unifying lesson: **a remote call is not a local call wearing a costume.** The moment a call crosses the network it inherits all eight of these. Most distributed-systems failures are one of these fallacies coming due.

### The system model - getting precise

To reason rigorously we fix a **model**. Sloppiness here is why people argue past each other about distributed systems.

**Processes and messages.** The system is a set of **processes** (nodes) that run code and hold state. They communicate *only* by passing **messages** over channels. **There is no shared memory**, a process learns about another's state *exclusively* by receiving a message. This is the defining constraint: no global variables, no peeking.

**Channels.** A channel carries messages from one process to another. We usually assume **fair-loss / asynchronous** channels: messages may be lost, delayed arbitrarily, and reordered, but if you retry infinitely a message eventually gets through. Some models add **FIFO** (a single channel delivers in send order), a useful, often-realistic strengthening.

**Synchrony, the most consequential axis.** How much do we know about *timing*?

- **Synchronous model:** known upper bounds on message delay *and* on relative clock drift *and* on processing time. If a reply doesn't come within the bound, the node is *definitely* dead. This model is *easy*, but it's a fantasy; real networks have no such bound.
- **Asynchronous model:** *no* timing bounds at all. Messages can take arbitrarily long; clocks drift arbitrarily. This is the *honest* model of the internet, and it's brutally hard, because **you can never conclude a node is dead** (it might just be slow). The famous FLP impossibility (Chapter 26.2) lives here.
- **Partially synchronous model:** the realistic middle. The system is asynchronous *most* of the time but eventually settles into periods where bounds *do* hold. Real consensus algorithms (Raft, Paxos) are designed for this: they use **timeouts** as a heuristic for "probably dead," accepting that timeouts are sometimes wrong.

**The failure-mode ladder.** What kinds of failure must we tolerate? From mildest to nastiest:

- **Crash-stop (fail-stop):** a process runs correctly, then halts permanently and never comes back. Simplest model. The node is gone; that's it.
- **Crash-recovery:** a process can crash and later *restart*, losing in-memory state but possibly keeping anything it wrote to **stable storage** (disk). This is realistic, servers reboot. Algorithms must persist enough to recover safely (Raft persists its log and term to disk for exactly this reason).
- **Omission:** a process fails to send or receive *some* messages while otherwise behaving, a subtle middle ground (e.g. a dropped reply, a full buffer).
- **Byzantine (arbitrary):** a process can do *anything*, send wrong data, lie, send different answers to different peers, collude maliciously. This models bugs, corruption, and adversaries. Tolerating it (Byzantine fault tolerance, BFT) is far more expensive, you need `3f + 1` nodes to tolerate `f` Byzantine ones, versus `2f + 1` for crashes. Blockchains live here; most internal systems assume non-Byzantine and rely on TLS/auth to keep adversaries off the network entirely.

For NYX and Anneava you'll assume **partially synchronous, crash-recovery, non-Byzantine**, the standard assumption behind Raft, Kafka, and Postgres replication. Knowing you're *making* that assumption is half the battle.

### Time and ordering - why physical clocks don't suffice

Here's the chapter's center of gravity. We desperately want to answer "did event A happen before event B?" The naive answer, "compare their timestamps", *fails*, and understanding *why* unlocks everything.

**Physical clocks drift and skew.** Every machine has a quartz oscillator that ticks at a slightly wrong rate (drift), so two clocks started in sync slowly diverge. The instantaneous difference between two clocks is **skew**. Typical drift is tens of microseconds per second, milliseconds per minute. Over a day, unsynchronized clocks can differ by *seconds*.

**NTP helps but doesn't save you.** The Network Time Protocol periodically nudges each machine's clock toward a reference. But NTP corrections themselves travel over the unreliable network, so they're only accurate to roughly **1-50 ms** in practice (worse across the open internet). Worse, to correct a fast clock NTP may **slew or even step the clock backward**, meaning *time can go backwards on a machine*. Code that assumes "later event ⇒ larger timestamp" breaks when a clock jumps back.

So the killer scenario: two events on two machines happen 1 ms apart in reality, but the machines' clocks are skewed by 10 ms. Compare timestamps and you get the order *exactly backwards*. **Physical timestamps cannot reliably order events across machines.** (Spanner's TrueTime, in 26.2, attacks this with atomic clocks + GPS and *explicit uncertainty bounds*, the exception that proves how hard the rule is.)

**The fix: order events by causality, not by clocks.** This is Leslie Lamport's 1978 insight, one of the most important in all of computer science.

#### The happened-before relation (→)

Define a partial order `→` ("happened-before") on events using *only* things we can observe locally:

1. **Same process:** if `a` and `b` are events in the *same* process and `a` comes before `b` in that process's local order, then `a → b`.
2. **Message:** if `a` is the *send* of a message and `b` is the *receive* of that same message, then `a → b` (a cause must precede its effect).
3. **Transitivity:** if `a → b` and `b → c`, then `a → c`.

If `a → b`, then `a` *could have* causally influenced `b`. Crucially, if **neither** `a → b` **nor** `b → a`, the events are **concurrent**, written `a ∥ b`, they happened with no causal connection, and **there is no fact of the matter about which came "first."** Concurrency isn't ignorance; it's a real property. Two clicks on two phones with no message between them are genuinely concurrent, and any global order you impose is arbitrary.

This is *the* mental shift: stop asking "what time did it happen?" and start asking "what could have caused what?" `→` is a **partial order**, some pairs are ordered, some are concurrent, and that's the honest picture of a distributed system.

#### Lamport logical clocks

Lamport clocks turn `→` into integer timestamps cheaply. Each process keeps a single integer counter `L`:

1. Before each *local* event (including a send): `L = L + 1`.
2. When **sending** a message, attach the new `L` to it.
3. When **receiving** a message with timestamp `L_msg`: set `L = max(L, L_msg) + 1`.

**The guarantee:** if `a → b` then `L(a) < L(b)`. Causality implies increasing timestamps.

**The catch, and it's vital:** the converse is *false*. `L(a) < L(b)` does **not** imply `a → b`. Two concurrent events can have any timestamp relationship, including `L(a) < L(b)` even though `a ∥ b`. So Lamport clocks give you a **total order consistent with causality** (great for tie-breaking, pair `(L, process_id)` and you get a deterministic global order to, say, grant a lock), but they **cannot detect concurrency**, they can't tell you whether two events are causally related or merely concurrent. For that we need more.

#### Vector clocks

Vector clocks fix the converse problem. Each of `N` processes keeps a **vector** `V` of `N` counters, `V[i]` = "how many events process `i` knows about from process `i`."

For process `i`:

1. Before each *local* event: `V[i] = V[i] + 1`.
2. When **sending**, attach the whole vector `V`.
3. When **receiving** a vector `V_msg`: for every `k`, `V[k] = max(V[k], V_msg[k])`, then increment own entry `V[i] = V[i] + 1`.

**Comparing vectors:**

- `V_a ≤ V_b` iff `V_a[k] ≤ V_b[k]` for *all* `k`.
- `a → b` iff `V_a < V_b` (≤ everywhere and strictly less somewhere).
- `a ∥ b` (concurrent) iff *neither* `V_a ≤ V_b` *nor* `V_b ≤ V_a`, each is ahead of the other in at least one coordinate.

Now the converse holds: vectors capture causality **exactly**, so you can *detect concurrency*. The price is size: each timestamp is `N` integers, growing with the number of processes (real systems use optimizations like dotted version vectors). This is precisely how Dynamo/Riak detect *conflicting* concurrent writes that need merging, a direct callback to the "multi-leader conflicts" problem in 7.4. We'll implement and *see* this below.

### Consistency models - stated precisely

A **consistency model** is a contract between a distributed store and its clients: *given the operations clients issue, what return values are legal?* It defines which apparent "anomalies" the system promises never to show you. From strongest (most intuitive, most expensive) to weakest (cheapest, most surprising):

**Linearizability (strong / atomic consistency).** The gold standard. The system behaves *as if* there were a single copy of the data and every operation took effect **atomically at some instant between its call and its return**, in an order consistent with real time. Formally: there's a total order of operations such that (a) it respects real-time order, if op A *returned* before op B *started*, A is before B, and (b) each read returns the value of the most recent write in that order. *Consequence:* once a write completes, **every** subsequent read (on any node) sees it. The whole cluster acts like one variable. Intuitive, but it requires coordination on every operation → latency, and it's impossible to maintain during a partition while staying available (CAP). Examples: a single-node DB, etcd/ZooKeeper, Spanner.

**Sequential consistency.** Weaker. There's a single total order of all operations that all processes agree on, and it respects *each process's own program order*, but it need **not** respect real-time order across processes. So if one client writes X and *then* (in real time, on a different client) you read, you might still see the old X, as long as *some* consistent global ordering exists. Rarely the explicit target in modern systems, but the classic stepping stone between linearizable and causal.

**Causal consistency.** Weaker still, and the sweet spot for many systems. Operations related by `→` (happened-before) are seen by everyone in that causal order; **concurrent** operations may be seen in *different* orders by different nodes. So if comment B is a reply to comment A (A → B), nobody ever sees B without A. But two unrelated posts may appear in different orders to different users, and that's fine. Causal consistency is the **strongest model still achievable while remaining available during a partition** (it doesn't need global coordination, only causal metadata, e.g. vector clocks). This is why it's prized.

**Eventual consistency.** The weakest commonly-used guarantee: *if writes stop, all replicas eventually converge to the same value.* It says **nothing** about *when*, or about what you see in the meantime, reads can go backwards, see stale data, or see writes out of order. It's cheap and highly available (Dynamo, Cassandra default, DNS). Fine for a like-count; alarming for a bank balance.

**Useful session guarantees (client-centric).** Often you don't need full strong consistency, just for *your own session* to make sense:

- **Read-your-writes (read-after-write):** after *you* write a value, *your* subsequent reads see that write (or newer). Fixes the maddening "I updated my profile and it still shows the old one" bug from 7.4.
- **Monotonic reads:** if you read a value, later reads never show an *older* value, you never see time go backwards within your session.
- **Monotonic writes:** your writes are applied in the order you issued them.
- **Writes-follow-reads:** if you read a value and then write, your write lands *after* (causally) the value you read.

These are cheap to provide (e.g. pin a client to one replica, or track a logical timestamp the client has seen) and eliminate most user-visible weirdness without paying for full linearizability. You'll lean on read-your-writes constantly in Anneava.

### The CAP theorem - done properly (recap of 07.3)

CAP is the most misquoted theorem in our field. Stated correctly:

> When a distributed system suffers a **network partition** (P), messages between some nodes are lost, it must choose between **C**onsistency (every read sees the most recent write, i.e. linearizability) and **A**vailability (every request to a non-failed node gets a non-error response). You cannot have both *during a partition*.

The crucial, constantly-missed nuance: **CAP is only about behavior during a partition.** It is *not* "pick two of three." Partitions are not optional, they *will* happen, so the real choice is just **C vs A when partitioned**:

- **CP (consistency over availability):** during a partition, refuse requests that can't be served consistently (return errors / block) rather than risk stale or divergent data. Examples: ZooKeeper, etcd, Spanner, HBase. *Anneava's clinical state leans here*, better to say "try again" than to show a clinician wrong data.
- **AP (availability over consistency):** during a partition, keep answering from whatever replica you can reach, accepting that answers may be stale or that writes may conflict and need later reconciliation. Examples: Dynamo, Cassandra, Riak. *NYX's "last trade price" widget can lean here*, a slightly stale price beats a blank screen.

And the classic misuse to avoid: **a single-node store is neither CP nor AP**, there's no partition to survive. If it dies, that's a downtime problem, not a CAP trade. Don't apply CAP to a single Postgres box.

### PACELC - the half CAP leaves out (recap of 07.3)

CAP only speaks about partition time. But systems trade off **even when the network is perfectly healthy**, because keeping replicas strongly consistent requires coordination, which costs latency. **PACELC** completes the picture:

> **If** there's a **P**artition, trade **A**vailability vs **C**onsistency; **E**lse (normal operation), trade **L**atency vs **C**onsistency.

| System | Partition (P → A/C) | Normal (E → L/C) | PACELC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dynamo / Cassandra (default) | choose **A** | choose **L** | **PA/EL** |
| Spanner | choose **C** | choose **C** | **PC/EC** |
| Default tuned Postgres + sync replica | choose **C** | lean **C** | **PC/EC** |

The deep takeaway: **strong consistency costs latency even when nothing is broken**, because a strongly-consistent write must coordinate (often a quorum round trip) before it can be acknowledged. There's no free lunch; PACELC just names which lunch you're buying.

### Replication and quorums - deriving `R + W > N`

Replication (7.4) keeps `N` copies of each piece of data. In a **leaderless / quorum** system (Dynamo-style), a write is sent to all `N` replicas but the client waits for only `W` acknowledgements before declaring success; a read queries replicas and waits for `R` responses, returning the freshest. We get to *tune* `R`, `W`, `N`.

**The quorum condition for strong-ish reads:** we want every read to be guaranteed to see the latest acknowledged write. A write touched `W` replicas; a read touched `R` replicas. By the **pigeonhole principle**, if

```
    R + W > N
```

then the `R` read-replicas and the `W` write-replicas **must overlap in at least one replica**, there are only `N` replicas total, and `R + W` "slots" can't all be distinct if their sum exceeds `N`. That overlapping replica holds the latest write, so the read sees it (we use vector clocks or timestamps to pick the freshest among the `R` responses). That's the whole derivation, pure pigeonhole.

Worked numbers with `N = 3`:

- `W = 2, R = 2`: `2 + 2 = 4 > 3` ✓, the common balanced quorum. Tolerates one node down for *both* reads and writes.
- `W = 3, R = 1`: writes hit all replicas (slow, fragile, one node down blocks writes), reads are fast and always consistent. Good for read-heavy, write-rare data.
- `W = 1, R = 3`: fast writes, slow consistent reads. Write available even with 2 nodes down.
- `W = 1, R = 1`: `1 + 1 = 2 ≤ 3` ✗, **no overlap guaranteed**. Fast and highly available, but *eventually consistent*: a read can miss the latest write. This is the "sloppy quorum" / pure-AP corner.

So the single inequality `R + W > N` is the dial between consistency and availability/latency, PACELC made concrete in three numbers. We'll *build* this below and *watch* the `W=1,R=1` anomaly appear, then fix it.

Quorum subtleties: overlap guarantees you *touch* a fresh replica, but you still need a way to know *which* response is freshest (timestamps/version vectors) and ideally **read-repair** (push the fresh value back to stale replicas you noticed). And "sloppy quorums" with hinted handoff (Dynamo) relax these during failures, trading the guarantee for availability.

## Build it: logical and vector clocks on a simulated network

No black boxes, let's *simulate* a message-passing system and watch causality and concurrency appear. We'll build a tiny deterministic event simulator: processes that do local events and send/receive messages, instrumented with both Lamport and vector clocks.

```python
"""
A tiny message-passing simulation to make happened-before concrete.
We model events as a hand-built schedule so the output is deterministic
and we can SEE causal vs concurrent events.
"""
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Optional

# ---------- Lamport logical clock ----------

class LamportProcess:
    def __init__(self, pid):
        self.pid = pid
        self.clock = 0          # single integer

    def local_event(self):
        self.clock += 1
        return self.clock

    def send(self):
        self.clock += 1         # a send is also an event
        return self.clock       # timestamp piggybacked on the message

    def receive(self, ts_msg):
        self.clock = max(self.clock, ts_msg) + 1
        return self.clock


def lamport_demo():
    print("=== Lamport clocks ===")
    A = LamportProcess("A")
    B = LamportProcess("B")

    a1 = A.local_event()                 # A: event a1
    s  = A.send();        m_ts = s       # A sends message m (carries m_ts)
    b1 = B.local_event()                 # B: event b1 (concurrent with a1/send!)
    b2 = B.receive(m_ts)                 # B receives m  -> learns about A
    a2 = A.local_event()                 # A continues
    b3 = B.local_event()

    print(f"A: a1={a1}  send={m_ts}  a2={a2}")
    print(f"B: b1={b1}  recv={b2}  b3={b3}")
    # Guarantee: send(=2) -> recv: L(send) < L(recv)  (2 < 3)  ✓ causality honored
    # But note: b1 (=1) and A's send (=2): L(b1) < L(send), yet b1 ∥ send !
    # Lamport CANNOT tell us they're concurrent. That's the limitation.
```

Run it and you'll see the send's timestamp is strictly less than the receive's, causality is honored. But you *cannot* look at the integers and conclude whether `b1` and the send were concurrent. Lamport gives order, not concurrency-detection. Now vector clocks, which *can*:

```python
# ---------- Vector clocks ----------

class VectorProcess:
    def __init__(self, pid, index, n):
        self.pid = pid
        self.i = index               # this process's slot in the vector
        self.v = [0] * n             # vector of counters

    def local_event(self):
        self.v[self.i] += 1
        return tuple(self.v)         # snapshot timestamp of this event

    def send(self):
        self.v[self.i] += 1
        return tuple(self.v)         # piggyback the whole vector

    def receive(self, v_msg):
        self.v = [max(a, b) for a, b in zip(self.v, v_msg)]
        self.v[self.i] += 1
        return tuple(self.v)


def vc_leq(x, y):                    # x <= y  (componentwise)
    return all(a <= b for a, b in zip(x, y))

def vc_relation(x, y):
    if x == y:                       return "equal"
    if vc_leq(x, y):                 return "x -> y  (x happened-before y)"
    if vc_leq(y, x):                 return "y -> x  (y happened-before x)"
    return "x || y  (CONCURRENT)"    # neither <= the other


def vector_demo():
    print("\n=== Vector clocks ===")
    # 2 processes: A=index 0, B=index 1
    A = VectorProcess("A", 0, 2)
    B = VectorProcess("B", 1, 2)

    a1 = A.local_event()             # A event
    msg = A.send()                   # A sends (vector piggybacked)
    b1 = B.local_event()             # B event, NO message between this and a1/send
    b2 = B.receive(msg)              # B receives A's message
    a2 = A.local_event()
    b3 = B.local_event()

    events = {"a1": a1, "A.send": msg, "b1": b1,
              "b2(recv)": b2, "a2": a2, "b3": b3}
    for name, v in events.items():
        print(f"  {name:9s} -> {v}")

    print("\nRelations:")
    print("  A.send vs b2(recv):", vc_relation(msg, b2))   # send -> recv (causal)
    print("  a1     vs b1      :", vc_relation(a1, b1))     # CONCURRENT, detected!
    print("  a1     vs b2(recv):", vc_relation(a1, b2))     # a1 -> b2 (causal via msg)
    print("  a2     vs b3      :", vc_relation(a2, b3))     # CONCURRENT
```

The payoff line: `a1 vs b1` prints **CONCURRENT**. Lamport could never tell us that; vector clocks detect it exactly. And `a1 vs b2` prints **causal**, because A's message carried A's history into B, so `b2` *knows about* `a1`. This is the machinery behind detecting conflicting writes in Dynamo-style stores: two writes whose vector clocks are *concurrent* are a genuine conflict that needs merging; two where one dominates the other are simply ordered.

```python
if __name__ == "__main__":
    lamport_demo()
    vector_demo()
```

**What you just proved:** ordering in a distributed system is not about wall-clock time, it's about the causal graph of messages, and vector clocks are the exact, observable encoding of that graph.

### Why this matters in real-time systems

For NYX, market events arrive from multiple feeds. If you tag each event with a vector clock (or a Lamport timestamp for a total order), you can reason about which events *causally* precede a trade decision versus which merely arrived around the same time. A naive "sort by received timestamp" can put a fill *before* the order that caused it if two feed handlers' clocks are skewed, a correctness bug that *logical* clocks eliminate. For Anneava, causal consistency means a clinician never sees a follow-up note without the note it replied to, `→` is exactly the guarantee you want, and vector clocks are how you enforce it across replicas.

## Mini-project: a small distributed key-value store with quorums

> Build a leaderless replicated KV store with `N` replicas, tunable `R`/`W` quorums, injectable replication lag/failure. Demonstrate a **consistency anomaly** with `W=1, R=1`, then **fix it** by setting `R + W > N`. This makes 7.4's replication-lag bug and this chapter's quorum math tangible.

```python
"""
A simulated leaderless quorum KV store.
- N replicas, each holds {key: (value, version)} where version is a
  monotonically increasing (timestamp, writer_id) -- a stand-in for a
  Lamport-style version used to pick the freshest copy.
- A coordinator writes to all replicas but waits for W acks (others may lag/fail).
- A read queries all replicas but waits for R responses, returns the freshest.
We control which replicas are "slow/failed" to reproduce anomalies deterministically.
"""
import itertools

class Replica:
    def __init__(self, rid):
        self.rid = rid
        self.store = {}                    # key -> (value, version)
        self.online = True

    def write(self, key, value, version):
        if not self.online:
            return False
        cur = self.store.get(key)
        # Last-writer-wins by version (higher version wins). Real Dynamo would
        # keep concurrent vector-clock siblings; we keep it simple but correct.
        if cur is None or version > cur[1]:
            self.store[key] = (value, version)
        return True

    def read(self, key):
        if not self.online:
            return None
        return self.store.get(key)         # (value, version) or None


class QuorumKV:
    def __init__(self, n, w, r):
        self.replicas = [Replica(i) for i in range(n)]
        self.N, self.W, self.R = n, w, r
        self._ctr = 0                       # global version source for the demo

    def _next_version(self, writer_id):
        self._ctr += 1
        return (self._ctr, writer_id)       # (logical_time, writer) -> total order

    def put(self, key, value, writer_id="client",
            target_replicas=None):
        """Write to replicas; succeed if >= W acks. target_replicas lets us
        simulate only some replicas receiving the write (lag/partition)."""
        version = self._next_version(writer_id)
        targets = self.replicas if target_replicas is None \
                  else [self.replicas[i] for i in target_replicas]
        acks = sum(rep.write(key, value, version) for rep in targets)
        ok = acks >= self.W
        print(f"  PUT {key}={value!r} v{version} -> acks={acks} "
              f"(need W={self.W}) {'OK' if ok else 'FAILED'}")
        return ok

    def get(self, key, from_replicas=None):
        """Read from replicas; gather >= R responses, return freshest by version."""
        pool = self.replicas if from_replicas is None \
               else [self.replicas[i] for i in from_replicas]
        responses = []
        for rep in pool:
            val = rep.read(key)
            if val is not None or rep.online:
                responses.append((rep.rid, val))
            if len(responses) >= self.R:
                break
        # pick freshest non-None by version
        best = None
        for rid, val in responses:
            if val is None:
                continue
            if best is None or val[1] > best[1]:
                best = val
        result = best[0] if best else None
        seen = [(rid, v) for rid, v in responses]
        print(f"  GET {key} from {[r for r,_ in seen]} -> {result!r} "
              f"(R={self.R})")
        return result


def anomaly_demo():
    print("=== ANOMALY: W=1, R=1, N=3  (R+W=2, NOT > N) ===")
    kv = QuorumKV(n=3, w=1, r=1)
    # Write 'balance'=100 but it only lands on replica 0 (W=1 satisfied immediately).
    kv.put("balance", 100, target_replicas=[0])
    # A reader hits replica 1 or 2 -> never saw the write -> STALE / missing!
    got = kv.get("balance", from_replicas=[2])
    print(f"  >> Reader saw: {got!r}  (expected 100) -- STALE READ ANOMALY\n")


def fixed_demo():
    print("=== FIX: W=2, R=2, N=3  (R+W=4 > N=3, quorums overlap) ===")
    kv = QuorumKV(n=3, w=2, r=2)
    # Write must reach >= 2 replicas to count as success.
    kv.put("balance", 100, target_replicas=[0, 1])   # 2 acks -> OK
    # Any 2-replica read is guaranteed to intersect {0,1} -> sees the write.
    for pair in itertools.combinations(range(3), 2):
        got = kv.get("balance", from_replicas=list(pair))
        assert got == 100, "quorum overlap should guarantee freshness!"
    print("  >> Every 2-replica read saw 100. Overlap guaranteed by R+W>N.\n")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    anomaly_demo()
    fixed_demo()
```

Running this, the anomaly demo prints a **stale read** (the reader hits a replica that never got the write), and the fixed demo *proves*, for *every* pair of replicas, that the read sees the latest value, because `R + W > N` forces overlap. You've now turned the pigeonhole derivation into something you can execute and trust.

**Extensions to deepen it (do at least one):**

1. **Read-repair:** when `get` notices a replica returned a stale/missing value, write the fresh value back to it. Watch the cluster self-heal.
2. **Vector-clock conflicts:** replace the `(time, writer)` version with a real vector clock; make two *concurrent* writes and detect them as siblings instead of silently last-writer-wins.
3. **Read-your-writes:** pin a client to track the highest version it wrote, and have `get` retry/wait until it reads a version ≥ that, eliminating the 7.4 "my profile looks unsaved" bug.
4. **Inject partition:** mark replicas offline mid-test and show how `W=2` *fails the write* (CP behavior) versus `W=1` *succeeding but going stale* (AP behavior), CAP, live.

## Common mistakes

- **"Just use timestamps to order events."** Physical clocks skew and even go *backwards* under NTP correction. Use logical/vector clocks for causal ordering; only trust physical time when you have explicit uncertainty bounds (TrueTime) or for coarse, non-critical purposes.
- **Treating a slow node as a dead node (or vice versa).** In an async system you *cannot* distinguish them. Timeouts are heuristics, not facts, design for "I declared it dead but I was wrong" (e.g. fencing tokens to stop a falsely-declared-dead leader from causing split-brain).
- **"CAP means pick two of three."** No, partitions are mandatory, so the real choice is C vs A *during a partition*. And CAP doesn't apply to single-node systems at all.
- **Confusing Lamport and vector clocks.** Lamport gives a total order *consistent* with causality but **can't detect concurrency**. Vector clocks detect concurrency exactly, at the cost of `O(N)` size. Pick by need.
- **Assuming exactly-once delivery exists end-to-end.** It doesn't across an unreliable network (recap 9.5). You get at-least-once + **idempotent** handlers, or you get at-most-once. "Exactly-once *processing*" is achievable via idempotency/dedup, not by magic delivery.
- **Ignoring the failure model you're assuming.** "Is this crash-stop or Byzantine? Synchronous or partially synchronous?" If you can't answer, you don't actually know what your algorithm guarantees.
- **Quorum overlap ≠ knowing which is fresh.** `R + W > N` guarantees you *touch* a fresh replica; you still need versioning to *identify* the freshest response, plus read-repair to converge the rest.

## On the job / interview angle

- **The follow-ups are always distributed.** In any system-design interview, once you draw two boxes, expect: "what happens when the link between them partitions?", "what's your consistency model and why?", "how do you order these events?". - **State your model out loud.** "I'll assume partially synchronous, crash-recovery, non-Byzantine, standard for an internal service." Naming the model signals you actually know what your guarantees rest on.
- **Map the consistency model to the *data*, not the system.** A great answer says "the like-count is fine eventually-consistent; the account balance needs linearizable or a transaction", different data in the *same* product can sit at different points on the spectrum.

## Exercises

**1. Spot the fallacy.** A teammate writes a service that calls a remote API in a tight loop, one item per call, with no timeout, and assumes the response is instant. Name every fallacy of distributed computing this violates.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

At least: (1) "the network is reliable", no timeout/retry, a single drop hangs forever; (2) "latency is zero", one-item-per-call ignores per-call round-trip cost (should batch); (3) "bandwidth is infinite" / (7) "transport cost is zero", many tiny calls waste overhead; arguably (5) "topology doesn't change" if the endpoint is hard-coded. The fix: batch requests, set timeouts, retry with backoff (and make the handler idempotent), use discovery for the endpoint.

</details>

**2. Lamport by hand.** Process P does events then sends m1; Q receives m1, does an event, sends m2; P receives m2. Starting all clocks at 0, give every event's Lamport timestamp. Then name a pair of concurrent events Lamport *cannot* distinguish from ordered ones.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

P: e1 → L=1; send(m1) → L=2 (m1 carries 2). Q: recv(m1) → L=max(0,2)+1=3; e2 → L=4; send(m2) → L=5 (m2 carries 5). P: recv(m2) → L=max(2,5)+1=6. Causality holds: send(m1)=2 < recv=3, send(m2)=5 < recv=6. Concurrency Lamport can't catch: if Q had done an independent event e0 *before* receiving m1 (say L=1 on Q), it'd share timestamp 1 with P's e1, yet e0 ∥ e1, equal/incomparable Lamport values give no concurrency info. Generally any two events with no message path between them are concurrent, and Lamport's single integer can't reveal that.

</details>

**3. Vector-clock conflict detection.** Two replicas of a shopping cart start at `[0,0]`. Replica A (index 0) adds "milk": its clock becomes `[1,0]`. *Concurrently* replica B (index 1) adds "eggs": `[0,1]`. They later exchange. Are these writes concurrent or ordered? What should the system do?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Compare `[1,0]` and `[0,1]`: neither is ≤ the other (A is ahead in slot 0, B ahead in slot 1) → **concurrent (∥)**. They're a genuine conflict, not one superseding the other. The system must **not** last-writer-wins (that would silently drop an item); it should keep both as siblings and **merge** (cart = {milk, eggs}), exactly how Dynamo/Riak handle concurrent cart writes. Merging the resulting vector gives `[1,1]`.

</details>

**4. Quorum tuning.** With `N=5`, give `(W, R)` for: (a) tolerate 2 nodes down for writes while keeping strongly-consistent reads; (b) fastest possible writes with strongly-consistent reads; (c) a configuration that is *not* strongly consistent.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

(a) Tolerating 2 down for writes means `W ≤ 3`; pick `W=3`, then need `R+W>5` → `R≥3`, so `R=3`. (`W=3,R=3`: classic majority quorum, tolerates 2 failures, strongly consistent.) (b) Fastest writes = smallest `W=1`; then `R>5-1=4` → `R=5`. (`W=1,R=5`: writes super fast/available, reads slow and must reach all nodes.) (c) Any `R+W≤5`, e.g. `W=1,R=1` (sum 2), no overlap guarantee → eventually consistent / possible stale reads.

</details>

**5. Consistency model match.** For each, name the *weakest* model that avoids user complaints: (a) a user editing their own profile; (b) a global counter of total signups shown on a marketing page; (c) a chat thread where replies must never appear before the message they reply to; (d) two users transferring money between shared accounts.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

(a) **Read-your-writes** (a session guarantee), the editor must see their own change; others can lag. (b) **Eventual consistency**, a slightly stale signup count harms nobody. (c) **Causal consistency**, reply→message ordering (`→`) must be preserved for everyone; concurrent unrelated messages can reorder. (d) **Linearizable** (or a serializable transaction), money requires a single agreed order with no anomalies; this is the one place you pay full price.

</details>

**6. Why timeouts, not certainty.** Explain, using the synchrony model, why Raft and Paxos rely on *timeouts* to suspect leader failure rather than *detecting* it for sure, and what bug a too-short timeout can cause.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Real networks are *partially synchronous* (often asynchronous): there's no guaranteed upper bound on message delay, so a node can't *prove* a peer is dead, silence might mean "crashed" or "slow." The only available heuristic is "no heartbeat within T ⇒ probably dead." A too-short `T` falsely suspects a live leader, triggering an unnecessary election and possibly *two* leaders briefly (split-brain) or repeated election churn that stalls progress. This is the practical face of the FLP impossibility (26.2): you can't have guaranteed termination in pure async, so you trade certainty for timeouts and accept occasional false suspicions.

</details>
