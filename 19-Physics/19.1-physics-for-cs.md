## Start here (the gentle on-ramp)

Throw a ball. It goes up, slows, stops at the top, comes back down, speeds up. You already have a perfect intuition for this, you've been catching things your whole life. Physics is just the bookkeeping that makes that intuition precise enough to *predict*.

Here's the entire skeleton of classical mechanics in plain English:

1. **Things have a state**: where they are (position) and how fast they're moving (velocity).
2. **Forces change velocity**, not position directly. A push doesn't teleport the ball; it changes how its velocity is changing. That's acceleration.
3. **To find the future, you integrate**: start with the state now, figure out the forces, nudge the velocity, nudge the position, and repeat for the next slice of time.

That's it. That third step *is* numerical integration, and it's the bridge between physics and code. A real game engine doesn't solve a differential equation symbolically, it does step 3 sixty times a second. When you watch a barrel roll down a ramp in a video game, you're watching `v += a*dt; x += v*dt` run in a loop.

The realization that unlocks it: **you don't need to be afraid of the math, because the math is just describing the loop you'd write anyway.** Calculus gives the exact answer for simple cases; the loop gives an approximate answer for *any* case, including the messy ones (drag, collisions, a hundred interacting bodies) where there's no clean formula. As a programmer, the loop is your home turf.

Let's make all of it precise.

## The scientific method, units, and dimensional analysis (the 5-minute version)

Before the physics, three habits that will save you from nonsense.

**The scientific method, operationally.** A theory in physics isn't "an opinion", it's a *machine that makes predictions you can check*. Newton's laws predict where Mars will be next Tuesday; if Mars isn't there, the theory is wrong, full stop. This is the same loyalty to falsifiable prediction that makes a good test suite valuable: a model that can't be wrong tells you nothing. Every simulation you write in this chapter is a tiny experiment, you'll *validate* it against a known exact answer or a conservation law, exactly the way you'd validate code against expected output.

**SI units.** Physics uses a small set of base units, and everything else is built from them:

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| length | meter | m |
| mass | kilogram | kg |
| time | second | s |
| electric current | ampere | A |
| temperature | kelvin | K |

Derived units are just combinations: velocity is m/s, acceleration is m/s², force (the **newton**, N) is kg·m/s², energy (the **joule**, J) is kg·m²/s², power (the **watt**, W) is J/s. You can always *reconstruct* a derived unit from the formula it lives in, that's the point of the next habit.

**Dimensional analysis** is the cheapest bug-catcher in science. Every equation must be dimensionally consistent: you can't add meters to seconds, and both sides must have the same units. If you write a formula and the units don't match, the formula is *wrong*, no further checking needed. Example: is kinetic energy `½mv` or `½mv²`? Energy has units kg·m²/s². `mv` has units kg·m/s (that's momentum, not energy). `mv²` has units kg·m²/s² ✓. So it must be `v²`. You just derived the *form* of the equation without remembering it, purely from units. Use this constantly as a sanity check, it's the physics equivalent of a type system. A unit mismatch is a compile error for the universe.

```
⚠️ Common mistake: dropping units mid-calculation and plugging numbers blindly.
The infamous 1999 Mars Climate Orbiter crash happened because one team used
pound-force-seconds and another used newton-seconds. $327M lost to a unit bug.
Carry your units like you'd carry types through a function signature.
```

## Kinematics: position, velocity, acceleration are just derivatives

This is where calculus (18.1) and physics become the *same subject*, and it's the single most important connection in the chapter.

Let `x(t)` be an object's position as a function of time. Then:

- **Velocity** is how fast position changes: `v(t) = dx/dt`. The derivative of position.
- **Acceleration** is how fast velocity changes: `a(t) = dv/dt = d²x/dt²`. The derivative of velocity, the second derivative of position.

That's the whole definition. Velocity is the slope of the position graph; acceleration is the slope of the velocity graph. If you understood derivatives as "instantaneous rate of change" in 18.1, you already understand kinematics, physics just renamed the variable from `f` to `x` and called the input "time."

And it runs **both directions**, via integration (the inverse of differentiation):

- Given acceleration, integrate to get velocity: `v(t) = v₀ + ∫a dt`.
- Given velocity, integrate to get position: `x(t) = x₀ + ∫v dt`.

**The special case everyone memorizes, and why.** When acceleration is *constant* (call it `a`), the integrals are easy:

```
v(t) = v₀ + a·t                 (integrate constant a once)
x(t) = x₀ + v₀·t + ½·a·t²       (integrate again)
```

Those are the "kinematics equations" from high school. But notice we didn't memorize them, we *derived* them by integrating a constant twice. The `½t²` comes from `∫t dt = ½t²`, pure calculus. This is the difference between knowing physics and reciting it: you can rebuild the equation from the definition any time you forget it.

**Free fall** is the marquee example: near Earth's surface, gravity gives every object the same downward acceleration `g ≈ 9.81 m/s²` (ignoring air). Drop a ball from height `h` with `v₀ = 0`: `x(t) = h − ½g·t²`. It hits the ground (`x = 0`) at `t = √(2h/g)`. A feather falls as fast as a hammer *in a vacuum*, Apollo 15 did this on the Moon on camera. Air resistance is what normally cheats the feather, and we'll add exactly that to our simulation soon.

```
💼 On the job: in graphics and games, an object's transform is its position;
its rigidbody stores velocity. Every frame the engine integrates a→v→x. When
you tween a UI element with an "ease-in" curve, you're shaping its velocity
profile. Kinematics is the math under all motion you'll ever animate.
```

## Newton's three laws - the rules of the game

Kinematics *describes* motion. **Dynamics** explains *why*, and it's three laws, due to Newton.

**First law (inertia).** An object keeps doing what it's doing, at rest or moving in a straight line at constant velocity, *unless a net force acts on it*. No force, no change in velocity. This is wildly counterintuitive on Earth (things stop on their own!) only because friction is a hidden force. In space, push something and it drifts forever. Velocity is the natural state; *changing* velocity is what requires a cause.

**Second law (the big one).** `F = m·a`, or more honestly `a = F/m`. The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Read it as: **force causes acceleration, and mass is the resistance to being accelerated.** Same force, double the mass → half the acceleration. This is the equation that turns "what forces are acting?" into "how does the motion evolve?", it's the engine of every simulation in this chapter. Given the forces, you get acceleration; from acceleration you integrate to velocity and position. **`F = ma` is the function that feeds your integration loop.**

(The deeper form is `F = dp/dt`, force equals the rate of change of momentum, we'll meet momentum `p` in a moment. For constant mass that reduces to `F = ma`.)

**Third law (action-reaction).** Every force comes in a pair: if A pushes on B with force `F`, then B pushes on A with force `−F`, equal and opposite. When you walk, you push the Earth backward and it pushes you forward. A rocket throws gas down and the gas throws the rocket up. This law is *why momentum is conserved* (next section), the internal pushes inside a system always cancel in pairs.

**The four fundamental forces** (so you know what's "really" out there): gravity, electromagnetism, and the two nuclear forces (strong and weak) that live inside atoms. At human scale, only two matter: gravity (chapter so far) and electromagnetism (everything from friction to circuits, second half of this chapter). Friction, the normal force from a table, tension in a rope, the springiness of a spring, *all* of these are electromagnetism in disguise, atoms pushing on atoms via their electron clouds.

```
⚠️ Common mistake: thinking a moving object needs a force to "keep going."
It doesn't - first law. A force is needed to *change* its motion. The constant
push you feel cycling on flat ground only balances friction + air drag; remove
those and one push lasts forever. Velocity is free; acceleration costs force.
```

## Momentum and its conservation

Define **momentum** as `p = m·v`, mass times velocity, a vector pointing the way the object moves. A truck at 5 m/s and a pebble at 5 m/s have wildly different momentum because of mass; momentum measures "how much motion," weighted by mass.

Newton's second law, in its honest form, is `F = dp/dt`: force is the rate of change of momentum. Rearranged over a time interval, `F·Δt = Δp`, a force applied over time changes momentum. The quantity `F·Δt` is called **impulse**, and it's why a longer follow-through in sports, or a crumple zone in a car, matters: spread the same momentum change over more time and you need less force (gentler on the body or the car).

**Conservation of momentum** is the headline result, and it falls right out of Newton's third law: *in a closed system with no external forces, total momentum stays constant.* Why? Internal forces come in equal-and-opposite pairs (third law), so they cancel when you sum over the whole system. Nothing changes the total.

```
Before:  ●→ (m₁,v₁)    ●→ (m₂,v₂)
                  they collide
After:   ●→ (m₁,v₁')   ●→ (m₂,v₂')

Always:  m₁v₁ + m₂v₂  =  m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'   (total momentum unchanged)
```

This is the law that lets you solve collisions *without knowing the messy details of the collision itself*. Two pucks smack into each other on an air-hockey table; you don't need to model the squish, just write down "total momentum before = total momentum after." Combined with energy (next), it fully determines the outcome of an elastic collision. **In the physics sandbox at the end, we'll verify momentum conservation numerically**, it's one of the best validation checks there is, because if your collision code conserves momentum to floating-point precision, it's almost certainly right.

```
💼 On the job: rocket science is literally momentum conservation - throw mass
out the back (exhaust), gain forward momentum. In game physics, every
collision resolver enforces momentum conservation. In particle physics
detectors, "missing momentum" is how you infer invisible particles like
neutrinos: the books must balance, so what's missing must have left unseen.
```

## Energy: kinetic, potential, work, and conservation

Momentum is one bookkeeping system for motion; **energy** is the other, and it's even more powerful. Energy is the universe's hardest currency: it changes form constantly but the total never changes.

**Kinetic energy** is the energy of motion: `KE = ½mv²`. (We derived the `v²` from dimensional analysis earlier.) Note it goes as `v²`, double the speed, *quadruple* the kinetic energy. That's why a car crash at 60 mph is four times as violent as at 30, not twice. Note also kinetic energy is a *scalar*, direction doesn't matter, only speed.

**Work** is force applied over a distance: `W = F·d` (more precisely the integral `∫F·dx`, and only the component of force along the motion counts). Work is how energy gets *transferred*. Push a box across the floor and you do work on it; that work becomes kinetic energy (it speeds up) or heat (friction).

**The work-energy theorem** ties them together: *the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy.* `W_net = ΔKE`. Push something, it speeds up by exactly the energy you put in. This is just `F = ma` integrated over distance, energy is what you get when you integrate force over *space* instead of time (momentum was force integrated over time). Two integrals of the same law, two conservation laws. Beautiful.

**Potential energy** is *stored* energy, energy by virtue of position or configuration:

- **Gravitational PE** near Earth: `PE = m·g·h`. Lift a mass up high and you've stored energy in it; let it fall and that PE converts to KE.
- **Spring PE**: `PE = ½k·x²`, where `k` is the spring's stiffness and `x` the stretch. Compress a spring and you've stored energy in it.

**Conservation of energy**, the crown jewel: *in a system with no friction or other losses, total mechanical energy `E = KE + PE` is constant.* As a pendulum swings down, PE → KE (speeds up); as it swings up the other side, KE → PE (slows down). At every instant the sum is the same. Where does friction go? It converts mechanical energy to *heat* (random molecular motion), still energy, just no longer organized and usable. Energy is never destroyed, only made messier (that's a preview of entropy, but not for this chapter).

**This is your single best simulation validator.** When you integrate a frictionless system numerically, the *total energy should stay flat.* If your plotted energy line drifts upward, your integrator is injecting fake energy (it'll explode); if it drifts down, your integrator is bleeding energy (everything slowly dies). A flat energy line means your numerics are honest. We'll use exactly this to compare integrators in **Build it**.

```
⚠️ Common mistake: confusing energy and momentum, or thinking one replaces
the other. They're independent books that must BOTH balance. In an elastic
collision, both are conserved. In an INELASTIC collision (things stick), only
momentum is conserved - some kinetic energy becomes heat/deformation. Knowing
which is conserved when is half of solving any collision problem.
```

## Gravity and circular motion

**Universal gravitation.** Newton's other masterpiece: every mass attracts every other mass with force `F = G·m₁·m₂ / r²`, where `G` is the gravitational constant and `r` is the distance between them. The `1/r²` falloff is the same shape we'll see for electric fields, both are "inverse-square" because they spread out over the surface of a sphere (area `4πr²`). Near Earth's surface this reduces to the constant `g` we used: plug Earth's mass and radius into `G·M/r²` and you get ≈ 9.81 m/s². Gravity is the same force whether it's an apple or the Moon, that was Newton's revolutionary unification.

**Circular motion.** To move in a circle, an object must constantly *change direction*, and changing velocity (direction counts!) is acceleration, which requires a force. An object in a circle of radius `r` at speed `v` has **centripetal acceleration** `a = v²/r` pointing *toward the center*. Some real force must supply it: tension (a ball on a string), gravity (a satellite orbiting), friction (a car turning). There is no outward "centrifugal force", the outward feeling is just your inertia (first law) trying to go straight while something yanks you inward. A satellite "falls" toward Earth forever but moves sideways fast enough to keep missing it, that's an orbit. It's *continuous free fall*.

```
💼 On the job: orbital mechanics powers every GPS satellite, every SpaceX
landing, every Kerbal Space Program clone. Centripetal math shows up in
camera-orbit controls in 3D editors, in the "rope swing" feel of platformers,
and in how a robot arm computes the force to whirl a payload around a joint.
```

## Oscillations: the spring and Simple Harmonic Motion (let's derive it)

The spring is the most important toy in physics, because *almost everything that wiggles near equilibrium behaves like a spring.* Master this one system and you understand pendulums, sound, light, atoms in a crystal, LC circuits, even the suspension in a car.

**Hooke's law**: a spring pulls back with force proportional to how far you stretch it, in the opposite direction: `F = −k·x`. The minus sign is everything, it's a **restoring force**, always pointing back toward equilibrium (`x = 0`). Stretch right, it pulls left; compress left, it pushes right.

Now combine Hooke's law with Newton's second law. `F = ma` and `F = −kx`, so:

```
m·a = −k·x
m·(d²x/dt²) = −k·x
d²x/dt² = −(k/m)·x
```

That last line is a **differential equation**, and it says something gorgeous: *the acceleration is proportional to the negative of the position.* The farther you pull it out, the harder it accelerates back. What function has a second derivative equal to negative itself (times a constant)? Sine and cosine! So the solution is:

```
x(t) = A·cos(ω·t + φ)      where  ω = √(k/m)
```

- `A` is the **amplitude** (how far it swings).
- `ω` (omega) is the **angular frequency**, set entirely by the spring stiffness and mass: `ω = √(k/m)`. Stiffer spring → faster wiggle. Heavier mass → slower wiggle.
- `φ` is the **phase** (where in the cycle it starts).
- The **period** (time for one full cycle) is `T = 2π/ω = 2π√(m/k)`, and the **frequency** is `f = 1/T = ω/2π` cycles per second (hertz).

This is **Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)**, smooth back-and-forth sinusoidal motion. The energy sloshes between KE (fastest at the middle) and spring PE (maximum at the extremes), and their sum stays constant. We'll simulate this and watch the energy line stay flat, or *not*, depending on which integrator we pick. The spring is the perfect integrator test bench *because* you know the exact answer (`A·cos(ωt)`) to compare against.

A connection worth holding onto: *anything* sitting at the bottom of a smooth potential-energy valley behaves like a spring for small wiggles, because near a minimum every curve looks like a parabola (a Taylor expansion, 18.1!), and a parabolic PE means a linear restoring force means SHM. That's why oscillations are everywhere.

## Build it - simulating motion (Euler vs Verlet vs RK4)

Time to make a particle move with code. This is the heart of the chapter. We'll build three integrators, run them on problems where we *know* the right answer, and watch which ones lie.

The setup is always the same: a particle has position `x` and velocity `v`. A force function tells us the acceleration `a = F/m` at any state. We chop time into steps of size `dt` and march forward.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# Test bench: the spring (SHM). We KNOW the exact answer, so we can
# measure each integrator's error and energy drift honestly.
#   m x'' = -k x   ->   a(x) = -(k/m) x
# -------------------------------------------------------------------
m, k = 1.0, 1.0           # mass, stiffness  ->  omega = sqrt(k/m) = 1
omega = np.sqrt(k / m)
x0, v0 = 1.0, 0.0         # start pulled out to x=1, released from rest
dt = 0.1
T_total = 40.0
steps = int(T_total / dt)

def accel(x):
    return -(k / m) * x   # Hooke's law / Newton's 2nd law

def energy(x, v):
    return 0.5 * m * v**2 + 0.5 * k * x**2   # KE + spring PE

# Exact analytic solution for comparison: x(t) = x0 cos(omega t)
t = np.linspace(0, T_total, steps + 1)
x_exact = x0 * np.cos(omega * t)
```

**Integrator 1, Explicit (Forward) Euler.** The most obvious thing: use the current velocity and acceleration to step everything forward. It's the `v += a*dt; x += v*dt` from the on-ramp.

```python
def euler():
    x, v = x0, v0
    xs, vs, Es = [x], [v], [energy(x, v)]
    for _ in range(steps):
        a = accel(x)
        x = x + v * dt          # use OLD velocity to move position
        v = v + a * dt          # use OLD acceleration to change velocity
        xs.append(x); vs.append(v); Es.append(energy(x, v))
    return np.array(xs), np.array(vs), np.array(Es)
```

Euler is simple and **wrong in a specific, instructive way**: for an oscillator it *gains* energy every cycle. The amplitude grows, the orbit spirals outward, and given enough time the simulation explodes. It's not a small bug you can ignore, it's structural.

**Integrator 2, Velocity Verlet.** The trick the game industry actually uses for stable, energy-respecting simulation. It's barely more code than Euler but it's *symplectic*: it doesn't conserve energy perfectly, but the energy error stays bounded forever instead of drifting. The orbit stays an orbit.

```python
def verlet():
    x, v = x0, v0
    a = accel(x)
    xs, vs, Es = [x], [v], [energy(x, v)]
    for _ in range(steps):
        x = x + v * dt + 0.5 * a * dt**2   # full position update using current a
        a_new = accel(x)                   # acceleration at the NEW position
        v = v + 0.5 * (a + a_new) * dt     # average old & new accel for velocity
        a = a_new
        xs.append(x); vs.append(v); Es.append(energy(x, v))
    return np.array(xs), np.array(vs), np.array(Es)
```

**Integrator 3, Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4).** The accuracy workhorse of scientific computing. It samples the slope four times per step (start, two midpoints, end) and takes a clever weighted average. It's extremely *accurate* per step, error shrinks like `dt⁵`, though for long oscillations it slowly leaks energy (it's not symplectic). Great when you want precision over a finite time, e.g. a spacecraft trajectory.

```python
def deriv(state):
    x, v = state
    return np.array([v, accel(x)])         # d/dt [x, v] = [v, a]

def rk4():
    state = np.array([x0, v0], dtype=float)
    xs, vs, Es = [state[0]], [state[1]], [energy(*state)]
    for _ in range(steps):
        k1 = deriv(state)
        k2 = deriv(state + 0.5 * dt * k1)
        k3 = deriv(state + 0.5 * dt * k2)
        k4 = deriv(state + dt * k3)
        state = state + (dt / 6.0) * (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)
        xs.append(state[0]); vs.append(state[1]); Es.append(energy(*state))
    return np.array(xs), np.array(vs), np.array(Es)
```

**Now run all three and let the energy plot tell the truth:**

```python
xe, ve, Ee = euler()
xv, vv, Ev = verlet()
xr, vr, Er = rk4()

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4.5))

ax1.plot(t, x_exact, 'k--', lw=1, label='exact  x = cos(t)')
ax1.plot(t, xe, label='Euler', alpha=0.8)
ax1.plot(t, xv, label='Verlet', alpha=0.8)
ax1.set_title('Position vs time'); ax1.set_xlabel('t'); ax1.set_ylabel('x')
ax1.legend()

E0 = energy(x0, v0)
ax2.axhline(E0, color='k', ls='--', lw=1, label='true energy')
ax2.plot(t, Ee, label='Euler (blows up)')
ax2.plot(t, Ev, label='Verlet (bounded)')
ax2.plot(t, Er, label='RK4 (tiny drift)')
ax2.set_title('Total energy vs time - the validator')
ax2.set_xlabel('t'); ax2.set_ylabel('KE + PE'); ax2.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

**What you'll see, and why it matters.** The Euler position spirals outward and its energy climbs steadily, it's manufacturing energy from nothing, which is physically impossible and numerically fatal. Verlet's position tracks the exact cosine and its energy wiggles slightly but stays pinned near the true value forever. RK4's position is essentially perfect over this window with only a hair of slow energy drift.

**The lesson is the whole reason game engines exist the way they do:** for interactive simulation that must stay stable indefinitely (a game running for hours), you want a *symplectic* integrator like Verlet, bounded energy beats raw accuracy. For a one-shot high-precision trajectory (a probe to Jupiter), you want RK4's accuracy. Plain Euler is almost never the right choice for oscillatory or orbital motion; it's the integrator people reach for first and regret. **You validate this not by trusting a textbook but by plotting a conserved quantity and watching it.** That habit, check a conservation law to validate a simulation, is the most transferable skill in this chapter.

### A projectile with air drag (no clean formula - the loop wins)

Free fall in a vacuum has a tidy closed-form answer. Add air resistance and the closed form disappears, but the *loop doesn't care.* Drag opposes velocity and grows with speed: `F_drag = −b·v·|v|` (quadratic drag). We just add it to the force and integrate.

```python
g = 9.81
b = 0.02          # drag coefficient (lumps in air density, area, shape)
mass = 0.145      # a baseball, kg

def step_projectile(pos, vel, dt):
    speed = np.linalg.norm(vel)
    F = np.array([0.0, -mass * g]) - b * speed * vel   # gravity + quadratic drag
    a = F / mass
    vel = vel + a * dt                                  # semi-implicit Euler:
    pos = pos + vel * dt                                # use NEW vel (stable!)
    return pos, vel

# Launch at 40 m/s, 45 degrees. Compare vacuum vs drag.
def fly(drag_b):
    global b; b = drag_b
    pos = np.array([0.0, 0.0]); vel = 40*np.array([np.cos(np.pi/4), np.sin(np.pi/4)])
    path = [pos.copy()]
    while pos[1] >= 0:
        pos, vel = step_projectile(pos, vel, 0.01)
        path.append(pos.copy())
    return np.array(path)

vac = fly(0.0); air = fly(0.02)
plt.figure(figsize=(8,4))
plt.plot(vac[:,0], vac[:,1], '--', label='vacuum (ideal parabola)')
plt.plot(air[:,0], air[:,1], label='with air drag (real baseball)')
plt.xlabel('x (m)'); plt.ylabel('y (m)'); plt.title('Projectile: drag kills range')
plt.legend(); plt.axis('equal'); plt.show()
```

The drag curve falls short and *asymmetric*, it climbs less steeply than it falls, exactly like a real thrown ball. There is no high-school formula for that trajectory, but the loop produces it effortlessly. **This is the superpower of numerical simulation: it handles the messy real world where symbolic math gives up.** We used *semi-implicit* Euler here (update velocity first, then use the new velocity for position) because it's far more stable than plain Euler for almost free, a one-line change, a huge robustness win.

```
💼 On the job: this exact loop is the core of any physics engine (Box2D,
Bullet, PhysX, Unity, Unreal). Ballistics in games, the arc of a thrown
grenade, cloth and hair sim, particle effects, and "ragdoll" deaths are all
this force-integrate-repeat loop with more forces and more bodies. Robotics
simulators (MuJoCo, Isaac Sim) train control policies entirely inside it.
```

### A bouncing ball - collisions and restitution (energy on purpose)

A ball falling under gravity and bouncing off the floor is the simplest *collision* simulation, and it teaches the one knob every game engine exposes: **restitution** `e`, the fraction of speed retained after a bounce. `e = 1` is a perfect (elastic) bounce that returns to the same height forever; `e = 0` is a dead splat that sticks; real balls live in between (a basketball is ≈ 0.8, a tennis ball ≈ 0.75). The collision rule is one line: when the ball hits the floor, *flip and scale* its vertical velocity, `v_y → −e·v_y`.

```python
import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
g, e, dt = 9.81, 0.8, 0.005
y, vy = 5.0, 0.0          # start at 5 m, at rest
ys, ts, Es = [], [], []
t = 0.0
while t < 8.0:
    vy -= g * dt          # gravity
    y  += vy * dt         # semi-implicit Euler
    if y < 0:             # hit the floor: reflect with energy loss
        y = 0.0
        vy = -e * vy      # restitution: keep fraction e of the speed
    ys.append(y); ts.append(t); Es.append(0.5*vy**2 + g*y)   # E per unit mass
    t += dt

fig, (a1, a2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
a1.plot(ts, ys); a1.set_title(f'Bouncing ball (e={e})'); a1.set_xlabel('t'); a1.set_ylabel('height')
a2.plot(ts, Es); a2.set_title('Energy steps DOWN at each bounce (e<1)')
a2.set_xlabel('t'); a2.set_ylabel('energy / mass'); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

The height plot shows the classic decaying series of arcs, each peak lower than the last by a factor `e²` (since `e` scales *speed* and energy goes as speed-squared). And here's the validation lesson with a twist: the energy plot is *not* flat, it drops in discrete steps, one per bounce. That's **correct** this time, because an inelastic bounce *should* lose energy (to heat and sound). The skill isn't "energy must always be flat", it's *knowing when it should be conserved and when it shouldn't*, and checking that the simulation matches that expectation. Set `e = 1.0` and the energy line goes flat (within integrator noise); set `e = 0` and the ball dies on first contact. Each is the right physics for its `e`.

### The damped, driven oscillator - resonance (where bridges fall down)

Real springs don't wiggle forever, friction *damps* them. Add a drag term `−c·v` to the spring, and optionally a periodic *driving* force `F₀·cos(ω_d·t)`, and you get the **damped driven harmonic oscillator**, arguably the most important system in all of engineering. Its equation:

```
m·x'' = −k·x − c·x' + F₀·cos(ω_d·t)
        spring   damping   driving
```

```python
import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
m, k = 1.0, 1.0
omega0 = np.sqrt(k/m)            # natural frequency
def simulate(c, F0, omega_d, dt=0.01, T=60):
    x, v = 0.0, 0.0; xs, ts = [], []
    t = 0.0
    while t < T:
        a = (-k*x - c*v + F0*np.cos(omega_d*t)) / m
        v += a*dt; x += v*dt      # semi-implicit Euler
        xs.append(x); ts.append(t); t += dt
    return np.array(ts), np.array(xs)

# Sweep driving frequency, record steady-state amplitude -> resonance curve
freqs = np.linspace(0.3, 2.0, 60)
amps = [np.abs(simulate(c=0.1, F0=1.0, omega_d=w)[1][-2000:]).max() for w in freqs]
plt.plot(freqs, amps); plt.axvline(omega0, ls='--', c='r', label='natural ω₀')
plt.title('Resonance: amplitude peaks when you drive at the natural frequency')
plt.xlabel('driving frequency ω_d'); plt.ylabel('steady-state amplitude'); plt.legend(); plt.show()
```

The amplitude curve **spikes** when the driving frequency `ω_d` matches the system's natural frequency `ω₀ = √(k/m)`, that's **resonance**. Push a child on a swing at just the right rhythm and small pushes build huge swings; push off-rhythm and nothing happens. Resonance is why a soprano can shatter a glass (driving it at its natural frequency), why the Tacoma Narrows Bridge tore itself apart in 1940, and why soldiers break step crossing a bridge. As a programmer, the same math governs the *step response* of a control loop or a PID controller, under-damped (`c` too small) and your robot arm oscillates and overshoots; over-damped (`c` too big) and it's sluggish; critically damped is the sweet spot. The spring you derived analytically, now with two extra terms, models an astonishing range of real systems, and the loop handles all of them identically.

## Waves (the quick tour) - frequency, wavelength, superposition

A wave is a disturbance that travels while the medium itself mostly stays put, think of a ripple crossing a pond or the "stadium wave" of fans standing up in sequence. The crucial mental shift: **the wave moves; the stuff doesn't travel with it.** Energy and pattern propagate; the water just bobs up and down.

Three numbers describe a simple (sinusoidal) wave, and they're tied together:

- **Wavelength** `λ`: distance between two crests (meters).
- **Frequency** `f`: how many crests pass a fixed point per second (hertz). Its inverse, the **period** `T = 1/f`, is the time per cycle.
- **Speed** `v = f·λ`: crests-per-second times meters-per-crest gives meters-per-second. Memorize this one relationship and you can derive the others.

For sound in air, `v ≈ 343 m/s`; a 440 Hz "A" note has `λ = 343/440 ≈ 0.78 m`. For light in vacuum, `v = c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s`, and `f` sets the color.

**Superposition** is the property that makes waves *interesting* and connects them straight to signal processing: *when two waves overlap, the displacements simply add.* Two crests meeting make a bigger crest (constructive interference); a crest meeting a trough cancels (destructive interference, this is how noise-cancelling headphones work, generating an inverted wave to sum to zero). Because waves add linearly, *any* complex waveform, a chord, a spoken word, an image scanline, can be decomposed into a sum of simple sine waves. That's the **Fourier** idea, and it's the bridge from this chapter to audio DSP, image compression (JPEG is cosine waves), and the spectrum analyzer in every music app.

```python
# Superposition in one line of NumPy: a chord is just sines added together.
fs = 8000; t = np.linspace(0, 0.02, int(fs*0.02))
A    = np.sin(2*np.pi*440*t)     # A4
Csh  = np.sin(2*np.pi*554*t)     # C#5
E    = np.sin(2*np.pi*659*t)     # E5
chord = A + Csh + E              # the displacements ADD - that's superposition
plt.plot(t, chord); plt.title('A-major chord = sum of three sine waves')
plt.xlabel('time (s)'); plt.ylabel('amplitude'); plt.show()
```

The jagged chord waveform *is* three clean sines added, and a Fourier transform would pull them back out as three spikes at 440/554/659 Hz. Waves are where physics quietly becomes signal processing.

## Electromagnetism at a usable level

Now the other great force, the one that runs every chip in this book. We'll keep it intuitive but real, and we'll keep pointing back to the semiconductor chapters (17.1, 17.2) because *this is the physics those chapters assumed you already had.*

**Charge.** Matter carries electric charge, positive (protons) or negative (electrons), measured in coulombs (C). Like charges repel, opposites attract. Charge is *conserved* (never created or destroyed, only moved) and *quantized* (always a whole-number multiple of the electron's charge `e ≈ 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C`). In 17.1 the whole story of doping was about controlling where charges sit and move in silicon, this is the bedrock under it.

**Coulomb's law**, the electric version of gravity, and same inverse-square shape: two charges `q₁, q₂` a distance `r` apart push or pull with force `F = k·q₁·q₂ / r²`. Same `1/r²` falloff as gravity (both spread over a sphere). The difference: gravity only attracts, but charge comes in two signs, so electric forces both attract *and* repel, which is exactly what lets us build switches.

**Electric field `E`.** Rather than tracking forces between every pair of charges, we say a charge fills the space around it with a *field*, an arrow at every point telling any other charge which way and how hard it'd be pushed. `F = q·E`: drop a charge `q` into a field `E` and it feels force `q·E`. The field is the *cause sitting in space*, waiting; the force is what a charge feels when it shows up. This field picture is exactly what's happening inside the depletion region of a P-N junction (17.1): separated charges create a built-in field that pushes carriers back.

```python
# Field plot: visualize the electric field of a +charge and a -charge (a dipole).
# Ties straight to 16.3 data-visualization (quiver/streamplot are field plots).
import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
gx, gy = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-3,3,30), np.linspace(-3,3,30))
def field(qx, qy, q):
    dx, dy = gx-qx, gy-qy
    r2 = dx**2 + dy**2 + 1e-3
    return q*dx/r2**1.5, q*dy/r2**1.5
Ex1,Ey1 = field(-1,0,+1);  Ex2,Ey2 = field(+1,0,-1)
Ex, Ey = Ex1+Ex2, Ey1+Ey2
plt.streamplot(gx, gy, Ex, Ey, density=1.2, color='steelblue')
plt.plot(-1,0,'ro'); plt.plot(1,0,'bo')
plt.title('Electric field of a dipole (+ red, − blue)'); plt.axis('equal'); plt.show()
```

**Electric potential and voltage.** Potential energy per unit charge is the **electric potential** (volts). The *difference* in potential between two points is **voltage** `V`. Intuition: voltage is "electrical pressure" or "height", charges roll downhill from high potential to low, just as a ball rolls downhill in gravity. A 9 V battery maintains a 9-volt "height difference" between its terminals, and that's the push that drives current. The field and the potential are linked: the field points "downhill," in the direction of steepest voltage drop (`E = −dV/dx`, a gradient, straight from 18.1 calculus).

**Current, resistance, and Ohm's law.** **Current `I`** is the flow rate of charge, coulombs per second, measured in amperes (A). Push charges with a voltage and they flow; the material's opposition to that flow is **resistance `R`** (ohms, Ω). **Ohm's law** ties them: `V = I·R`. More voltage → more current; more resistance → less current. The water analogy is genuinely good: voltage is the pump pressure, current is the gallons-per-second flowing, resistance is how narrow the pipe is.

```python
# Ohm's law and a simple series circuit. A 9V battery, two resistors in series.
V = 9.0
R1, R2 = 100.0, 200.0      # ohms, in series -> resistances add
R_total = R1 + R2          # series: R = R1 + R2
I = V / R_total            # Ohm's law: one current through the whole loop
V1, V2 = I*R1, I*R2        # voltage "drops" across each resistor
P = V * I                  # power delivered = V*I (watts)
print(f"Current        I = {I*1000:.2f} mA")
print(f"Drop across R1   = {V1:.2f} V")
print(f"Drop across R2   = {V2:.2f} V   (V1+V2 = {V1+V2:.2f} V = supply ✓)")
print(f"Power            = {P*1000:.1f} mW")
```

Two rules cover most simple circuits, both just conservation laws (Kirchhoff's laws): **charge is conserved at junctions** (current in = current out) and **energy is conserved around a loop** (voltage drops sum to the supply). In *series*, resistances add and the same current flows through all. In *parallel*, the voltage is shared and currents add (`1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂`). Notice `V1 + V2` equals the 9 V supply exactly, that's Kirchhoff's voltage law, energy conservation for the loop. This is the level of circuit reasoning the transistor chapter (17.2) leans on: a MOSFET is a *voltage-controlled* switch, and "voltage controls current" is the entire game.

**Magnetism and induction (the intuition).** Moving charges make **magnetic fields**, a current in a wire wraps a magnetic field around it. And the reverse, the discovery that powers the modern world: a *changing* magnetic field pushes on charges, driving a current. That's **electromagnetic induction** (Faraday's law). Spin a magnet near a coil and you generate electricity, that's every power plant on Earth, from the coal turbine to the wind farm to the hydroelectric dam: something spins a magnet near a coil. Run it backwards (current makes a field that pushes a magnet) and you have an electric motor. Generators and motors are the *same device* run in opposite directions, and induction is the hinge.

**Light is an electromagnetic wave.** Maxwell's great unification: a changing electric field makes a magnetic field, which makes an electric field, which makes a magnetic field, a self-sustaining ripple that propagates through empty space at speed `c`. *That ripple is light.* Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, UV, X-rays, gamma rays, all the *same* phenomenon, electromagnetic waves, differing only in frequency/wavelength. Your WiFi and your eyesight run on the identical physics, separated only by a frequency dial. This is why the next chapter (19.2) can say "a photon is a particle of light" and mean something precise: light is an EM wave that also comes in quantized lumps. The handoff from this chapter to the quantum one happens right here.


## Mini-project - a 2D physics sandbox with gravity and collisions

Let's put it all together: a tiny 2D world of bouncing balls under gravity, with wall and ball-ball collisions, and we'll *prove it's correct* by checking that momentum and energy obey their conservation laws. This is a miniature version of a real 2D physics engine, and the validation step is what separates a toy from a tool.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class World:
    def __init__(self, n=6, g=0.0, box=10.0, seed=1):
        # g=0 (no gravity) so total energy & momentum are BOTH conserved
        # -> clean validation. Flip g on afterward to watch it bounce.
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        self.box = box
        self.pos = rng.uniform(1, box-1, size=(n, 2))
        self.vel = rng.uniform(-3, 3, size=(n, 2))
        self.r   = np.full(n, 0.5)
        self.m   = np.full(n, 1.0)
        self.g   = np.array([0.0, -g])

    def total_momentum(self):
        return (self.m[:, None] * self.vel).sum(axis=0)

    def total_energy(self):
        ke = 0.5 * (self.m * (self.vel**2).sum(axis=1)).sum()
        pe = (self.m * 9.81 * self.pos[:, 1]).sum() if self.g[1] != 0 else 0.0
        return ke + pe

    def step(self, dt):
        # 1) integrate motion (semi-implicit Euler - stable)
        self.vel += self.g * dt
        self.pos += self.vel * dt

        # 2) wall collisions: reflect velocity, keep inside box (elastic)
        for d in (0, 1):
            lo = self.pos[:, d] - self.r < 0
            hi = self.pos[:, d] + self.r > self.box
            self.vel[lo | hi, d] *= -1
            self.pos[lo, d] = self.r[lo]
            self.pos[hi, d] = self.box - self.r[hi]

        # 3) ball-ball elastic collisions (equal mass -> swap velocity
        #    components along the line of centers; conserves p AND KE)
        n = len(self.m)
        for i in range(n):
            for j in range(i+1, n):
                d = self.pos[j] - self.pos[i]
                dist = np.hypot(*d)
                overlap = self.r[i] + self.r[j] - dist
                if overlap > 0 and dist > 1e-9:
                    nrm = d / dist
                    self.pos[i] -= nrm * overlap/2     # separate them
                    self.pos[j] += nrm * overlap/2
                    rel = self.vel[i] - self.vel[j]
                    approaching = rel @ nrm
                    if approaching > 0:                # only if closing in
                        # equal-mass elastic impulse along normal:
                        self.vel[i] -= approaching * nrm
                        self.vel[j] += approaching * nrm

# ---- Run WITHOUT gravity and validate both conservation laws ----
w = World(n=6, g=0.0, seed=7)
p0, E0 = w.total_momentum(), w.total_energy()
ps, Es = [], []
for _ in range(4000):
    w.step(0.01)
    ps.append(w.total_momentum().copy()); Es.append(w.total_energy())
ps, Es = np.array(ps), np.array(Es)

fig, (a1, a2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4))
a1.plot(ps[:,0], label='total p_x'); a1.plot(ps[:,1], label='total p_y')
a1.axhline(p0[0], ls='--', c='k', lw=1); a1.axhline(p0[1], ls='--', c='gray', lw=1)
a1.set_title('Momentum conserved? (flat = yes)'); a1.legend()
a2.plot(Es); a2.axhline(E0, ls='--', c='k', lw=1)
a2.set_title('Energy conserved? (flat = yes)'); a2.set_ylim(E0*0.9, E0*1.1)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print(f"momentum drift: {np.abs(ps - p0).max():.2e}   (should be ~0)")
print(f"energy   drift: {np.abs(Es - E0).max():.2e}   (should be ~0)")
```

**What success looks like.** With gravity off, both momentum lines and the energy line should be *flat*, drifts at the `1e-12`-ish floating-point-noise level. That flatness is *proof* your collision math is physically honest: elastic, equal-mass collisions must conserve both, and if your code does too, it's correct. If you'd botched the impulse (say, used `+=` where you needed `-=`), the energy line would visibly drift and you'd catch the bug *instantly from the plot* without ever reading the collision code line-by-line. **The conservation law is the test oracle.** Then flip `g=9.81` back on and watch the balls bounce in the box, now energy sloshes between KE and PE but the *total* (with PE included) still holds. Extending this is the fun part: add unequal masses (the impulse formula generalizes), add restitution `< 1` for inelastic bounces that lose energy to "heat," or add a spatial grid so it scales past `O(n²)` collision checks, which is exactly the optimization path a real engine takes.

## Teach it back

If you can explain these in your own words without notes, you own this chapter:

1. **Kinematics is calculus.** Velocity is the derivative of position, acceleration the derivative of velocity. Integrate acceleration twice (for constant `a`) and you *derive* `x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at²`, no memorizing.
2. **Newton's laws.** Inertia (no force, no velocity change); `F = ma` (force causes acceleration, mass resists it); action-reaction (forces come in equal-opposite pairs, which is *why* momentum is conserved).
3. **Two bookkeeping systems for motion.** Momentum `p = mv` (force integrated over *time*, conserved in closed systems) and energy `KE = ½mv²` + PE (force integrated over *space*, conserved without friction). Both must balance; knowing which is conserved in a given collision solves it.
4. **SHM derived.** `F = −kx` plus `F = ma` gives `x'' = −(k/m)x`, whose solution is `cos(ωt)` with `ω = √(k/m)`. Anything near a potential minimum is a spring.
5. **Integrators differ by their conservation behavior.** Euler injects energy and explodes on oscillators; Verlet is symplectic (bounded energy, stable forever, what games use); RK4 is highly accurate per step (great for trajectories). You *validate* any sim by plotting a conserved quantity and checking it's flat.
6. **EM at a usable level.** Charge → field → force (`F = qE`); voltage is electrical "height," current is charge flow, `V = IR` (Ohm); series adds resistances, Kirchhoff's laws are just conservation of charge and energy. Changing magnetic fields induce currents (generators/motors), and light is a self-propagating EM wave, the physics under every chip (17.1, 17.2) and sensor.

## Exercises

**1.** A ball is dropped (`v₀ = 0`) from a 20 m roof. Using `g = 9.81 m/s²`, how long until it lands, and how fast is it going then?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Position: `x(t) = 20 − ½g t²`. Lands when `x = 0`: `t = √(2·20/9.81) = √4.08 ≈ 2.02 s`. Speed at landing: `v = g·t = 9.81 × 2.02 ≈ 19.8 m/s`. Cross-check with energy: `mgh = ½mv²` → `v = √(2gh) = √(2·9.81·20) ≈ 19.8 m/s` ✓. Two methods, same answer, that's the kind of redundant check you can trust.
</details>

**2.** Why does plain (explicit) Euler *gain* energy on a spring, while velocity Verlet doesn't? Answer conceptually.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Explicit Euler updates velocity using the acceleration at the *start* of the step and position using the *old* velocity, so it consistently overshoots on the way out and undershoots the restoring effect, each step the state lands slightly "outside" the true elliptical orbit in phase space, and these errors accumulate in one direction, pumping in energy. Verlet is *time-symmetric* (run it backward and you retrace your steps) and *symplectic* (it exactly preserves phase-space area), which mathematically forces the energy error to oscillate around the true value rather than drift. Symmetry of the algorithm → conservation in the result. This is why "use a symplectic integrator" is standard advice for any long-running simulation.
</details>

**3.** A 2 kg cart at 3 m/s hits a stationary 1 kg cart and they stick together (perfectly inelastic). Find the final speed, and how much kinetic energy was lost.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Momentum is conserved (no external horizontal force): `2·3 + 1·0 = (2+1)·v` → `v = 6/3 = 2 m/s`. KE before: `½·2·3² = 9 J`. KE after: `½·3·2² = 6 J`. Lost: `3 J` (became heat/deformation in the sticking). This is the key lesson: in an *inelastic* collision momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is *not*. Use the right conservation law for the situation.
</details>

**4.** A 12 V battery drives a 100 Ω and a 50 Ω resistor in *parallel*. Find the total current from the battery and the power dissipated.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Parallel resistance: `1/R = 1/100 + 1/50 = 0.01 + 0.02 = 0.03`, so `R = 33.3 Ω`. Total current: `I = V/R = 12/33.3 = 0.36 A`. Power: `P = VI = 12 × 0.36 = 4.32 W`. Sanity check by adding branch currents: `I₁ = 12/100 = 0.12 A`, `I₂ = 12/50 = 0.24 A`, sum `0.36 A` ✓, Kirchhoff's current law holds.
</details>

**5.** Middle C is 262 Hz. What's its wavelength in air (`v = 343 m/s`)? If you and a friend play 262 Hz and 264 Hz together, what do you hear?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

`λ = v/f = 343/262 ≈ 1.31 m`. Two close frequencies *superpose* and produce **beats**, the amplitude pulses at the *difference* frequency, `264 − 262 = 2 Hz`, so you hear the volume throb twice per second. Musicians tune by ear exactly this way: adjust until the beats slow to zero, meaning the frequencies match. Superposition you can *hear*.
</details>
