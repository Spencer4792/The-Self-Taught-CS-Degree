## Start here - the on-ramp (read this even if you're brand new)

Forget software for a second. A chart is a translation. On one side you have numbers, sales per month, a patient's heart rate, the price of a stock. On the other side you have *marks on a page*, dots, bars, lines, colors. **A visualization is the rulebook that says "this number becomes that mark."**

Here's the whole field in one sentence: *we draw data because the human eye is a massively parallel pattern detector, and a table of numbers wastes it.* Your eyes can spot the outlier in a scatter plot in a quarter of a second. Reading the same outlier out of a 500-row spreadsheet takes minutes and a headache. Visualization is offloading computation from your slow conscious brain onto your fast visual cortex.

So every chart is a bet: *if I map this number to position (or length, or color), will the reader's eye recover the truth I put in?* Sometimes the bet pays off, bar charts let you compare lengths instantly. Sometimes it fails, pie charts ask you to compare angles, which humans are terrible at. And sometimes the bet is *rigged*: a truncated axis can make a 2% change look like a cliff. Learning viz is learning which bets are honest and which encodings the eye actually reads well.

Three words you'll hear constantly, demystified up front:
- **Encoding** = the rule mapping a data value to a visual property (value → x-position, value → bar height, category → color). The single most important concept in the whole chapter.
- **Mark** = the geometric thing you draw (point, line, bar, area, arc).
- **Channel** = the visual property of a mark you vary to carry data (position, length, angle, area, color, shape). Some channels are read accurately; some aren't. That ranking is *science*, not taste.

Keep this picture in your head: **data → encoding → marks + channels → the reader's eye → a recovered value.** Every section below makes one link in that chain precise. If you only remember one thing: *position and length are the honest channels; angle, area, and color are the treacherous ones.* The rest is detail.

## Principles first: what makes a chart honest and effective

Don't learn five libraries before you understand what they're *for*. The tooling is interchangeable; the principles are not.

### Encodings and the channel ranking (Cleveland & McGill)

In 1984 Cleveland and McGill ran the experiment that should be tattooed on every dashboard team's wall. They showed people the same data encoded different ways and measured how accurately they could read values back. The result is a ranked list of **how perceptually accurate each channel is**, from best to worst:

1. **Position along a common scale** (a scatter plot, a dot plot), the gold standard.
2. **Position along non-aligned scales** (small multiples with separate axes).
3. **Length** (bar charts).
4. **Angle / slope** (pie slices, line steepness).
5. **Area** (bubble size).
6. **Volume / curvature**.
7. **Color hue / saturation**, the *least* accurate for reading precise magnitudes.

The practical rule falls right out of this: **encode your most important quantity in the most accurate channel you have available.** Want people to compare exact values? Use position or length. The pie chart sits at #4-5, that's *why* it's mocked, not because it's unfashionable. If you must show "share of a whole," a sorted bar chart beats a pie almost every time because you've upgraded the reader from angle-judging to length-judging.

Color sits at the bottom for *magnitude*, but it's excellent for one thing position can't do: **categorical identity** (which series is which) and **highlighting** (one red bar in a field of gray). Use channels for what they're good at.

### Chart-type selection: match the question to the encoding

Don't pick a chart by browsing a gallery. Ask *what relationship am I showing?* and the chart falls out:

| Question you're answering | Encoding that fits | Chart |
|---|---|---|
| How does one value compare across categories? | length / position | bar, dot plot |
| How does a value change over time? | position over a continuous x | line, area |
| How are two variables related? | position in 2D | scatter plot |
| How is one variable distributed? | position + count | histogram, box, violin |
| How do parts make up a whole? | length (sorted) > angle | stacked/sorted bar > pie |
| How does a matrix of values vary? | color on a grid | heatmap |
| How do many variables relate at once? | many positions | scatterplot matrix, parallel coords |

The mistake juniors make is picking the chart first ("let's do a donut") and forcing the data in. Pick the *question*, then the encoding, then the chart type is nearly determined.

### Avoiding lies: the honesty rules

A chart is a rhetorical device, and rhetoric can deceive. The classic lies:

- **Truncated axes.** Starting a bar chart's y-axis at 90 instead of 0 turns a 2% difference into a visual chasm. **Bar charts must start at zero**, the bar's *length* is the encoding, and length from a non-zero base lies. (Line charts *may* truncate, because the encoding there is the *slope/position*, not length, but label it.)
- **Dual y-axes.** Two series on two different scales let you manufacture any correlation you want by sliding the axes. Avoid unless both scales are meaningful and labeled; prefer two stacked panels.
- **Area for 1D data.** If a quantity doubles and you double *both* width and height of an icon, the area quadruples, the eye reads "4×." Encode 1D data in 1D (length), not area.
- **3D pie/bar charts.** Perspective distorts the very lengths and angles you're asking people to compare. Never.
- **Cherry-picked bins / time windows.** A histogram's story changes with bin width; a trend's story changes with the start date. Show the sensitivity.
- **The Lie Factor (Tufte):** `Lie Factor = (size of effect shown in graphic) / (size of effect in data)`. Honest charts have a Lie Factor of 1.0. A truncated bar chart might have a Lie Factor of 5.

### Tufte's data-ink ratio

Edward Tufte's central metric: `data-ink ratio = ink used to show data / total ink`. Maximize it. Every pixel that isn't encoding data, heavy gridlines, drop shadows, redundant legends, chartjunk borders, background gradients, is *noise competing with signal*. The discipline:

1. Erase non-data ink (chrome, borders, background fills).
2. Erase redundant data ink (don't label every point *and* draw a legend *and* a data table).
3. What remains, refine, light gridlines, direct labels on lines instead of a detached legend.

The result is a chart where the data is the loudest thing on the page. This isn't minimalism for aesthetics; it's signal-to-noise engineering.

### Color, done right

- **Sequential** scales (light → dark of one hue) for ordered magnitude: 0 → max.
- **Diverging** scales (two hues meeting at a neutral midpoint) for data with a meaningful center: profit vs. loss around zero, anomaly above/below a baseline.
- **Categorical** (qualitative) palettes for unordered groups, distinct *hues*, ≤ ~8 before they blur.
- Use **perceptually uniform** maps (`viridis`, `cividis`, `magma`). The old `jet`/rainbow map lies: it has bright bands that the eye reads as edges that aren't in the data, and it's unreadable to colorblind viewers.

### Accessibility (not optional)

~8% of men have some color vision deficiency. Rules to bake in:
- **Never encode meaning by color alone.** Pair color with shape, pattern, position, or a direct label. (A red/green up/down chart is invisible to a deuteranope, add ▲/▼.)
- Use colorblind-safe palettes (viridis, ColorBrewer's "colorblind safe" sets, Okabe-Ito).
- Ensure text/background contrast (WCAG AA ≈ 4.5:1). The WCAG mechanics are covered in **08.1 (frontend)**.
- Give charts real `<title>`/`alt`/ARIA descriptions so screen readers get the takeaway.

### The grammar of graphics (the big idea)

Wilkinson's *Grammar of Graphics* (the "gg" in `ggplot2`) is the unifying theory, and once it clicks you stop seeing "chart types" and start seeing *compositions*. A statistical graphic is built from independent layers:

- **Data**, a tidy table (one row per observation; see **16.2**).
- **Aesthetics / encodings**, the mapping of columns to channels (`x = month, y = revenue, color = product`).
- **Geometries (marks)**, point, line, bar, area.
- **Scales**, how data units become pixels/colors (linear, log, the color ramp).
- **Statistics**, transforms applied before drawing (bin for a histogram, smooth for a trend line, aggregate for a mean).
- **Facets**, splitting into small multiples by a variable.
- **Coordinates**, Cartesian, polar (a pie is just a stacked bar in polar coords!), geographic.

A "pie chart" isn't a primitive, it's `geom_bar` + `coord_polar`. A "bubble chart" is `geom_point` with `size` mapped. This is the model behind ggplot2, Vega-Lite, Plotly Express, and (under the hood) the way you should *think* even in D3 or Matplotlib. **Charts are sentences in a grammar, not items in a menu.** We'll keep referencing these seven pieces below.

## The Python stack: Matplotlib, seaborn, Plotly

### Matplotlib - the real object model (figure / axes)

Everyone learns Matplotlib through `plt.plot()` and then is mystified when they need two subplots. The fix is to learn the **object model**, which is the *actual* API; `plt.something` is just a convenience layer that scribbles on a hidden "current axes."

The hierarchy:
- **Figure**, the whole canvas/window. One figure can hold many axes.
- **Axes**, a single plot (its own x-axis, y-axis, title, data). *This is the thing you draw on.* (Confusingly named: an "Axes" is a plot, not an axis line.)
- **Axis**, the x or y scale/ticks living inside an Axes.
- **Artists**, every drawable (line, text, patch, tick) is an Artist.

The professional pattern is **always create the figure and axes explicitly** and call methods on the axes:

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

x = np.linspace(0, 10, 200)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.5))   # one Figure, one Axes - explicit
ax.plot(x, np.sin(x), label="sin", lw=2)
ax.plot(x, np.cos(x), label="cos", lw=2, ls="--")
ax.set_title("Trig, the object-oriented way")
ax.set_xlabel("x"); ax.set_ylabel("amplitude")
ax.legend(frameon=False)         # Tufte: lose the legend box
ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)  # erase non-data ink
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("trig.png", dpi=150)
```

Subplots are just an array of axes, small multiples for free:

```python
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(9, 6), sharex=True, sharey=True)
for ax, freq in zip(axes.flat, [1, 2, 3, 4]):
    ax.plot(x, np.sin(freq * x))
    ax.set_title(f"freq = {freq}")
fig.suptitle("Small multiples = faceting (grammar of graphics!)")
fig.tight_layout()
```

That `sharey=True` is a *perception* decision: shared scales make the panels comparable (Cleveland channel #1 vs #2). Once you see `subplots` as faceting, the grammar from above maps straight onto Matplotlib.

**When to reach for raw Matplotlib:** maximum control, publication figures, anything custom or weird. It's the assembly language of Python viz, verbose but total power.

### seaborn - statistical charts, grammar-flavored

seaborn sits *on top* of Matplotlib and bakes in the grammar of graphics plus good defaults. It speaks tidy DataFrames natively (columns → encodings) and does the statistical transforms for you.

```python
import seaborn as sns
import pandas as pd

tips = sns.load_dataset("tips")   # built-in demo data

# One line: faceting + color encoding + a regression stat layer
g = sns.lmplot(
    data=tips, x="total_bill", y="tip",
    hue="smoker",      # color encoding
    col="time",        # facet into small multiples
    height=4, scatter_kws={"alpha": 0.6},
)
g.set_axis_labels("Bill ($)", "Tip ($)")
```

Note how directly that maps to the grammar: data = `tips`, aesthetics = `x/y/hue`, geom = points + reg line, stat = linear fit, facet = `col`. seaborn's `displot` (distributions), `catplot` (categorical), `heatmap`, and `pairplot` cover 80% of exploratory data analysis. **Reach for seaborn for fast statistical EDA on a DataFrame.**

### Plotly - interactivity for free

Matplotlib/seaborn make *static* images. Plotly emits *interactive* HTML/JS (hover tooltips, zoom, pan, legend toggling) and runs identically in a notebook, a Dash app, or a web page. `plotly.express` is the grammar-of-graphics front door:

```python
import plotly.express as px

fig = px.scatter(
    tips, x="total_bill", y="tip",
    color="smoker", size="size", facet_col="time",
    hover_data=["day"],
    labels={"total_bill": "Bill ($)", "tip": "Tip ($)"},
    title="Tips - interactive (hover, zoom, toggle)",
)
fig.update_layout(template="plotly_white")  # Tufte-clean
fig.write_html("tips.html")   # a standalone interactive page
```

Same grammar again (`x/y/color/size/facet_col`), but now the output is explorable. **Reach for Plotly when the reader needs to interrogate the data themselves**, dashboards, notebooks you're sharing, anything web-facing. It's also your bridge to the JS world, since the underlying `plotly.js` is the same library you can drop into NYX.

## Scales, annotation, and the finishing details that separate amateur from pro

The chart-type choice is the skeleton; these details are the muscle. Juniors skip them and ship charts that are *technically correct but unreadable*.

### Log vs. linear scales - a perception, not a default

A **linear** scale maps equal *differences* to equal pixel distances (10→20 looks the same as 1000→1010). A **log** scale maps equal *ratios* to equal distances (10→100 looks the same as 100→1000). The rule:

- **Use a log scale when the data spans orders of magnitude** (incomes, populations, latencies, viral case counts), a linear axis crushes everything small into a flat line against one giant value. On a log axis, a constant *growth rate* shows as a *straight line*, which is exactly what you want to read off exponential data.
- **Use linear for additive comparisons** where the absolute difference is the story.
- **Always label a log axis loudly**, a reader who assumes linear will badly misread a log chart (a small visual gap can be a 10× difference). Log scales are honest only if announced.

```python
fig, (a1, a2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))
y = [1, 10, 100, 1000, 10000]
a1.plot(y, marker="o"); a1.set_title("linear - small values invisible")
a2.plot(y, marker="o"); a2.set_yscale("log"); a2.set_title("log - exponential = straight line")
```

### Annotation and direct labeling (the Tufte payoff)

The single highest-use polish: **label the data directly instead of forcing the eye to a detached legend.** Put the series name at the end of each line; annotate the one point that matters; drop the legend box. Every saccade the reader doesn't make between legend and line is cognitive load saved.

```python
ax.plot(months, revenue, color="#2563eb")
ax.annotate("launch", xy=(6, revenue[6]), xytext=(6, revenue[6]+20),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->"))     # call out the meaningful event
ax.text(months[-1], revenue[-1], " Revenue", va="center")  # label the line directly
```

A chart that *narrates itself*, title states the takeaway ("Revenue doubled after launch", not "Revenue over time"), the key point is annotated, series are labeled in place, is worth ten charts that make the reader hunt.

### Overplotting - when too many points become a blob

With thousands of points a scatter plot turns into an opaque smear that hides the density. Fixes, in order of preference: **transparency** (`alpha=0.1` so density shows as darkness), **hexbin/2D-histogram** (bin into cells, color by count, a heatmap of density), **sampling** (draw a random subset), or **contours**. Never ship a solid-black scatter and call it done, that's *hiding* data, not showing it.

```python
ax.hexbin(x, y, gridsize=40, cmap="viridis")    # density as color - beats a black blob
```

### Animation and transitions - motion as an encoding

Motion is a *channel* too: a transition that morphs bar A into bar B lets the eye track *which* element changed (object constancy), which is exactly why D3's enter/update/exit transitions and Plotly's animation frames matter. Use animation to show *change over a third variable* (time) or to maintain identity across an update, **not** as decoration. A gratuitous spin lowers the data-ink ratio; a meaningful transition raises comprehension.

## The Web/JS stack: Chart.js, Recharts, D3 - and when to use which

If your day job is web, here's the decision tree to use, from highest-level to lowest:

| Library | Level | Use when | NYX/Anneava fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chart.js** | High | you need a standard chart *fast*, vanilla JS, canvas | quick admin readouts |
| **Recharts** | High (React) | React app, standard charts, declarative | **NYX dashboards** |
| **Plotly.js** | High/Mid | scientific/3D/statistical, interactivity built-in | high-dim exploratory views |
| **D3.js** | Low | bespoke, novel, or highly custom visuals | custom NYX viz, a specialty |

The rule: **use the highest-level tool that can express your chart, and drop to D3 only when nothing higher can.** D3 is power *and* effort; don't pay for it unless the chart is truly custom.

### Chart.js - the quick one

Config-object API over `<canvas>`. Minimal code, looks good by default, great for the 90% case.

```html
<canvas id="rev"></canvas>
<script src="https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/chart.js"></script>
<script>
new Chart(document.getElementById("rev"), {
  type: "bar",
  data: {
    labels: ["Q1", "Q2", "Q3", "Q4"],
    datasets: [{ label: "NYX Revenue ($k)", data: [120, 150, 180, 240] }],
  },
  options: {
    scales: { y: { beginAtZero: true } },   // honesty: bars from zero!
    plugins: { legend: { display: false } },
  },
});
</script>
```

**Reach for Chart.js for quick, standard charts in plain JS.** It renders to canvas (one bitmap), so it's fast with lots of points but you can't style individual elements with CSS or attach per-bar React state.

### Recharts - React-native charts (tie to NYX)

Recharts is **components, not config**, each chart piece is a React element, which means it composes with the rest of your NYX UI, takes props/state, and re-renders reactively. This is why it's the default for the NYX dashboard.

```jsx
import {
  ResponsiveContainer, LineChart, Line, XAxis, YAxis,
  CartesianGrid, Tooltip, Legend,
} from "recharts";

function NyxBalanceChart({ data }) {
  // data = [{ date: "2026-01", balance: 1200, spend: 800 }, ...]
  return (
    <ResponsiveContainer width="100%" height={320}>
      <LineChart data={data} margin={{ top: 8, right: 24, bottom: 8, left: 0 }}>
        <CartesianGrid strokeDasharray="3 3" stroke="#eee" /> {/* light gridlines */}
        <XAxis dataKey="date" />
        <YAxis />
        <Tooltip />
        <Legend />
        <Line type="monotone" dataKey="balance" stroke="#2563eb" dot={false} />
        <Line type="monotone" dataKey="spend" stroke="#dc2626" dot={false} />
      </LineChart>
    </ResponsiveContainer>
  );
}
```

The chart is *declarative*: you describe the marks (`<Line>`), the scales (`<XAxis>/<YAxis>`), and Recharts diffs and re-renders when `data` changes, same mental model as the rest of React (see **08.1**). It renders SVG, so each element is a DOM node you can style, animate, and click. **Reach for Recharts in React apps that need standard charts wired into app state**, exactly NYX.

### D3.js - the powerful low-level one

D3 is not a chart library. **D3 is a library for binding data to the DOM and computing the math (scales, layouts, shapes) to turn data into visual attributes.** It draws *nothing* by itself, *you* decide every pixel. That's why every bespoke, award-winning, "how did they make that" visualization is D3 (or built on its scale/shape modules). It's the deepest tool, and a worthy specialty.

#### The data-join: enter / update / exit (the core model)

This is *the* D3 idea, and it trips up everyone. The data-join answers one question: **"Given my data array and the DOM elements currently on screen, which elements should I create, which should I update, and which should I remove?"**

D3 computes this by *joining* a data array against a selection of DOM nodes, splitting the result into three sets:

- **enter**, data points with *no* matching DOM node yet → you need to **create** elements.
- **update**, data points that *already* have a matching node → you **modify** existing elements.
- **exit**, DOM nodes whose data point is *gone* → you **remove** elements.

It's exactly the reconciliation React does for you internally, except in D3 *you* drive it explicitly. Once you see it as "create / update / remove, keyed by data," it's just a diff. The modern `selection.join()` wraps all three:

```js
import * as d3 from "https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/d3@7/+esm";

const svg = d3.select("#chart").append("svg")
  .attr("width", 480).attr("height", 240);

const x = d3.scaleBand().range([40, 460]).padding(0.2);   // category → pixels
const y = d3.scaleLinear().range([220, 20]);              // value    → pixels

function render(data) {
  x.domain(data.map(d => d.label));
  y.domain([0, d3.max(data, d => d.value)]);

  svg.selectAll("rect")
    .data(data, d => d.label)        // KEY function: identity by label, not index
    .join(
      enter => enter.append("rect")  // NEW bars: create + start collapsed
          .attr("x", d => x(d.label))
          .attr("width", x.bandwidth())
          .attr("y", y(0)).attr("height", 0)
          .attr("fill", "#2563eb"),
      update => update,              // EXISTING bars: fall through to the shared transition
      exit => exit.transition().duration(400)  // GONE bars: animate out + remove
          .attr("y", y(0)).attr("height", 0).remove()
    )
    // applied to BOTH enter + update: animate every bar to its true height
    .transition().duration(600)
      .attr("x", d => x(d.label))
      .attr("width", x.bandwidth())
      .attr("y", d => y(d.value))
      .attr("height", d => 220 - y(d.value));
}

render([{label:"A",value:30},{label:"B",value:80},{label:"C",value:45}]);
// later: render([{label:"B",value:50},{label:"C",value:90},{label:"D",value:70}]);
//   -> A exits (animates out), B/C update (animate to new heights), D enters (grows in)
```

Everything hinges on the **key function** `d => d.label`. Without it, D3 joins by *array index*, so "the second bar" always updates in place even if it's now a different category, leading to the classic bug where data appears to "teleport" between bars. With a key, D3 tracks *identity*: bar "B" stays bar "B" and smoothly animates, "A" exits, "D" enters. **Enter/update/exit + a key function is 80% of understanding D3.** Everything else (scales, axes, shape generators, force/tree/treemap layouts) is helper math feeding attributes into this join. Full worked example with the update transition is in the mini-project.

**Reach for D3 when the chart is truly custom**, a novel encoding, a bespoke interaction, a network/tree/geo layout, or pixel-perfect brand work. For a bar chart in a React app, Recharts is faster to ship; for the *unusual* thing, D3 is the only tool deep enough.

## High-dimensional visualization (a specialty worth building)

Two channels (x, y) plus color, size, and shape gets you maybe 5 dimensions before the chart turns to mud. Real data has dozens. Here are the honest tools for *p > 3* dimensions.

### Scatterplot matrix (SPLOM)

Show *every pairwise* scatter in a grid: an N×N matrix of small scatter plots for N variables, with histograms/densities on the diagonal. It's faceting (grammar!) over variable pairs. Great for spotting which pairs correlate before modeling.

```python
import seaborn as sns
iris = sns.load_dataset("iris")
sns.pairplot(iris, hue="species", corner=True, diag_kind="kde")
```

Scales as O(N²) panels, so it's honest up to ~8 variables, then overwhelming.

### Parallel coordinates

Give each variable its *own vertical axis*, lined up side by side, and draw each data row as a **line** crossing all axes at its values. Now an observation is a path, and a *cluster* is a bundle of parallel paths. It defeats the 2D limit by trading position for many-axis polylines, you can read 10+ dimensions at once.

```python
import plotly.express as px
px.parallel_coordinates(
    iris, color="species_id",
    dimensions=["sepal_width","sepal_length","petal_width","petal_length"],
    color_continuous_scale=px.colors.diverging.Tealrose,
).show()
```

Watch-out: the story depends on **axis order** (adjacent axes show their relationship most clearly) and reversing/scaling axes, reorder interactively and you'll find structure. Crossing lines between two axes signal *negative* correlation; parallel bundles signal *positive*.

### Dimensionality reduction for viz: PCA, t-SNE, UMAP (intuition)

When p is huge, project down to 2D so the eye can work, *preserving structure*. Three workhorses (the math for PCA is derived in **14.1**):

- **PCA**, *linear*. Finds the orthogonal directions of maximum variance (eigenvectors of the covariance matrix) and keeps the top 2. **Fast, deterministic, distances and global layout are meaningful, axes are interpretable.** But it can only capture linear structure, a curled-up manifold stays curled.
- **t-SNE**, *nonlinear, local*. Optimizes the 2D layout so that points which are *neighbors in high-D stay neighbors in 2D*. Brilliant at revealing **clusters**. But: global distances and cluster *sizes/gaps are meaningless* (don't read "these clusters are far apart"), it's stochastic (different seed → different picture), and the `perplexity` knob changes everything. Use it to *find* clusters, never to measure them.
- **UMAP**, *nonlinear*, builds a graph of local neighborhoods then lays it out. **Faster than t-SNE, preserves more global structure**, scales to big data. A solid default modern choice for "throw 100-D embeddings onto a 2D plot."

```python
import umap                       # pip install umap-learn
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

X, yy = load_digits(return_X_y=True)        # 64-dim handwritten digits
pca = PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(X)   # linear, fast, global-faithful
um  = umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1).fit_transform(X)  # nonlinear, cluster-faithful
# Scatter both, colored by digit label; UMAP separates the 10 digits far cleaner.
```

The honest workflow: **PCA first** (cheap, tells you intrinsic dimensionality via the explained-variance curve), then **UMAP/t-SNE** for cluster discovery, and always caption *which* you used, because the two tell different lies if you forget their rules.

### Heatmaps

A matrix of values → a grid of colored cells. The right tool for correlation matrices, confusion matrices (ties to **14.1**), gene expression, or any 2D table of magnitudes. Use a **diverging** scale centered at 0 for correlations; cluster the rows/columns (a *clustermap*) to surface block structure.

```python
import numpy as np, seaborn as sns
corr = iris.drop(columns=["species"]).corr()
sns.heatmap(corr, annot=True, vmin=-1, vmax=1, cmap="vlag", center=0)
```

## Dashboards (tie to Grafana)

A *chart* answers one question; a **dashboard** answers a *workflow's* worth of questions on one screen, kept fresh. The engineering principles:

- **One job per panel.** Each chart = one clear question. Don't cram.
- **Layout for scanning.** Most-important / highest-level KPIs top-left (F-pattern reading); detail/breakdowns below. The eye lands top-left first.
- **Consistent encodings across panels.** "Revenue" should be the *same blue* in every chart. Inconsistent color across a dashboard is a top usability bug.
- **Coordinated/linked views.** Filtering or brushing one panel updates the others (Plotly Dash callbacks, Recharts shared state, D3 brushes). This is what turns charts into an *analysis tool*.
- **Latency budget.** A dashboard refreshes; pre-aggregate so each panel query is cheap (see DB query optimization, **07.2**, and caching, **09.2**).

**Grafana** is the canonical ops dashboard: it connects to a time-series store (Prometheus, InfluxDB) and renders live panels, request rate, p95 latency, error rate, CPU. It's the visualization front end of the observability stack covered in **13.x**. The lesson from Grafana that transfers to *any* dashboard you build: **the dashboard is a saved set of queries plus panels plus a refresh interval, not a one-off picture.** Treat panels as queries-over-time, not static art.

## Build it - a Tufte-clean, honest bar chart, three ways

Same data (NYX quarterly revenue), three stacks, so you can *feel* the level differences.

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
q = ["Q1","Q2","Q3","Q4"]; rev = [120,150,180,240]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6,3.5))
bars = ax.bar(q, rev, color="#2563eb")

ax.spines[["top","right"]].set_visible(False)  # data-ink
ax.set_title("NYX Revenue ($k)")
for b, v in zip(bars, rev):                  # direct labels > separate legend

```

## Mini-project: a multi-view analytics dashboard + a D3 enter/update/exit chart

**Part A, Plotly Dash multi-view dashboard** over a real-ish NYX transactions dataset. Three coordinated panels: a time-series of spend, a category breakdown, and a KPI strip; a dropdown filters all three.

```python
# pip install dash plotly pandas
import pandas as pd, numpy as np
import plotly.express as px
from dash import Dash, dcc, html, Input, Output

# --- a real-ish dataset: 6 months of NYX transactions ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
days = pd.date_range("2026-01-01", "2026-06-30", freq="D")
cats = ["Groceries","Rent","Transport","Dining","Subscriptions"]
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "date": rng.choice(days, size=4000),
    "category": rng.choice(cats, size=4000, p=[.30,.10,.18,.27,.15]),
    "amount": rng.gamma(2.0, 25, size=4000).round(2),
})

app = Dash(__name__)
app.layout = html.Div([
    html.H2("NYX - Spend Analytics"),
    dcc.Dropdown(id="cat", options=["All", *cats], value="All", clearable=False,
                 style={"width":"260px"}),
    html.Div(id="kpis", style={"display":"flex","gap":"24px","margin":"12px 0"}),
    dcc.Graph(id="ts"),       # time series
    dcc.Graph(id="bycat"),    # category breakdown
])

@app.callback(
    Output("kpis","children"), Output("ts","figure"), Output("bycat","figure"),
    Input("cat","value"))
def update(cat):
    d = df if cat == "All" else df[df.category == cat]          # ONE filter drives ALL panels
    daily = d.groupby(d.date.dt.to_period("M").dt.to_timestamp())["amount"].sum().reset_index()
    bycat = d.groupby("category")["amount"].sum().sort_values(ascending=False).reset_index()

    kpis = [
        html.Div([html.Small("Total spend"), html.H3(f"${d.amount.sum():,.0f}")]),
        html.Div([html.Small("Transactions"), html.H3(f"{len(d):,}")]),
        html.Div([html.Small("Avg / txn"),    html.H3(f"${d.amount.mean():,.2f}")]),
    ]
    ts = px.line(daily, x="date", y="amount", markers=True,
                 title="Monthly spend").update_layout(template="plotly_white")
    cb = px.bar(bycat, x="amount", y="category", orientation="h",   # sorted bars > pie
                title="By category").update_layout(template="plotly_white",
                                                    yaxis={"categoryorder":"total ascending"})
    return kpis, ts, cb

if __name__ == "__main__":
    app.run(debug=True)
```

Run it, change the dropdown, watch all three panels update together, *that's* a dashboard (coordinated views + a refresh path), not three loose charts.

**Part B, the D3 enter/update/exit bar chart, fully interactive.** Drop this in an `.html` file and open it. Click the button to mutate the data and *watch* enter/update/exit animate.

```html
<!DOCTYPE html>
<div id="chart"></div>
<button onclick="shuffle()">Mutate data</button>
<script type="module">
import * as d3 from "https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/d3@7/+esm";
const W=480,H=260,M={t:20,r:10,b:30,l:30};
const svg=d3.select("#chart").append("svg").attr("width",W).attr("height",H);
const x=d3.scaleBand().range([M.l,W-M.r]).padding(0.2);
const y=d3.scaleLinear().range([H-M.b,M.t]);
const xAxis=svg.append("g").attr("transform",`translate(0,${H-M.b})`);

function render(data){
  x.domain(data.map(d=>d.label));
  y.domain([0, d3.max(data,d=>d.value)]).nice();
  xAxis.transition().duration(500).call(d3.axisBottom(x));

  svg.selectAll("rect").data(data, d=>d.label).join(
    enter=>enter.append("rect")
        .attr("x",d=>x(d.label)).attr("width",x.bandwidth())
        .attr("y",y(0)).attr("height",0).attr("fill","#2563eb"),
    update=>update.attr("fill","#16a34a"),          // recolor survivors to SEE updates
    exit=>exit.transition().duration(500)
        .attr("y",y(0)).attr("height",0).style("opacity",0).remove()
  ).transition().duration(700)
     .attr("x",d=>x(d.label)).attr("width",x.bandwidth())
     .attr("y",d=>y(d.value)).attr("height",d=>y(0)-y(d.value));
}
window.shuffle=()=>{
  const labels="ABCDEF".split("");
  const data=labels.filter(()=>Math.random()>0.35)            // randomly drop some -> exits
    .map(l=>({label:l,value:Math.round(Math.random()*90+10)})); // random values -> updates/enters
  render(data);
};
shuffle();
</script>
```

Click "Mutate data" repeatedly: bars that *stay* turn green and glide to new heights (**update**), new letters grow up from the axis (**enter**), and dropped letters shrink and fade out (**exit**). Seeing the three sets behave differently is the whole lesson. Extend it: add a key-less version (`.data(data)` with no key fn) and watch bars *teleport* their labels, that contrast is the best way to internalize why the key function matters.

## Common mistakes

- **Truncated bar-chart axes.** Bars encode *length*; non-zero baselines lie. Start at zero. (Lines may truncate, different encoding.)
- **Pie charts for >3 slices or for precise comparison.** You're forcing angle-judging (channel #4). Sorted bar chart instead.
- **Rainbow / `jet` colormaps.** Perceptually non-uniform (fake edges) and colorblind-hostile. Use `viridis`.
- **Encoding meaning by color alone.** ~8% of men can't read red/green. Always pair with shape, position, or label.
- **3D charts and dual axes.** Distortion and manufactured correlations. Avoid.
- **D3 data-join without a key function.** Joins by index → data "teleports" between elements on update. Always pass `d => d.id`.
- **Reading global distance off t-SNE.** Cluster gaps and sizes are *meaningless* in t-SNE. Use UMAP/PCA if global structure matters.
- **Chartjunk.** Drop shadows, gradients, heavy gridlines, they lower the data-ink ratio and compete with the signal.
- **Wrong tool for the level.** Hand-rolling a basic bar chart in D3 (slow to ship) or trying to build a novel network viz in Chart.js (impossible). Match tool to customization need.

## On the job / interview angle

- **"Why is this chart misleading?"** is a real interview/PR-review question. Answer with the vocabulary: truncated axis, Lie Factor, channel choice, dual axes, area-for-1D. Show you reason about *perception*, not aesthetics.
- **"Pick a chart for X."** Don't name a chart, name the *question* and the *encoding* first, then the chart falls out. "It's a part-to-whole over time → stacked area, or sorted bars if precise comparison matters."
- **"Chart.js vs Recharts vs D3?"** The level/customization tradeoff: highest-level tool that expresses the chart; D3 only for bespoke. Mention SVG (stylable, few elements) vs canvas (fast, many points) rendering tradeoff, it shows depth.
- **Explain the D3 data-join** on a whiteboard: data array ⋈ DOM selection → enter/update/exit, keyed by identity. It's *the* D3 interview question and most candidates fumble it. You won't.
- **High-dim viz** is your edge, be ready to contrast PCA (linear/global) vs t-SNE (local/clusters, lies about distance) vs UMAP, and when each is honest. Ties directly to your resume.
- **Dashboards:** frame them as "saved queries + panels + refresh," cite Grafana, mention coordinated views and pre-aggregation for latency. That's senior-level framing.

## Exercises

Solutions follow.

1. **(Easy)** A bar chart's y-axis runs 90→100 and bar A is twice as tall as bar B. The underlying values are 98 and 96. What's the Lie Factor (roughly), and what's the fix?
2. **(Easy)** Rank these channels for reading a precise magnitude, best to worst: color hue, position on a common scale, area, length.
3. **(Easy)** You have 12 unordered categories to color. What kind of palette, and what's the practical limit before it fails?
4. **(Medium)** Translate a pie chart into the grammar of graphics (data, geom, coordinate system). What single change turns it into a stacked bar?
5. **(Medium)** In the D3 mini-project, why does removing the key function `d => d.label` cause bars to "teleport" their labels on update?
6. **(Medium)** Your colleague reads "cluster X is far from cluster Y" off a t-SNE plot. Why is this likely wrong, and what would you use instead?
7. **(Hard)** You must show 8 numeric variables for 2,000 rows on one screen. Propose two encodings (one per chart) and state each one's failure mode.
8. **(Hard)** Design the panel layout and *coordination* for a NYX "account health" dashboard with: balance over time, spend-by-category, and three KPIs. What drives the latency budget?

### Solutions

1. The *data* effect is `98/96 ≈ 1.02` (a 2% difference). The *shown* effect is `2.0` (bar A twice as tall, because lengths are measured from the truncated 90 baseline: `(98-90)/(96-90)=8/6≈1.33` for the visible portions, and a careless reader sees ~2× the *ink*). Lie Factor ≈ shown/data ≈ well above 1. **Fix:** start the axis at 0 so bar length is proportional to value; the bars then look nearly equal, which is the truth.

2. Best → worst: **position on a common scale → length → area → color hue.** (Cleveland-McGill ranking.)

3. A **categorical/qualitative** palette (distinct hues, not a ramp). Practical limit is **~7-8 distinguishable colors**; with 12 you can't reliably tell them apart, so reduce categories (group the long tail into "Other"), or use small multiples / direct labels instead of color.

4. **Data** = a table of category + value; **geom** = bar (`geom_bar`); **stat** = each bar's length = its share; **coordinate system** = *polar*. A pie is a single stacked bar wrapped around a circle. **Change polar → Cartesian** and you get a stacked bar chart (and unsorting it into separate bars upgrades the reader from angle to length judging).

5. Without a key, D3 joins data to DOM **by array index**. So "the 2nd `<rect>`" is always bound to "the 2nd data item," even if that item is now a *different* category. On update, an existing bar keeps its position but gets new data → its label/height jumps to whatever item lands at that index, instead of the *same identity* smoothly transitioning. The key `d => d.label` makes D3 track identity, so bar "B" stays "B" (updates), missing ones exit, new ones enter.

6. t-SNE optimizes only **local** neighbor preservation; it actively distorts **global** distances and cluster sizes/gaps to fit everything in 2D. So "far apart" on a t-SNE plot carries no real meaning, and it changes with the `perplexity` and random seed. Use **UMAP** (better global structure) or **PCA** (linear, distances meaningful) if you need to reason about how far apart groups are.

7. **(a) Scatterplot matrix (SPLOM):** all 28 pairwise scatters. *Failure mode:* 8 vars = 28 panels, each tiny, readable but near the upper limit; overplotting with 2,000 points needs alpha/density. **(b) Parallel coordinates:** 8 vertical axes, each row a polyline. *Failure mode:* story depends on axis order (only adjacent axes show their relation clearly) and 2,000 lines overplot into a smear, needs alpha, brushing, or sampling. (Bonus honest answer: **UMAP/PCA to 2D** colored by a key variable, *failure mode:* you lose the original axes' interpretability.)

8. **Layout (F-pattern):** KPI strip across the top (current balance, monthly spend, savings rate), the eye lands top-left; **balance-over-time line** as the hero panel below the KPIs (left/full width); **spend-by-category sorted bar** beside or beneath it. **Coordination:** a shared date-range selector and a category filter drive *all* panels (one filter → re-query all). **Latency budget** is set by the slowest panel query, drive it down with **pre-aggregated monthly rollups** (don't sum raw transactions on every load; see **07.2** indexing and **09.2** caching), and cache the KPI numbers.

## Teach it back

1. **State the Cleveland-McGill channel ranking and use it to justify "sorted bars beat a pie."** (Position > length > angle > area > color for magnitude. A pie asks for angle-judging (#4); a sorted bar upgrades the reader to length (#3) and adds a position-along-common-scale ordering. More accurate perception, same data.)

2. **Explain the grammar of graphics and decompose a bubble chart and a pie chart into its parts.** (Data + aesthetics/encodings + geom + scales + stats + facets + coords. Bubble = `geom_point` with size mapped; pie = `geom_bar` + `coord_polar`. Charts are compositions, not menu items.)

3. **Walk through D3's enter/update/exit on a whiteboard and explain the key function.** (Join data array against current DOM selection → enter = create, update = modify, exit = remove. The key `d => d.id` makes the join track *identity* not index, so elements transition smoothly and don't teleport.)

4. **When do you pick Chart.js vs Recharts vs D3, and why?** (Highest-level tool that expresses the chart. Chart.js = quick vanilla canvas; Recharts = declarative SVG in React, wired to app state (NYX); D3 = bespoke/novel/custom, the only tool deep enough for the unusual. Canvas = fast/many points; SVG = stylable/interactive/fewer elements.)

5. **Contrast PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP for visualizing high-dimensional data, including what each *lies* about.** (PCA: linear, fast, global distances meaningful, only linear structure. t-SNE: nonlinear, great clusters, but global distances/gaps meaningless and stochastic. UMAP: nonlinear, faster, keeps more global structure, modern default. Always caption which you used.)

6. **Give three ways to make a chart honest and three to make it accessible.** (Honest: zero baseline for bars, no dual axes, Lie Factor ≈ 1 / no area-for-1D. Accessible: never color alone (add shape/label), colorblind-safe palette like viridis, real alt/title/ARIA text and sufficient contrast.)
