# Chapter 16.4 - Performance Optimization & Profiling

> Goal: Turn "it's slow" from a vibe into a *measurement*, and turn "make it fast" from heroic guessing into a *workflow*. We'll derive Amdahl's law so you know the ceiling before you touch a line, profile a real slow Python script to find the one function eating 95% of the time, and then climb the speedup ladder, algorithm, vectorize, cache, go native, parallelize, documenting each win with numbers. By the end, "10-100x faster" is a repeatable engineering process, not luck. If your resume says throughput, optimization, and HPC, this chapter makes you able to *defend* every one of those words.

The discipline has one commandment that everything else hangs off: **measure before you optimize.** Smart people spend a day micro-tuning a function that ran 0.1% of the time. This chapter is the antidote: find the truth with a profiler, attack the actual hot path, and prove the win.

---

## Start here - the on-ramp (read this even if you're brand new)

Imagine your program is a relay race with a hundred runners, and you want the *team* to finish faster. The naive instinct is "make everyone train harder." But here's the thing: if one runner takes 9 minutes and the other 99 take 1 second *combined*, training the 99 fast runners is a waste, the team's time is dominated by that one slow runner. **The entire art of performance is finding the slow runner and *only* fixing them.**

That slow runner has a name: the **bottleneck**, or **hot path**, the small fraction of code where the program actually spends its time. The tool that finds it is a **profiler**, think of it as a stopwatch on every runner at once, telling you exactly who's slow. The cardinal sin of optimization is *guessing* who the slow runner is. Humans are catastrophically bad at this guess; the slow part is almost never where you think. So we measure.

Three words to demystify up front:
- **Latency** = how long *one* request/operation takes (the time for one runner to finish their leg).
- **Throughput** = how many operations finish *per second* (how many relay teams cross the line per minute). These are different goals and sometimes you trade one for the other.
- **Profiling** = measuring where time/memory actually goes, so you optimize facts not feelings.

And one law to anchor everything: if a part of your program is *inherently sequential*, it can't be sped up or parallelized, then no amount of effort on the rest can get you past it. That's **Amdahl's law**, and it sets the ceiling before you start. We'll derive it below.

Keep this picture: **measure → find the hot path → understand *why* it's slow → apply the cheapest fix that moves it → measure again.** Loop until fast enough (not infinitely, "fast enough" is a real, defined target). Everything below is detail on that loop.

## The discipline: measure first, and know your ceiling

### The optimization workflow

Performance work is a loop, and skipping steps is how you waste days:

1. **Set a target.** "p95 under 200ms" or "process 1M rows in <10s." Without a target you optimize forever. *Fast enough* is an engineering decision, not "as fast as possible."
2. **Measure the baseline.** Time it, profile it. Write the number down.
3. **Find the hot path.** The profiler tells you where the time *actually* goes. Trust it over intuition.
4. **Form a hypothesis.** *Why* is the hot function slow? Algorithm? Allocation? I/O wait? Python overhead?
5. **Apply the cheapest fix that addresses the cause.** Cheapest = least code/risk for the win.
6. **Re-measure.** Did it help? By how much? Keep the win or revert.
7. **Repeat** until you hit the target. Then *stop*, premature and *excessive* optimization both cost maintainability.

Donald Knuth's full quote (always half-quoted): *"Premature optimization is the root of all evil. Yet we should not pass up our opportunities in that critical 3%."* The discipline is finding that 3% with a profiler, ignoring the other 97%.

### Amdahl's law - derived

This is the law that tells you the *ceiling* before you spend a dollar. Say a fraction `p` of your program's runtime can be sped up (parallelized, or optimized away), and the remaining fraction `(1 - p)` is inherently serial and untouchable. If you speed up that parallel part by a factor `s`, the new total time, relative to an original time of 1, is:

$$T_{\text{new}} = \underbrace{(1 - p)}_{\text{serial, unchanged}} + \underbrace{\frac{p}{s}}_{\text{sped-up part}}$$

The overall speedup is old over new:

$$\boxed{\;\text{Speedup}(s) = \frac{1}{(1 - p) + \dfrac{p}{s}}\;}$$

Now the killer insight, take the limit as `s → ∞` (infinite cores, or you eliminate the hot part *entirely*):

$$\lim_{s \to \infty} \text{Speedup} = \frac{1}{1 - p}$$

**The serial fraction is a hard wall.** If 10% of your program is serial (`p = 0.9`), the *maximum* speedup is `1/0.1 = 10×`, even with infinite processors. If 50% is serial, you can never beat 2×, period. This is *why* step 3 matters so much: if you optimize a part that's only 20% of runtime, your ceiling is `1/0.8 = 1.25×` no matter how brilliant your fix. **Profile to find the *biggest* `p`, because Amdahl says the rest is capped.**

Worked example: a script spends 95% of its time in one function (`p = 0.95`). If you make that function 100× faster, speedup `= 1/(0.05 + 0.95/100) = 1/(0.05 + 0.0095) = 1/0.0595 ≈ 16.8×`. If you instead made it *infinitely* fast, ceiling `= 1/0.05 = 20×`. So 100× on the hot path already captures 84% of the theoretical max, diminishing returns kick in fast, which tells you where to *stop*.

(Its sibling, **Gustafson's law**, points out that with more compute we usually *grow the problem* rather than fix the same one, relevant to the HPC section, where the workload scales with the machine.)

### Algorithmic vs. micro optimization

Two completely different levers, and order matters:

- **Algorithmic**, change the *complexity class* (see **01.4**). Turning an O(n²) loop into an O(n log n) sort, or an O(n) membership scan into an O(1) set lookup. For large `n` this is the *only* thing that matters: no amount of micro-tuning saves an O(n²) algorithm on a million items. **Always exhaust algorithmic wins first.**
- **Micro**, make the same algorithm execute faster per operation: vectorize, cache, avoid allocations, use faster data structures, drop to C. This matters *after* the big-O is right, and is where the 2×-100× constant-factor wins live.

Rule of thumb: **fix the algorithm, then fix the constant factor.** A 1000× algorithmic win dwarfs a 2× micro win, but once you're at the best big-O, micro wins are real money.

## Profiling Python

You cannot optimize what you haven't measured. Here's the toolbox, from coarse to surgical.

### `timeit` - quick microbenchmarks

For "is A faster than B?" on a small snippet. It runs many loops and reports the best time, dodging noise:

```python
import timeit
timeit.timeit("sum(range(1000))", number=100_000)        # ~ baseline
timeit.timeit("[x for x in range(1000)]", number=100_000)
# In a notebook: %timeit sum(range(1000))
```

Use it to *settle a micro question*, never to find the hot path in a whole program.

### `cProfile` - the function-level map

The first thing to run on a slow script. It records every function call's cumulative and total time:

```python
import cProfile, pstats
cProfile.run("main()", "out.prof")
p = pstats.Stats("out.prof")
p.sort_stats("cumulative").print_stats(15)   # top 15 by cumulative time
# or from the shell, no code change:
#   python -m cProfile -s tottime slow.py
```

Read two columns: **`tottime`** = time *in that function itself* (excluding callees), this is your hot spot; **`cumtime`** = time in the function *and everything it calls*, this shows the expensive call *chains*. The function with the biggest `tottime` is where the CPU actually burns. Visualize the call tree with **`snakeviz out.prof`**, a flame-graph-style sunburst that makes the hot path obvious at a glance.

### `line_profiler` - which *line* is slow

cProfile points at a function; `line_profiler` points at the **exact line**. Decorate the suspect function with `@profile` and run with `kernprof`:

```python
# pip install line_profiler
@profile                       # injected by kernprof, no import needed
def hot(data):
    total = 0
    for row in data:           # line_profiler shows % time PER LINE
        total += expensive(row)
    return total
# kernprof -l -v slow.py
```

The output's "% Time" column tells you the single line eating the function. This is the surgical step right before you write the fix.

### `py-spy` - profile a *running* process, no code changes

`py-spy` is a *sampling* profiler that attaches to an already-running Python process (including production!) without restarting or instrumenting it:

```bash
pip install py-spy
py-spy top --pid 12345          # live, htop-style view of hot functions
py-spy record -o flame.svg --pid 12345   # generate a flame graph
py-spy dump --pid 12345         # snapshot the stack of a hung process
```

It's the go-to for "the prod service is slow *right now*" and for catching a hang (the `dump` shows exactly where it's stuck). Sampling = low overhead, so it's production-safe, unlike cProfile's per-call instrumentation.

### Memory profiling

Time isn't the only resource. To find *memory* hot spots:
- **`tracemalloc`** (stdlib), snapshot allocations and diff them to find what's growing (great for leak hunting):
  ```python
  import tracemalloc
  tracemalloc.start()
  run_workload()
  for stat in tracemalloc.take_snapshot().statistics("lineno")[:10]:
      print(stat)               # top 10 lines by allocated memory
  ```
- **`memory_profiler`**, line-by-line memory like line_profiler for time (`@profile` + `mprof run`).
- **`memray`** (Bloomberg's), the modern, powerful one: flame graphs of allocations, tracks native allocations too.

Watch for the classic Python memory sins: holding the whole dataset in a list when a generator would stream it, and accidental quadratic memory from concatenating in a loop.

## Python-specific speedups

Once the profiler names the hot path, here are the highest-use Python fixes, roughly in order of payoff.

### Vectorization (the biggest single Python win)

Python loops are slow because every iteration pays interpreter overhead (bytecode dispatch, boxing/unboxing objects). **NumPy/pandas push the loop into compiled C** that runs over a contiguous array with no per-element Python overhead, often **10-100× faster** for numeric work:

```python
import numpy as np
xs = np.random.rand(10_000_000)

# Slow: Python loop, interpreter overhead per element
total = 0.0
for x in xs: total += x*x            # seconds

# Fast: vectorized, the loop runs in C
total = np.sum(xs**2)                # tens of milliseconds - ~100x
```

The rule: **if you're writing a `for` loop over a numeric array, you're probably leaving 50× on the table.** Express it as an array operation instead. (This is also the conceptual on-ramp to SIMD, see HPC below.)

### Better data structures (algorithmic, but cheap to apply)

The single most common real-world slowdown you'll find: **membership testing in a list.** `x in some_list` is O(n); `x in some_set` is O(1). Swapping one to the other on a hot loop is frequently a 100×-10000× win for free (see **02.4** hash tables):

```python
# O(n) per lookup -> O(n*m) total. Disaster on big inputs.
seen = []
for x in stream:
    if x not in seen: seen.append(x)
# O(1) per lookup -> O(n) total.
seen = set()
for x in stream:
    if x not in seen: seen.add(x)
```

Also: `dict`/`defaultdict` for grouping (instead of repeated scans), `collections.deque` for O(1) ends (instead of `list.pop(0)` which is O(n)), `heapq` for top-k (instead of full sorts), `bisect` for sorted insertion.

### Caching / memoization

If a pure function is called repeatedly with the same arguments, *remember the answer*. `functools.lru_cache` turns exponential recomputation into linear lookup:

```python
from functools import lru_cache
@lru_cache(maxsize=None)
def fib(n):
    return n if n < 2 else fib(n-1) + fib(n-2)
# Without the cache: O(2^n). With it: O(n). For fib(40), seconds -> microseconds.
```

This is computation caching; the *systems*-level cousin (Redis, CDN) is in **09.2**.

### Generators / laziness

Don't materialize a giant list if you only iterate once. Generators stream one item at a time, O(1) memory instead of O(n), and they let the pipeline start producing immediately:

```python
# Builds a 10M-element list in RAM first
squares = [x*x for x in range(10_000_000)]; total = sum(squares)
# Streams - constant memory, no giant allocation
total = sum(x*x for x in range(10_000_000))      # note: () not []
```

For data pipelines this is the difference between fitting in memory and an OOM crash.

### Avoiding quadratic patterns

The sneakiest slowdowns are accidental O(n²):
- **String concatenation in a loop** (`s += piece`) rebuilds the whole string each time → O(n²). Use `"".join(pieces)`.
- **Building a list with repeated `in` checks** (the dedup example above).
- **Nested loops over the same data** when a dict/set lookup would flatten one loop away (the classic two-sum: O(n²) brute force → O(n) with a seen-dict).

Profilers surface these as a function whose time grows faster than the input, but knowing the patterns lets you spot them in review before they ship.

## Concurrency for throughput

When a single optimized core isn't enough, do *more at once*. The first question, covered in depth in **05.2**, is **what is the work waiting on?**

- **I/O-bound** (waiting on network, disk, DB): the CPU is *idle* during the wait. Run other work in that gap. Use **`asyncio`** (one thread, cooperative; thousands of concurrent waits, lowest overhead) or **threads** (`ThreadPoolExecutor`). The GIL doesn't hurt you because the bottleneck is waiting, not computing.
- **CPU-bound** (number crunching, parsing, compression): the CPU is *busy*. Python's **GIL** means threads can't run Python bytecode in parallel, so threading gives ~no speedup. Use **`multiprocessing`** (separate processes, separate interpreters, true parallelism across cores), or go native (next section), which releases the GIL.

The decision table:

| Workload | Tool | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Many network/DB calls | `asyncio` | cheap concurrency, no GIL pain (it's waiting) |
| Moderate I/O, simple code | `ThreadPoolExecutor` | threads, easy, GIL released during I/O |
| Heavy CPU on multiple cores | `multiprocessing` / `ProcessPoolExecutor` | dodges the GIL via separate processes |
| Heavy CPU, want one process | NumPy/Numba/Cython | releases the GIL in C, uses all cores |

```python
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor, ProcessPoolExecutor
# I/O-bound: threads overlap the waits
with ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=32) as ex:
    results = list(ex.map(fetch_url, urls))
# CPU-bound: processes use all cores
with ProcessPoolExecutor() as ex:
    results = list(ex.map(crunch, chunks))
```

The throughput payoff is Amdahl again: parallelism only helps the *parallelizable* fraction, and inter-process communication (pickling data between processes) is overhead that can eat your gains if the chunks are too small. Profile to confirm the win.

## Going native

When vectorization isn't enough and you need C speed on Python-shaped code:

- **NumPy**, already covered: vectorized C under a Python array API. The first and easiest native win.
- **Numba**, a JIT compiler. Add `@njit` to a numeric function and it compiles to machine code on first call, often matching C, *and releases the GIL* so `@njit(parallel=True)` uses all cores. Almost free for tight numeric loops:
  ```python
  from numba import njit
  @njit
  def monte_carlo_pi(n):
      inside = 0
      for _ in range(n):                 # this loop is now compiled, not interpreted
          x, y = np.random.random(), np.random.random()
          if x*x + y*y <= 1.0: inside += 1
      return 4.0 * inside / n             # 50-200x over pure Python
  ```
- **Cython**, write Python with C type annotations (`cdef int i`), compile to a C extension. More work than Numba but more control; powers pandas and scikit-learn internals.
- **C extensions / `ctypes` / `cffi` / PyO3 (Rust)**, drop the hottest kernel into actual C/Rust and call it from Python. Maximum speed, maximum effort. Reach for this only when profiling proves a single kernel dominates and nothing higher-level suffices (ties to **05.1** on what "native" memory layout buys you).

The intuition: **Python is glue; the hot kernel should be compiled.** Most fast Python is a thin Python script orchestrating compiled NumPy/Numba/C kernels.

## Systems-level performance

Beyond a single script, the throughput levers live in the architecture (cross-links to the system-design chapters):

- **Throughput vs. latency (09.1).** Throughput = ops/sec; latency = time per op. Often a tradeoff: **batching** improves throughput (amortize fixed per-op overhead across many items) but *adds* latency (each item waits for the batch to fill). Pick based on the SLA, a real-time API optimizes latency; a nightly ETL optimizes throughput.
- **Batching.** Instead of 1000 single DB inserts (1000 round trips), one bulk insert of 1000 rows (1 round trip). Same data, a fraction of the overhead. Applies to DB writes, API calls, GPU work, log flushing, anywhere fixed per-call cost dominates.
- **Connection pooling.** Opening a DB/HTTP connection is expensive (TCP + TLS handshake). A pool keeps warm connections and hands them out, eliminating per-request setup. Always pool DB connections in a service.
- **DB query optimization (07.2).** Often the *real* bottleneck in a web app isn't the code, it's an unindexed query or an N+1 pattern (one query per row in a loop). Add the index, batch the queries, and a "slow service" becomes fast without touching the language. Profile the DB (EXPLAIN) as well as the code.
- **Caching (09.2).** The fastest work is work you don't do. Cache expensive computed results / DB reads in memory or Redis; serve static assets from a CDN. Layered with `lru_cache` above, same idea at different scales.

The senior move: **before optimizing code, check whether the bottleneck is actually I/O, the DB, or the network.** A profiler that shows your service spending 90% of its time `await`-ing the database means the answer is an index or a cache, not a faster loop.

## Benchmarking honestly - how to not fool yourself

Half of "optimizations" don't actually help; they just look like they do because the measurement was sloppy. The rules for a benchmark you can *trust*:

- **Warm up first.** The first run pays one-time costs, imports, JIT compilation (Numba/PyPy), cold caches, lazy initialization. Discard it and measure steady state, or your "before" number is inflated and your "after" looks magically better.
- **Run many times and report a distribution, not one number.** A single timing is noise. Report the **minimum** (the run least disturbed by the OS scheduler, closest to the "true" cost) plus the **median** and spread. `timeit` reports the best of many loops for exactly this reason.
- **Hold everything else constant.** Same machine, same data, same load, no Spotify indexing in the background. Compare like with like or the comparison is meaningless.
- **Measure the thing you care about.** If your goal is *throughput* (ops/sec under load), a single-call microbenchmark can mislead, measure under realistic concurrency. If it's *p95 latency*, an average hides the tail. Measure the metric that matches the target.
- **Beware the optimizer deleting your benchmark.** In compiled languages (and even with Numba), if a result is unused the compiler may remove the whole computation, you'll "measure" near-zero. Always *use* the result (print it, accumulate it).
- **Validate correctness, not just speed.** A 100× speedup that returns the wrong answer is a 100× faster bug. Assert the fast version matches the slow version's output before celebrating.

```python
import time, statistics
def bench(fn, *args, warmup=2, repeats=11):
    for _ in range(warmup): fn(*args)              # discard cold runs
    ts = sorted(_one(fn, *args) for _ in range(repeats))
    return {"min": ts[0], "median": statistics.median(ts), "max": ts[-1]}
def _one(fn, *a):
    t = time.perf_counter(); r = fn(*a); return time.perf_counter() - t
# compare AND verify identical output:
assert slow(data) == fast(data), "fast version changed the answer!"
print("slow", bench(slow, data)); print("fast", bench(fast, data))
```

The discipline: **a speedup isn't real until it's measured honestly, repeatedly, on the right metric, with the output verified.** "It feels faster" and "the first run was quicker" are how people ship regressions.

## A brief intro to HPC / large-scale compute

If your resume lists HPC and throughput, here's the honest map of when you actually need the big iron, and the vocabulary to defend it.

- **SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data).** Modern CPUs have vector units (AVX, NEON) that apply one instruction to 4/8/16 numbers *at once*. Vectorized NumPy already exploits this under the hood, that's *part* of why `np.sum(xs**2)` crushes a Python loop. SIMD is data-parallelism inside one core.
- **GPUs.** Thousands of simple cores built for *massive* data-parallelism, the same operation over millions of elements (matrix multiplies, convolutions). This is why deep learning (**14.2**) lives on GPUs: training is mostly big matmuls. Tools: CUDA, CuPy (NumPy-on-GPU), JAX, PyTorch. A GPU helps when the work is *embarrassingly parallel* and arithmetic-heavy; it's useless for branchy, sequential logic.
- **Parallel vs. distributed.** *Parallel* = many cores/GPUs in **one machine** (shared memory). *Distributed* = many **machines** over a network (no shared memory, you pay communication cost). Distributed frameworks: MPI (classic HPC), Dask / Ray / Spark (Python-friendly, see **16.x** big data).
- **Job schedulers (Slurm).** On a real HPC cluster you don't `ssh` and run things, you *submit a job* describing the resources you need (nodes, cores, GPUs, time limit, memory) and **Slurm** queues it, allocates nodes when free, and runs it. It's the OS scheduler (**05.3**) scaled to a whole cluster of machines shared by many users:
  ```bash
  #!/bin/bash
  #SBATCH --nodes=4 --ntasks-per-node=8 --gres=gpu:2 --time=02:00:00
  srun python train.py            # Slurm runs this across the allocated nodes
  ```
- **When do you *actually* need HPC?** Only when the problem genuinely doesn't fit on a beefy single machine, training large models, scientific simulation, genomics, datasets in the terabytes. **Most "slow" problems are solved by Amdahl + a profiler + vectorization + one good index, *not* by a cluster.** The expensive mistake is reaching for distributed compute (with all its networking and coordination overhead) before exhausting the single-machine wins. Distributed compute has its *own* Amdahl tax: the serial coordination and the network shuffles cap your scaling. Scale up (bigger box) before you scale out (more boxes).

## The hardware reality: why memory layout beats cleverness

Two algorithms with the *same* big-O can differ 10× in practice because of how the CPU actually fetches data, and ignoring this is why a "theoretically optimal" rewrite sometimes runs *slower*. The key facts (ties to **05.1 memory**):

- **The memory hierarchy is a cliff, not a slope.** Reading from a CPU register is ~1 cycle; L1 cache ~4 cycles; L2 ~12; main RAM ~200+; SSD ~100,000+; network/disk millions. Each level down is roughly an *order of magnitude* slower. Performance is overwhelmingly about *keeping the data the CPU needs in the fast levels*.
- **The CPU fetches whole cache lines (~64 bytes), not single values.** When you read one array element, the 64 bytes around it come along for free. So **sequential access** (walking an array in order) is fast, the next element is already cached, while **random access** (chasing pointers around the heap, as in a linked list) constantly misses and pays the full RAM penalty.
- **This is why arrays crush linked lists in practice** even when both are O(n): the array is contiguous (cache-friendly, prefetchable), the linked list scatters nodes across the heap (cache-hostile). It's also why NumPy's contiguous arrays beat lists-of-objects, and why "struct of arrays" often beats "array of structs" for numeric work.
- **Locality of reference** is the principle: code that touches data close together in time (temporal) and space (spatial) runs far faster. Block your loops, keep hot data contiguous, prefer flat arrays over pointer-chasing structures on hot paths.

The practical takeaway: when two implementations tie on big-O, **the one with better memory locality wins**, often by a lot. This is the layer below "vectorize", vectorization is fast partly *because* it streams contiguous memory in cache-friendly order. You rarely hand-tune cache behavior in Python (NumPy does it for you), but knowing it explains *why* the vectorized version is fast and guides your data-structure choices.

## Build it - a profiling harness

A tiny reusable pattern to measure any function honestly: warm up, time repeatedly, report best and median (best dodges noise; median catches variance).

```python
import time, statistics
from contextlib import contextmanager

@contextmanager
def timed(label):
    t0 = time.perf_counter()           # perf_counter: monotonic, high-res
    yield
    print(f"{label:<24} {time.perf_counter()-t0:8.4f}s")

def bench(fn, *args, repeats=7):
    times = []
    for _ in range(repeats):
        t0 = time.perf_counter(); fn(*args); times.append(time.perf_counter()-t0)
    print(f"{fn.__name__:<24} best={min(times):.4f}s  median={statistics.median(times):.4f}s")

with timed("whole pipeline"):
    run_everything()
```

Always report the *number*, before and after. "It feels faster" is not an engineering claim; "it went from 4.2s to 0.04s, a 105× win" is.

## Mini-project: take a slow program from baseline to 10 - 100× faster

Here's a deliberately slow script and the *documented* climb. The task: given two big lists of integer IDs, for each ID in `a`, count how many times it appears in `b`, then sum the squares of the counts.

**Step 0, the slow baseline (commit the number).**
```python
import random, time
random.seed(0)
a = [random.randint(0, 50_000) for _ in range(20_000)]
b = [random.randint(0, 50_000) for _ in range(200_000)]

def slow():
    total = 0
    seen = []                                  # SIN 1: list for membership
    for x in a:
        if x in seen:                          # O(n) membership in a loop
            continue
        seen.append(x)
        count = 0
        for y in b:                            # SIN 2: rescans all of b per id -> O(|a|*|b|)
            if y == x:
                count += 1
        total += count * count
    return total

t0 = time.perf_counter(); r = slow(); print("slow:", r, f"{time.perf_counter()-t0:.3f}s")
# -> slow: <result>   ~14.0s    (BASELINE)
```

**Step 1, profile it.** `python -m cProfile -s tottime slow.py` (or wrap `slow()` in `cProfile.run`). The profiler screams that ~all the time is the inner `for y in b` loop, that's the hot path. Amdahl: that loop is essentially 100% of runtime, so fixing it is uncapped.

**Step 2, fix the algorithm (the big-O win).** The inner loop is recomputing counts from scratch. **Count `b` once** with a hash map, then look up each id in O(1). This collapses O(|a|·|b|) → O(|a|+|b|):
```python
from collections import Counter
def fast_algo():
    counts = Counter(b)                        # one O(|b|) pass, builds id -> count
    total = 0
    for x in set(a):                           # set() dedups in O(|a|), no O(n) membership
        c = counts[x]                          # O(1) lookup
        total += c * c
    return total
# -> ~0.03s   ~460x over baseline. The algorithmic win dominates everything.
```
That single change, recognizing the quadratic and replacing it with a hash count, is the 100×+ jump. **This is why algorithm-first.**

**Step 3, squeeze the constant factor (vectorize the arithmetic).** If the counts are dense and numeric, push the final reduction into NumPy:
```python
import numpy as np
def fast_vectorized():
    counts = np.bincount(np.array(b), minlength=50_001)   # C-level counting over the range
    uniq = np.unique(np.array(a))                          # dedup in C
    c = counts[uniq]                                       # fancy-index lookup
    return int((c * c).sum())                              # vectorized square + sum
# -> ~0.008s   another ~4x on top, ~1700x total. Now the bottleneck is just building arrays.
```

**Step 4, measure, and know when to stop.** Tabulate the climb:

| Version | Time | Speedup vs. baseline | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| `slow` | ~14.0 s | 1× | O(\|a\|·\|b\|) + list membership |
| `fast_algo` | ~0.030 s | ~460× | hash count, set dedup → O(\|a\|+\|b\|) |
| `fast_vectorized` | ~0.008 s | ~1700× | NumPy `bincount`/`unique`, C reduction |

The lesson made concrete: **the algorithmic fix delivered the 460×; vectorization added a 4× constant-factor polish on top.** If you'd started by micro-tuning the inner loop (e.g., `@njit` on the O(n²) version), Amdahl would have capped you, you'd have made a quadratic algorithm 50× faster and still been crushed by a million ID lookups. *Profile → fix the big-O → then the constant factor → then stop.* We stop here because we've blown past any reasonable target; chasing the last 8ms isn't worth the maintainability cost.

## Common mistakes

- **Optimizing without profiling.** You'll tune the wrong 97%. Measure first, every time.
- **Ignoring Amdahl.** Speeding up a part that's 20% of runtime caps you at 1.25×. Find the *biggest* slice.
- **Micro-optimizing past an O(n²) algorithm.** No constant-factor win beats fixing the complexity class on large inputs.
- **`x in list` on a hot path.** O(n) per check; use a `set`/`dict` for O(1). The most common real-world Python slowdown.
- **String `+=` in a loop.** Accidental O(n²); use `"".join`.
- **Threads for CPU-bound Python.** The GIL means no speedup. Use multiprocessing or native code.
- **Reaching for a cluster too early.** Scale *up* (bigger box, vectorize, index) before scaling *out*. Distributed compute adds network + coordination overhead that often erases the gain.
- **Not re-measuring.** "It should be faster" isn't a measurement. Prove every win with numbers.
- **Optimizing forever.** "Fast enough" is a real target. Stop when you hit it; further tuning costs maintainability.

## On the job / interview angle

- **"This endpoint is slow, what do you do?"** The answer is the *workflow*: reproduce, measure (profile the code *and* the DB), find the hot path, hypothesize the cause, fix the cheapest thing, re-measure. Mentioning "I'd check if it's I/O-bound (DB/network) before assuming it's the code" reads as senior.
- **Derive Amdahl's law** and use it to explain why "we added 16 cores and only got 2× faster" (the serial fraction was large). This is a classic systems question.
- **I/O-bound vs CPU-bound** + the GIL is *the* Python concurrency interview question. Know `asyncio` vs threads vs multiprocessing cold (ties to **05.2**).
- **Name the speedup ladder:** algorithm → data structure → vectorize → cache → native → parallelize. Showing you go top-down (algorithm first) signals you won't waste effort.
- **Throughput vs latency + batching** is a system-design staple (**09.1**), be ready to explain the tradeoff and when batching *hurts* (latency-sensitive paths).
- **HPC honesty:** know SIMD/GPU/Slurm vocabulary, but signal judgment by saying *most* problems are solved single-machine; you reach for HPC only when the data genuinely doesn't fit. That maturity is the differentiator.

## Exercises

Solutions follow.

1. **(Easy)** A program spends 80% of its time in a function you can parallelize. What's the maximum possible speedup, even with infinite cores?
2. **(Easy)** You make a function 50× faster, but it was only 4% of total runtime. What's the overall speedup?
3. **(Easy)** Name the right concurrency tool for: (a) 10,000 HTTP GETs, (b) resizing 10,000 images on an 8-core box, (c) one heavy numeric loop you want on all cores in one process.
4. **(Medium)** A loop does `result += str(x)` for a million `x`. Why is it O(n²), and what's the O(n) fix?
5. **(Medium)** cProfile shows function `f` has tiny `tottime` but huge `cumtime`. What does that tell you, and where do you look next?
6. **(Medium)** You replace `x in big_list` with `x in big_set` inside a loop over n items, where the list/set has m items. Give the before/after total complexity.
7. **(Hard)** A service is "slow," but profiling shows 92% of wall-clock time is `await db.query(...)`. The code is already vectorized. What are your top 3 fixes, and why isn't a faster algorithm one of them?
8. **(Hard)** You profiled and found a single numeric kernel is 95% of runtime. Walk the speedup ladder: which technique at each rung, and where does Amdahl tell you to stop?

### Solutions

1. `p = 0.8`, ceiling `= 1/(1-p) = 1/0.2 = 5×`. Even infinite cores can't beat 5× because 20% is serial.

2. Speedup `= 1/((1-0.04) + 0.04/50) = 1/(0.96 + 0.0008) = 1/0.9608 ≈ 1.04×`. A ~4% improvement, Amdahl punishes optimizing a tiny slice. You optimized the wrong thing.

3. (a) **`asyncio`** (or a thread pool), I/O-bound, the GIL is released while waiting, cheap concurrency. (b) **`multiprocessing`/`ProcessPoolExecutor`**, CPU-bound, dodge the GIL across 8 cores. (c) **Numba `@njit(parallel=True)`** (or NumPy), releases the GIL in compiled code and uses all cores within one process.

4. Python strings are immutable, so `result += str(x)` allocates a *new* string and copies all previous characters every iteration. After k iterations the string has ~k characters, so the copies cost `1+2+...+n ≈ n²/2` → **O(n²)**. Fix: append pieces to a list and `"".join(pieces)` once at the end → **O(n)**.

5. Small `tottime`, large `cumtime` means `f` itself does little work but the things it *calls* are expensive, `f` is a wrapper/orchestrator, not the hot spot. Look at `f`'s **callees**: re-sort by `tottime`, or use snakeviz to follow the call tree down to the function with the big *self* time. Optimize *that* leaf, not `f`.

6. Before: each `x in big_list` is O(m), done for n items → **O(n·m)**. After: each `x in big_set` is O(1) average, done for n items → **O(n)** (plus O(m) once to build the set). For n=m=10⁵ that's 10¹⁰ → 10⁵ operations: a ~100,000× win.

7. The bottleneck is the **database, not the Python**. Top fixes: **(1) add/repair an index** so the query stops scanning (run EXPLAIN, see **07.2**); **(2) eliminate N+1 queries** by batching into one query / join instead of one-per-row; **(3) cache** the hot read in Redis or in-memory (**09.2**) so most requests skip the DB entirely. Connection pooling helps too. A faster *algorithm* won't help because almost no time is spent in CPU/algorithm, it's spent *waiting on I/O*; Amdahl says optimizing the 8% of CPU work caps you near 1.09×.

8. `p = 0.95`. **Rung 1, algorithm:** is the kernel itself an avoidable O(n²)? Fix that first (uncapped). **Rung 2, vectorize:** rewrite the loop as NumPy array ops (often 50-100× via C + SIMD). **Rung 3, cache/memoize** any repeated sub-computation. **Rung 4, native:** `@njit`/Cython/C if NumPy can't express it. **Rung 5, parallelize:** multiprocessing/GPU across the now-fast kernel. **Where to stop:** Amdahl's ceiling is `1/0.05 = 20×`. Making the kernel 100× faster already yields `1/(0.05+0.0095) ≈ 16.8×`, 84% of the max. Past that, further kernel speedup barely moves the needle (the serial 5% dominates), so you stop and, if you need more, attack the serial 5% instead.

## Teach it back

1. **State the optimization workflow and explain why "measure first" is non-negotiable.** (Target → baseline → profile to find the hot path → hypothesize the cause → cheapest fix → re-measure → repeat until target. Humans guess the bottleneck wrong almost always; the profiler is ground truth, and Amdahl says effort off the hot path is capped.)

2. **Derive Amdahl's law and state the ceiling for a 90%-parallel program.** (`Speedup = 1/((1-p)+p/s)`; as `s→∞` it's `1/(1-p)`. For p=0.9, max speedup = 10×, even with infinite cores, the serial 10% is a wall.)

3. **Contrast algorithmic vs micro optimization, and state the order.** (Algorithmic changes the big-O, O(n²)→O(n log n), and dominates for large n; micro speeds up the constant factor, vectorize, cache, native. Always fix the algorithm first, then the constant factor.)

4. **Walk the Python profiling toolbox from coarse to surgical.** (cProfile = function-level map, `tottime` is self-time; line_profiler = which line; py-spy = sample a *running* process with no code change; tracemalloc/memray = memory. snakeviz visualizes the call tree.)

5. **Explain I/O-bound vs CPU-bound and the right tool for each, including the GIL's role.** (I/O-bound waits on network/disk, use asyncio/threads, GIL released during the wait. CPU-bound is busy computing, the GIL blocks parallel Python threads, so use multiprocessing or native code that releases the GIL.)

6. **When do you actually need HPC, and what's the order of escalation?** (Only when the problem genuinely doesn't fit a single beefy machine, large model training, simulation, terabyte data. Escalate: vectorize/SIMD → multi-core → GPU → multi-machine (Slurm/MPI/Dask). Scale *up* before *out*; distributed adds network + coordination overhead with its own Amdahl tax.)
