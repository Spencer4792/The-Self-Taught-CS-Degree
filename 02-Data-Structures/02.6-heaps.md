## Why we keep coming back to heaps

The question that motivates this chapter: what data structure lets you repeatedly grab the smallest (or largest) thing in a collection, fast, while still letting you add new things cheaply?

A sorted list gives you the minimum in O(1) (it's at the front), but inserting costs O(n) because you have to shift everything to keep it sorted. An unsorted list inserts in O(1) (append to the end), but finding the minimum costs O(n) because you have to scan the whole thing. Both are lopsided.

A **binary heap** splits the difference:

| Operation        | Sorted array | Unsorted array | **Binary heap** |
| ---------------- | ------------ | -------------- | --------------- |
| `peek` (min)     | O(1)         | O(n)           | **O(1)**        |
| `insert`         | O(n)         | O(1)           | **O(log n)**    |
| `extract_min`    | O(n)         | O(n)           | **O(log n)**    |
| `build` from n   | O(n log n)   | O(n)           | **O(n)**        |

Two O(log n) operations and an O(1) peek. Whenever you need a **priority queue** ("always give me the most urgent task next"), a heap is almost always the answer. Dijkstra's shortest paths, Huffman coding, event simulation, the OS scheduler, heapsort, streaming top-k... all heaps underneath.

The key idea is that a heap is a **tree that secretly lives in a flat array**: no node objects, no pointers, just arithmetic on indices. Let's build up to why that works.

---

## Start here: the emergency-room triage analogy

Before any math, let's anchor the idea with something you can picture; every later section makes more sense once this clicks.

Imagine a hospital emergency room. Patients keep streaming in (a sprained ankle, a heart attack, a paper cut, a broken arm) in random order. The one rule that matters: the next patient seen is always the most urgent one currently waiting. Not the one who arrived first (that's a regular queue), and not the one whose name comes first alphabetically.

How does the triage nurse run this? She does not keep a perfectly sorted list of every patient ranked 1st, 2nd, 3rd, down to last. That would be exhausting: every time someone new walked in, she'd have to find their exact rank and shuffle the whole list to slot them in. And she doesn't need a full ranking. She only ever cares about one thing at a time: who's the most urgent person right now? Everybody below the top can stay loosely organized.

That "loosely organized, but the most urgent is instantly on top" arrangement is a heap. The trade you'll keep making in this chapter:

> You give up knowing the exact full order, and in exchange you get the extreme element (most urgent / smallest / largest) instantly, plus cheap inserts.

**Why not just keep it fully sorted?** A fully sorted waiting list means every new arrival forces work proportional to the list's length: find the slot, shift everyone over. That's the O(n) insert we complained about up top. The heap's insight is that sorting more than you need is wasted effort. You only need the top, so you maintain just enough structure to keep the top correct.

**The tournament-bracket picture (the other half of the intuition).** Think of a single-elimination tournament drawn as a pyramid. Each match's winner moves up to play the next round, and the champion sits at the top of the bracket. A min-heap is the same shape, with "wins" meaning "is smaller":

```
                champion (smallest)
                /              \
          small-ish        small-ish
          /     \           /      \
       bigger  bigger    bigger   bigger
```

Every parent "beat" its children (is smaller, for a min-heap). The smallest value bubbled all the way up to the root by winning every comparison on its path. And (this trips people up) two players in different branches of the bracket were never compared to each other. The runner-up isn't necessarily the second-smallest overall; it's just whoever the champion beat last. That's exactly why a heap is *not* a sorted list: siblings and cousins are unranked relative to each other. The heap only promises the champion is on top.

When you `insert`, it's like a new player walking into the bracket and fighting their way *up* only as far as they keep winning (that's **sift-up**). When you `extract_min`, the champion leaves, you grab some leaf to temporarily fill the throne, and it fights its way back *down* until it's sitting where it belongs (**sift-down**). Both walks are along a single root-to-leaf path, which is why both cost only O(log n): the bracket is only `log₂ n` rows tall.

Keep both pictures in your head for the rest of the chapter:
- **Triage nurse** → "always grab the most urgent thing, don't waste effort fully sorting."
- **Tournament bracket** → "the extreme value bubbles to the top by winning a chain of comparisons; everything else stays loosely ranked."

Everything below (the index arithmetic, the two sift operations, the O(n) build proof) just makes these two pictures precise and fast.

## The shape: a complete binary tree

A **binary heap** is first and foremost a *complete binary tree*. Let's nail down what "complete" means, because everything else depends on it.

A **complete binary tree** is one where:

1. Every level is **completely filled**, _except possibly the last_.
2. The last level is filled **left to right**, with no gaps.

So it fills in like reading a book: top to bottom, left to right. Here's one with 9 nodes:

```
                 (0)
               /     \
            (1)       (2)
           /   \     /   \
         (3)   (4) (5)   (6)
        /  \
      (7)  (8)
```

The labels in parentheses are the order in which the nodes were filled, and that ordering is the secret. Notice there are no holes: you can't put a node at the right child of (3)'s sibling while (3) still has an empty slot. The "left to right, no gaps" rule is what makes the array trick work.

**Why completeness matters for height.** A complete binary tree with `n` nodes has height `h = ⌊log₂ n⌋`. Intuitively: level `k` (0-indexed from the top) holds up to `2^k` nodes, so the first `h` full levels hold `1 + 2 + 4 + ... + 2^(h-1) = 2^h − 1` nodes. To get more nodes than that, you need an `(h)`th level. So `n` nodes force the height to grow only logarithmically. **This is the source of every O(log n) in this chapter:** the tree is never taller than `log₂ n`.

## The array trick: deriving the index arithmetic

Here's the move that makes heaps so cheap. Because the tree is *complete* (no gaps), you can lay the nodes out in an array in exactly the fill order, level by level, left to right, and the parent/child relationships become pure arithmetic.

Let's write the indices on the same tree:

```
index:   0
        / \
       1   2
      / \ / \
     3  4 5  6
    / \
   7   8

array:  [ A0, A1, A2, A3, A4, A5, A6, A7, A8 ]
index:     0   1   2   3   4   5   6   7   8
```

Now let's **derive** the formulas instead of memorizing them. Look at node `0`. Its children are `1` and `2`. Node `1`'s children are `3` and `4`. Node `2`'s children are `5` and `6`. Node `3`'s children are `7` and `8`. Let's tabulate:

| node `i` | left child | right child |
| -------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| 0        | 1          | 2           |
| 1        | 3          | 4           |
| 2        | 5          | 6           |
| 3        | 7          | 8           |

Stare at the left-child column: 0→1, 1→3, 2→5, 3→7. That's `1, 3, 5, 7`, the odd numbers, which is `2i + 1`. The right child is always one more: `2i + 2`.

**Why `2i + 1`?** In fill order, children get placed in the same order as their parents: node 0's children first, then node 1's, and so on. Each of the `i` nodes before node `i` claims exactly two child slots, and the very first child slot in the array is index 1 (node 0's left child). So node `i`'s children land at `1 + 2i` and `2 + 2i`:

$$\text{left}(i) = 2i + 1, \qquad \text{right}(i) = 2i + 2.$$

And the parent? If `left = 2i + 1`, then `i = (left − 1) / 2`. If `right = 2i + 2`, then `i = (right − 2) / 2 = (right − 1) / 2` after integer floor. Both children collapse to the same formula with floor division:

$$\text{parent}(i) = \left\lfloor \frac{i - 1}{2} \right\rfloor.$$

Let's verify: parent of 7 is `(7−1)//2 = 3` ✓. Parent of 8 is `(8−1)//2 = 3` ✓. Parent of 5 is `(5−1)//2 = 2` ✓. Parent of 6 is `(6−1)//2 = 2` ✓. 

So you never store a single pointer; the tree structure is implicit in the index math. That's why heaps are so cache-friendly and memory-light: it's just one contiguous array.

```python
def parent(i: int) -> int:
    return (i - 1) // 2

def left(i: int) -> int:
    return 2 * i + 1

def right(i: int) -> int:
    return 2 * i + 2

# quick sanity check against the tree above
assert left(0) == 1 and right(0) == 2
assert left(3) == 7 and right(3) == 8
assert parent(7) == 3 and parent(8) == 3
assert parent(5) == 2 and parent(6) == 2
print("index arithmetic OK")
```

> **One-indexed variants exist.** Some textbooks store the heap starting at index 1 (leaving index 0 empty), which makes the math `left = 2i`, `right = 2i+1`, `parent = i//2`, slightly prettier. We're using **0-indexed** because it matches Python's `heapq` and avoids wasting a slot. Just be consistent.

## The heap property (the ordering invariant)

Shape is half the story. The other half is the **heap property**, which controls how values relate to each other.

For a **min-heap**:

> **Min-heap invariant:** for every node `i` (other than the root), `heap[parent(i)] <= heap[i]`.

In words: **every parent is ≤ both of its children.** This propagates: the root is ≤ everything below it, so the global minimum is always at index 0. That's why `peek` is O(1).

A **max-heap** flips the comparison: every parent is `>=` its children, so the maximum sits at the root. Min-heap and max-heap are mirror images; everything you derive for one works for the other by swapping `<` for `>`.

Here's a valid min-heap:

```
            1
          /   \
         3      2
        / \    / \
       7   4  5   9
array: [1, 3, 2, 7, 4, 5, 9]
```

Check it: 1 ≤ 3 and 1 ≤ 2 ✓; 3 ≤ 7 and 3 ≤ 4 ✓; 2 ≤ 5 and 2 ≤ 9 ✓. Invariant holds everywhere.

**One subtlety:** the heap property is weak. It says nothing about siblings or cousins: `3` and `2` are out of left-to-right order, and that's fine. A heap is **not** a sorted array. It only guarantees the min is on top. This weakness is exactly why it's cheaper to maintain than a fully sorted structure: you only ever fix one vertical path, never the whole thing.

## Build it: sift-up (bubble-up) and insert

When you insert, you want to keep both invariants, shape *and* ordering. The shape rule "fill left to right" tells you exactly where the new element goes: **append it to the end of the array** (the next open slot in the last level). That preserves completeness instantly.

But appending might violate the *ordering* invariant, the new value could be smaller than its parent. So you **sift it up**: compare it with its parent, and if it's smaller, swap. Repeat until either it's no longer smaller than its parent, or it reaches the root.

Intuition with a picture. Insert `0` into the heap above:

```
Append 0 at index 7 (child of index 3 = value 7):
            1
          /   \
         3      2
        / \    / \
       7   4  5   9
      /
     0           <- 0 < 7, swap up

After swaps: 0<7 → 0<3 → 0<1 → 0 is now root.
            0
          /   \
         1      2
        / \    / \
       3   4  5   9
      /
     7
```

The element climbs a single root-to-leaf path. Since the tree's height is `⌊log₂ n⌋`, sift-up does **at most `log₂ n` swaps**, so insert is **O(log n)**.

```python
class MinHeap:
    def __init__(self):
        self._data = []

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self._data)

    def peek(self):
        if not self._data:
            raise IndexError("peek from empty heap")
        return self._data[0]

    def _sift_up(self, i):
        # Bubble element at index i toward the root while it's
        # smaller than its parent. Restores the min-heap property
        # after appending a new element at the end.
        while i > 0:
            p = (i - 1) // 2
            if self._data[i] < self._data[p]:
                self._data[i], self._data[p] = self._data[p], self._data[i]
                i = p
            else:
                break

    def insert(self, value):
        self._data.append(value)          # keep completeness
        self._sift_up(len(self._data) - 1) # restore ordering
```

## Build it: sift-down (bubble-down) and extract_min

`extract_min` is the trickier sibling. The minimum is at index 0, so returning it is easy, but then you have a hole at the root, and you must restore *both* invariants.

The clever standard trick:

1. Save `heap[0]` (the answer).
2. Move the **last** element into index 0 and shrink the array by one. This keeps the tree complete (you removed the last leaf, which is the only one you're allowed to remove without leaving a gap).
3. That moved value is probably too big to be the root, so **sift it down**: compare with its children, swap with the **smaller** child (must be the smaller one, or you'd break the invariant on the other side), and repeat until it's ≤ both children or it becomes a leaf.

```
extract_min from:
            0
          /   \
         1      2
        / \    / \
       3   4  5   9
      /
     7

1) answer = 0
2) move last (7) to root, drop it from the end:
            7
          /   \
         1      2
        / \    / \
       3   4  5   9
3) sift down: children of 7 are {1,2}; smaller is 1 → swap
            1
          /   \
         7      2
        / \    / \
       3   4  5   9
   children of 7 (now idx 1) are {3,4}; smaller is 3 → swap
            1
          /   \
         3      2
        / \    / \
       7   4  5   9
   7 is now a leaf → done.  Min-heap restored. Returned 0.
```

Sift-down also walks one root-to-leaf path, so it's **O(log n)**, making `extract_min` **O(log n)**.

> **Why swap with the *smaller* child?** The new parent must end up ≤ *both* children. If you swapped with the larger child, the smaller child would now be greater than the new parent, invariant broken. Swapping with the smaller child guarantees the promoted value is ≤ its new sibling.

```python
    def _sift_down(self, i):
        n = len(self._data)
        while True:
            l, r = 2 * i + 1, 2 * i + 2
            smallest = i
            if l < n and self._data[l] < self._data[smallest]:
                smallest = l
            if r < n and self._data[r] < self._data[smallest]:
                smallest = r
            if smallest == i:
                break  # neither child is smaller -> heap property holds
            self._data[i], self._data[smallest] = (
                self._data[smallest],
                self._data[i],
            )
            i = smallest

    def extract_min(self):
        if not self._data:
            raise IndexError("extract_min from empty heap")
        top = self._data[0]
        last = self._data.pop()      # remove last leaf
        if self._data:               # if heap not now empty,
            self._data[0] = last     # move it to root
            self._sift_down(0)        # and restore ordering
        return top
```

We'll fold this into the full class below, but first let's handle the most interesting operation: building a heap from a pile of unsorted data all at once.

## Build-heap, and the proof that it's O(n)

Suppose you already have `n` values in an array, in any order, and you want to turn the whole array into a valid heap. The naive way: insert them one at a time, `n` inserts × O(log n) each = **O(n log n)**.

But there's a slicker way, **Floyd's build-heap**, that runs in O(n), truly linear:

> Start from the **last non-leaf node** and sift-down each node, walking *backwards* to the root.

```python
    @classmethod
    def build_heap(cls, values):
        h = cls()
        h._data = list(values)        # O(n) copy
        n = len(h._data)
        # last non-leaf is the parent of the last element:
        start = (n - 1 - 1) // 2      # parent(n-1)
        for i in range(start, -1, -1):
            h._sift_down(i)
        return h
```

**Why start at `parent(n-1)`?** Every index past the last non-leaf is a *leaf*, and a single leaf is already a trivially valid heap (no children to violate anything). So you skip all `~n/2` leaves and only fix the internal nodes, working bottom-up so that when you sift-down node `i`, both its subtrees are already valid heaps.

### The proof it's O(n) - the converging series

The lazy analysis says: there are `n/2` internal nodes, each sift-down costs O(log n), so O(n log n). That bound is correct but loose, because most nodes are near the bottom, where sift-down is cheap.

A sift-down from a node at **height `h`** (height measured from the *bottom*: leaves are height 0) costs at most `h` swaps, it can only fall as far as the bottom of its subtree. So you need to sum, over all nodes, the height of each node.

In a complete tree of `n` nodes, the number of nodes at height `h` is at most `⌈n / 2^(h+1)⌉`. (Leaves, height 0: about `n/2` of them. Height 1: about `n/4`. Height 2: about `n/8`. Each level up has half as many nodes.) So the total work is bounded by:

$$
T(n) \;\le\; \sum_{h=0}^{\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor} \frac{n}{2^{\,h+1}} \cdot h
\;=\; \frac{n}{2} \sum_{h=0}^{\infty} \frac{h}{2^{\,h}}.
$$

Now you just need that infinite series. There's a classic identity:

$$\sum_{h=0}^{\infty} \frac{h}{2^{h}} = 2.$$

Let's convince ourselves it converges to 2. Start from the geometric series sum, for `|x| < 1`:

$$\sum_{h=0}^{\infty} x^{h} = \frac{1}{1-x}.$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to `x`:

$$\sum_{h=1}^{\infty} h\,x^{h-1} = \frac{1}{(1-x)^2}.$$

Multiply both sides by `x`:

$$\sum_{h=1}^{\infty} h\,x^{h} = \frac{x}{(1-x)^2}.$$

Plug in `x = 1/2`:

$$\sum_{h=1}^{\infty} \frac{h}{2^{h}} = \frac{1/2}{(1/2)^2} = \frac{1/2}{1/4} = 2.$$

(The `h=0` term is 0, so including or excluding it doesn't matter.) Therefore:

$$
T(n) \;\le\; \frac{n}{2} \cdot 2 \;=\; n \;=\; O(n).
$$

**Build-heap is linear.** The intuition to keep: nearly half the nodes are leaves that cost nothing, a quarter cost at most 1, an eighth cost at most 2... the cheap-and-numerous nodes dominate, and the expensive root is just one node. The series converges, and the `log n` factor evaporates.

Let's sanity-check the series numerically before you trust it:

```python
# Empirically confirm sum_{h} h / 2^h -> 2
total = sum(h / (2 ** h) for h in range(0, 60))
assert abs(total - 2.0) < 1e-9, total
print("series sum(h/2^h) =", round(total, 12), "-> converges to 2  ✓")
```

### Traced walkthrough - build-heap bottom up

The proof tells you *why* it's O(n); a trace tells you *what actually happens*. Let's run Floyd's build-heap on `[5, 3, 8, 1, 2, 7]` by hand and watch the array (and its tree shape) transform. This is a **min-heap** build, so each sift-down pushes the *bigger* value down and pulls the smaller child up.

First, lay the array into its (currently invalid) tree shape, just the fill order, ignoring the heap property for now:

```
index:  0   1   2   3   4   5
array: [5,  3,  8,  1,  2,  7]

                 5            <- index 0
               /   \
             3       8        <- index 1, 2
            / \     /
           1   2   7          <- index 3, 4, 5
```

There are 6 elements. The last non-leaf is `parent(n-1) = parent(5) = (5-1)//2 = 2`. So you sift down indices `2, 1, 0`, backwards, bottom-up, and skip indices `3, 4, 5` (they're leaves, already valid single-node heaps).

**Step 1, sift-down index 2 (value 8).** Its children are index 5 (value 7) and index 6 (out of range). Smaller child is 7, and `7 < 8`, so swap them.

```
before:               after swap 8<->7:
        8                     7
       /                     /
      7                     8

index:  0   1   2   3   4   5        index:  0   1   2   3   4   5
array: [5,  3,  8,  1,  2,  7]  -->  array: [5,  3,  7,  1,  2,  8]

                 5                                   5
               /   \                               /   \
             3       8                           3       7
            / \     /                           / \     /
           1   2   7                           1   2   8
```

8 is now a leaf; subtree rooted at index 2 is a valid min-heap (`7 ≤ 8`). ✓

**Step 2, sift-down index 1 (value 3).** Its children are index 3 (value 1) and index 4 (value 2). Smaller child is 1, and `1 < 3`, so swap.

```
index:  0   1   2   3   4   5        index:  0   1   2   3   4   5
array: [5,  3,  7,  1,  2,  8]  -->  array: [5,  1,  7,  3,  2,  8]

                 5                                   5
               /   \                               /   \
             3       7                           1       7
            / \     /                           / \     /
           1   2   8                           3   2   8
```

3 lands at index 3, which is a leaf, done. Subtree at index 1 is now valid (`1 ≤ 3` and `1 ≤ 2`). ✓

**Step 3, sift-down index 0 (value 5).** This is the interesting one: it can fall more than one level. Children are index 1 (value 1) and index 2 (value 7). Smaller is 1, `1 < 5`, swap.

```
index:  0   1   2   3   4   5        index:  0   1   2   3   4   5
array: [5,  1,  7,  3,  2,  8]  -->  array: [1,  5,  7,  3,  2,  8]

                 5                                   1
               /   \                               /   \
             1       7                           5       7
            / \     /                           / \     /
           3   2   8                           3   2   8
```

5 is now at index 1. Keep sifting: its children are index 3 (value 3) and index 4 (value 2). Smaller is 2, `2 < 5`, swap.

```
index:  0   1   2   3   4   5        index:  0   1   2   3   4   5
array: [1,  5,  7,  3,  2,  8]  -->  array: [1,  2,  7,  3,  5,  8]

                 1                                   1
               /   \                               /   \
             5       7                           2       7
            / \     /                           / \     /
           3   2   8                           3   5   8
```

5 is now at index 4, a leaf, done. **Final heap:** `[1, 2, 7, 3, 5, 8]`. Let's verify the invariant: `1 ≤ 2`, `1 ≤ 7`, `2 ≤ 3`, `2 ≤ 5`, `7 ≤ 8`. All hold. ✓

```python
# Confirm the hand-trace matches the code
h = MinHeap.build_heap([5, 3, 8, 1, 2, 7])
assert h._data == [1, 2, 7, 3, 5, 8], h._data
assert h._is_valid()
print("build-heap trace matches:", h._data)
```

Notice how little work this was: leaves were free, index 2 cost one swap, index 1 cost one swap, and only the *root* cost two swaps. That lopsided "cheap-and-many at the bottom, expensive-but-rare at the top" pattern is the converging series made concrete.

### Traced walkthrough - heapsort moves the max to the back

The same intuition runs heapsort, except in a **max-heap** so the *largest* sits on top and gets evicted to the back. Let's trace the first few iterations of `heapsort([5, 3, 8, 1, 2, 7])`.

**Phase 1, build a max-heap in place.** Sifting indices `2, 1, 0` bottom-up with the *larger* child rising gives:

```
[5, 3, 8, 1, 2, 7]  --build max-heap-->  [8, 3, 7, 1, 2, 5]

                 8            <- max is on top
               /   \
             3       7
            / \     /
           1   2   5
```

(Quick check: `8 ≥ 3`, `8 ≥ 7`, `3 ≥ 1`, `3 ≥ 2`, `7 ≥ 5`. Valid max-heap.) The sorted region at the back is empty so far; we'll write it after a `|`.

**Iteration 1 (`end = 5`).** Swap root (8) with the last heap slot (index 5, value 5). 8 is now in its final, correct position at the very back. Shrink the heap to indices `0..4` and sift the new root (5) down within that region.

```
swap a[0]<->a[5]:   [5, 3, 7, 1, 2 | 8]      (| = sorted tail)
sift-down 5 in a[0:5]: children {3,7}, larger is 7, 7>5 -> swap
                    [7, 3, 5, 1, 2 | 8]
   5 now at idx 2, children {} -> leaf, stop.
heap region: [7, 3, 5, 1, 2]   sorted tail: [8]
```

**Iteration 2 (`end = 4`).** Root is now 7 (the next-largest). Swap it with index 4 (value 2). 7 takes its final spot just before 8. Shrink to `0..3`, sift 2 down.

```
swap a[0]<->a[4]:   [2, 3, 5, 1 | 7, 8]
sift-down 2 in a[0:4]: children {3,5}, larger is 5, 5>2 -> swap
                    [5, 3, 2, 1 | 7, 8]
   2 now at idx 2, children {} -> leaf, stop.
heap region: [5, 3, 2, 1]   sorted tail: [7, 8]
```

**Iteration 3 (`end = 3`).** Root is 5. Swap with index 3 (value 1). 5 takes its place. Shrink to `0..2`, sift 1 down.

```
swap a[0]<->a[3]:   [1, 3, 2 | 5, 7, 8]
sift-down 1 in a[0:3]: children {3,2}, larger is 3, 3>1 -> swap
                    [3, 1, 2 | 5, 7, 8]
   1 now at idx 1, children {} -> leaf, stop.
heap region: [3, 1, 2]   sorted tail: [5, 7, 8]
```

See the pattern: each iteration peels the current maximum off the top of the heap and parks it at the front of the growing sorted tail, which marches leftward. Two more iterations finish the job to `[1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8]`.

```python
# Confirm the partial traces and the final result
assert heapsort([5, 3, 8, 1, 2, 7]) == [1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8]
print("heapsort trace OK")
```

## Build it: the complete MinHeap class

Let's assemble everything into one clean, tested class.

```python
class MinHeap:
    """A binary min-heap stored in a flat list (0-indexed)."""

    def __init__(self):
        self._data = []

    # --- structural helpers ------------------------------------------
    def __len__(self):
        return len(self._data)

    def __bool__(self):
        return bool(self._data)

    def is_empty(self):
        return not self._data

    def peek(self):
        if not self._data:
            raise IndexError("peek from empty heap")
        return self._data[0]

    # --- the two engines ---------------------------------------------
    def _sift_up(self, i):
        while i > 0:
            p = (i - 1) // 2
            if self._data[i] < self._data[p]:
                self._data[i], self._data[p] = self._data[p], self._data[i]
                i = p
            else:
                break

    def _sift_down(self, i):
        n = len(self._data)
        while True:
            l, r = 2 * i + 1, 2 * i + 2
            smallest = i
            if l < n and self._data[l] < self._data[smallest]:
                smallest = l
            if r < n and self._data[r] < self._data[smallest]:
                smallest = r
            if smallest == i:
                break
            self._data[i], self._data[smallest] = (
                self._data[smallest],
                self._data[i],
            )
            i = smallest

    # --- public API ---------------------------------------------------
    def insert(self, value):
        self._data.append(value)
        self._sift_up(len(self._data) - 1)

    def extract_min(self):
        if not self._data:
            raise IndexError("extract_min from empty heap")
        top = self._data[0]
        last = self._data.pop()
        if self._data:
            self._data[0] = last
            self._sift_down(0)
        return top

    @classmethod
    def build_heap(cls, values):
        h = cls()
        h._data = list(values)
        n = len(h._data)
        for i in range((n - 2) // 2, -1, -1):
            h._sift_down(i)
        return h

    # --- a debug invariant checker (priceless for testing) -----------
    def _is_valid(self):
        n = len(self._data)
        for i in range(n):
            for c in (2 * i + 1, 2 * i + 2):
                if c < n and self._data[i] > self._data[c]:
                    return False
        return True
```

## The Priority Queue ADT

Step back from implementation. A **priority queue** is an *abstract data type*, a contract, defined by these operations:

| Operation             | Meaning                                         |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| `insert(item, prio)`  | add an item with a priority                     |
| `peek()` / `find_min` | look at the highest-priority item               |
| `pop()` / `extract`   | remove and return the highest-priority item     |
| `is_empty()` / `len`  | size queries                                    |
| `decrease_key`        | (optional) raise an item's priority             |

Notice the ADT says nothing about heaps; that's an implementation choice. You could back a priority queue with a sorted list, a balanced BST, a Fibonacci heap, etc. But the binary heap is the workhorse default because it hits O(log n) insert/pop with O(1) peek and a tiny constant factor, all in a single array.

The `MinHeap` above is already a priority queue where "priority" *is* the value. To attach priorities to arbitrary payloads, the standard idiom is to push `(priority, payload)` tuples: Python compares tuples lexicographically, so it orders by priority first. We'll lean on this in the mini-project.

## Build it: heapsort

Heapsort is the heap's party trick: sort an array **in place** in **O(n log n)** using nothing but heap operations.

The plan:
1. Build a **max-heap** out of the array in place, O(n) via Floyd's method (max-heap so the largest is at the root).
2. Repeatedly swap the root (the current max) with the last element of the heap region, then shrink the heap region by one and sift-down the new root. Each extracted max lands in its final sorted position at the back.

After `n` extractions, the array is sorted ascending, in place, no extra memory.

```python
def _sift_down_max(a, i, n):
    """Sift a[i] down within a[0:n], maintaining the MAX-heap property."""
    while True:
        l, r = 2 * i + 1, 2 * i + 2
        largest = i
        if l < n and a[l] > a[largest]:
            largest = l
        if r < n and a[r] > a[largest]:
            largest = r
        if largest == i:
            break
        a[i], a[largest] = a[largest], a[i]
        i = largest

def heapsort(a):
    """Sort list `a` ascending, in place, in O(n log n)."""
    n = len(a)
    # Phase 1: build a max-heap in place - O(n)
    for i in range((n - 2) // 2, -1, -1):
        _sift_down_max(a, i, n)
    # Phase 2: repeatedly move the max to the back - O(n log n)
    for end in range(n - 1, 0, -1):
        a[0], a[end] = a[end], a[0]   # max goes to its final slot
        _sift_down_max(a, 0, end)     # restore heap over a[0:end]
    return a
```

**Cost derivation.** Phase 1 is the linear build-heap we already proved is O(n). Phase 2 does `n − 1` extractions, each a sift-down costing O(log n), so O(n log n). The sum is `O(n) + O(n log n) = O(n log n)`, worst, average, and best case. Unlike quicksort, heapsort has no bad inputs.

> **Heapsort is NOT stable.** Equal elements can be reordered, because moving the last leaf to the root and sifting it down jumps elements across the array without regard to their original order. If stability matters, reach for mergesort. Heapsort's selling points are its guaranteed O(n log n) and O(1) extra space.

## Meeting `heapq`, now that you've earned it

Only *now*, having built the whole thing, do you let yourself use Python's standard library. `heapq` is a set of functions operating on a **plain list** as a **min-heap**, with exactly the index arithmetic you derived:

We'll use real `heapq` in the mini-project, but you understand every byte of what it's doing, because you built it.

## Mini-project (a): a Dijkstra-ready priority scheduler

Let's build a priority queue that:

- stores arbitrary tasks with numeric priorities (lower number = more urgent),
- pops the most urgent task,
- supports **`decrease_key`**, lowering a task's priority on the fly (essential for Dijkstra, where you relax an edge and need to bump a vertex's tentative distance down).

Binary heaps don't natively support "find and update an arbitrary element" cheaply: there's no fast way to locate a task inside the array. The two standard fixes:

1. **Lazy deletion (the `(priority, item)` tuple approach):** push a *new* entry with the better priority and mark the old one stale; skip stale entries when popping. Simple, and what most real Dijkstra code does.
2. **Indexed heap:** keep a `dict` mapping each item to its current array position, and update it on every swap, so `decrease_key` can `_sift_up` in O(log n).

We'll build the indexed version because it's the more instructive one and gives true O(log n) `decrease_key` with no stale buildup. (Lazy deletion is also fine, noted in a comment.)

```python
class PriorityQueue:
    """An indexed min-priority-queue supporting decrease_key in O(log n).

    Lower priority value = higher urgency (pops first).
    Each `task` must be hashable and unique.
    """

    def __init__(self):
        self._heap = []        # list of [priority, task]
        self._pos = {}         # task -> its index in self._heap

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self._heap)

    def __contains__(self, task):
        return task in self._pos

    def is_empty(self):
        return not self._heap

    # --- index-aware swap keeps self._pos in sync --------------------
    def _swap(self, i, j):
        self._heap[i], self._heap[j] = self._heap[j], self._heap[i]
        self._pos[self._heap[i][1]] = i
        self._pos[self._heap[j][1]] = j

    def _sift_up(self, i):
        while i > 0:
            p = (i - 1) // 2
            if self._heap[i][0] < self._heap[p][0]:
                self._swap(i, p)
                i = p
            else:
                break

    def _sift_down(self, i):
        n = len(self._heap)
        while True:
            l, r = 2 * i + 1, 2 * i + 2
            smallest = i
            if l < n and self._heap[l][0] < self._heap[smallest][0]:
                smallest = l
            if r < n and self._heap[r][0] < self._heap[smallest][0]:
                smallest = r
            if smallest == i:
                break
            self._swap(i, smallest)
            i = smallest

    # --- public API ---------------------------------------------------
    def add_task(self, task, priority):
        if task in self._pos:
            raise KeyError(f"task {task!r} already present; use decrease_key")
        self._heap.append([priority, task])
        i = len(self._heap) - 1
        self._pos[task] = i
        self._sift_up(i)

    def peek(self):
        if not self._heap:
            raise IndexError("peek from empty queue")
        prio, task = self._heap[0]
        return task, prio

    def pop_min(self):
        if not self._heap:
            raise IndexError("pop from empty queue")
        prio, task = self._heap[0]
        last = self._heap.pop()
        del self._pos[task]
        if self._heap:
            self._heap[0] = last
            self._pos[last[1]] = 0
            self._sift_down(0)
        return task, prio

    def decrease_key(self, task, new_priority):
        """Lower a task's priority (more urgent). O(log n)."""
        i = self._pos[task]            # O(1) lookup, no scanning needed
        if new_priority > self._heap[i][0]:
            raise ValueError("decrease_key cannot raise priority")
        self._heap[i][0] = new_priority
        self._sift_up(i)               # priority only went down -> sift up

    def add_or_decrease(self, task, priority):
        """Convenience for Dijkstra-style relaxation."""
        if task in self._pos:
            if priority < self._heap[self._pos[task]][0]:
                self.decrease_key(task, priority)
        else:
            self.add_task(task, priority)
```

### Using it for Dijkstra (the payoff)

With O(log n) `decrease_key` in hand, Dijkstra falls right out: relax each edge, shortening the neighbor's tentative distance in the queue:

```python
def dijkstra(graph, source):
    """graph: dict[node] -> list[(neighbor, weight)]. Returns dist dict."""
    INF = float("inf")
    dist = {v: INF for v in graph}
    dist[source] = 0
    pq = PriorityQueue()
    for v in graph:
        pq.add_task(v, dist[v])
    while not pq.is_empty():
        u, d = pq.pop_min()
        if d == INF:
            break  # remaining nodes unreachable
        for w, weight in graph[u]:
            nd = d + weight
            if nd < dist[w]:
                dist[w] = nd
                pq.add_or_decrease(w, nd)  # relaxation via decrease_key
    return dist

def test_dijkstra():
    g = {
        "A": [("B", 1), ("C", 4)],
        "B": [("C", 2), ("D", 5)],
        "C": [("D", 1)],
        "D": [],
    }
    assert dijkstra(g, "A") == {"A": 0, "B": 1, "C": 3, "D": 4}
    print("test_dijkstra: all passed ✓")

test_dijkstra()
```

Shortest path A→D is A→B→C→D = 1+2+1 = 4, and the test confirms it. The priority queue is doing all the heavy lifting.

## Mini-project (b): streaming top-k with a size-k min-heap

The setup: a **stream** of `n` numbers, possibly far too many to hold in memory, and you want the **k largest** of them. Sorting is out: it needs all `n` at once and costs O(n log n).

The elegant trick: keep a **min-heap of size at most `k`** holding the *k largest seen so far*. For each new element `x`:

- if the heap has fewer than `k` items, just push `x`;
- otherwise, compare `x` against the heap's **minimum** (the smallest of your current top-k). If `x` is larger, the current minimum no longer belongs in the top-k, so pop it and push `x`; if `x` is ≤ the minimum, ignore it.

**Why a *min*-heap to find the *largest* k?** Because the one element you might need to evict is the *smallest* among your current champions, and a min-heap puts exactly that element at the root for O(1) inspection. A max-heap would put the wrong element on top. The heap's root is your "bar to clear": anything that beats the smallest champion replaces it.

**Cost:** you process `n` elements; each does O(1) peek plus at most one push and one pop on a heap of size `k`, i.e. O(log k). Total **O(n log k)**, with only **O(k)** memory. When `k ≪ n`, that's dramatically better than O(n log n) sorting, and it streams: you never need all `n` in memory at once.

```python
import heapq

def top_k_stream(stream, k):
    """Return the k largest elements from an iterable, in O(n log k) time
    and O(k) space. Output is ascending (heap order drained)."""
    if k <= 0:
        return []
    heap = []  # min-heap of the current top-k
    for x in stream:
        if len(heap) < k:
            heapq.heappush(heap, x)
        elif x > heap[0]:                # x beats the weakest champion
            heapq.heapreplace(heap, x)   # pop min, push x, in one O(log k) op
    return sorted(heap)                  # the k largest, ascending


def test_top_k_stream():
    import random

    # basic correctness vs. brute force
    for _ in range(300):
        n = random.randint(0, 80)
        data = [random.randint(-100, 100) for _ in range(n)]
        k = random.randint(0, n + 3)
        got = top_k_stream(iter(data), k)             # iter() => true stream
        expected = sorted(data)[max(0, len(data) - k):]  # k largest, ascending
        assert got == expected, (data, k, got, expected)

    # k larger than the stream returns everything
    assert top_k_stream([3, 1, 2], 10) == [1, 2, 3]
    # k = 0 returns nothing
    assert top_k_stream([5, 4, 3], 0) == []
    # duplicates and a real "stream" (generator)
    def gen():
        for v in [5, 5, 1, 9, 9, 2, 9]:
            yield v
    assert top_k_stream(gen(), 3) == [9, 9, 9]

    print("test_top_k_stream: all passed ✓")

test_top_k_stream()
```

The 300-trial fuzz against the brute-force `sorted(data)[-k:]` is the real proof of correctness. And `top_k_stream(gen(), 3)` shows it works on a genuine generator, never materializing the whole sequence.

> **The mental model:** the size-k min-heap is a bouncer at an exclusive club of capacity k. The root is the least-cool person currently inside. Each newcomer is compared only to that person; if cooler, the bouncer swaps them in. O(1) to check the bar, O(log k) to do the swap.

## Common mistakes

These are the bugs you (and almost everyone) actually hit when writing heaps from memory under pressure. Each one corrupts the heap silently: the code runs, it just gives wrong answers.

1. **Sifting down toward the *larger* child in a min-heap.** The single most common heap bug. In a min-heap, `_sift_down` must swap with the **smaller** of the two children; in a max-heap, the **larger**. Swap with the wrong one and you re-break the invariant on the *other* side instantly. A good mental check: "in a min-heap, the smallest thing must end up on top, so the smallest of the three (parent + two kids) rises." If you ever copy a min-heap and mutate it into a max-heap, you have to flip *every* comparison, including the child-selection one, missing it here is a classic.

2. **Forgetting a heap is NOT sorted.** A valid heap guarantees *only* that each parent beats its children. Siblings, cousins, and anything across branches are **unordered**. So `heap[1]` is not "the second smallest," and iterating the array does **not** visit elements in sorted order. The only way to read a heap in sorted order is to repeatedly `extract_min` (which is exactly heapsort). It's easy to fool yourself by printing `heap._data` and expecting it sorted.

3. **Off-by-one in the parent/child index math (0- vs 1-indexed).** With **0-indexing** (what this chapter uses): `left = 2i+1`, `right = 2i+2`, `parent = (i-1)//2`. With **1-indexing**: `left = 2i`, `right = 2i+1`, `parent = i//2`. Mixing the two, e.g. writing `parent = i//2` in a 0-indexed heap, gives a *wrong but plausible* parent for many indices and corrupts the structure subtly. Pick one convention and never mix. Also: the last non-leaf for build-heap is `parent(n-1) = (n-2)//2`, not `n//2`.

4. **Assuming `heapq` is a max-heap.** Python's `heapq` is **min-only**. There is no `max` flag. For a max-heap, negate on the way in and out (`heappush(h, -x)`; `-heappop(h)`), or push `(-priority, item)` tuples. Forgetting this and calling `heappop` expecting the largest is a guaranteed wrong answer that *looks* like working code.

5. **Mishandling the empty / single-element case in extract.** `extract_min` pops the last element first, then, *only if the heap is still non-empty*, moves it to the root and sifts down. If you forget the "still non-empty" guard, you'll move a value you just popped back onto an empty list, or index `[0]` on nothing and crash. The single-element case is the sneaky one: pop returns the lone element, and the heap must end up truly empty, not holding a duplicate.

6. **Building with `n` inserts (O(n log n)) when `build_heap` is O(n).** If you already have all the data up front, calling `insert` in a loop is asymptotically worse than one Floyd `build_heap` / `heapq.heapify`. It still *works*, but you just threw away the chapter's headline result. Reach for `heapify` whenever the data is available all at once.

7. **Mutating the heap list directly.** Doing `heap.append(x)`, `heap.sort()`, `heap[i] = y`, or `heap.pop()` (from the end) on a `heapq`-managed list *without* going through the heap functions silently destroys the invariant. After any raw mutation the structure is no longer a heap, and the next `heappop` returns garbage. Only touch it through `heappush` / `heappop` / `heapreplace` / `heapify`.

8. **Comparing tuples whose payload isn't comparable on ties.** Pushing `(priority, payload)` works great, until two priorities tie, at which point Python falls back to comparing the *payloads*. If the payload is, say, a dict or a custom object with no `__lt__`, you get `TypeError: '<' not supported`. Fixes: insert a unique, always-comparable **tiebreaker** in the middle, `(priority, counter, payload)` with a monotonically increasing `counter` (this also makes ordering stable / FIFO on ties), or wrap the payload in a class with a defined ordering.

## On the job / interview angle

Heaps and priority queues are a **top-tier interview topic**, easily in the same league as hash maps and binary search for "shows up constantly." The reason: a huge family of problems reduces to *"repeatedly take the smallest/largest remaining thing"* or *"maintain the k best so far,"* and a heap turns those from O(n²) or O(n log n) into O(n log k) with O(k) memory. Interviewers love them because the naive solution is obvious and the heap solution is a clean, teachable upgrade, it tests whether you can *recognize* the pattern, not just code it.

Two patterns pay for themselves over and over; memorize these two and you'll solve most heap interview questions:

- **The size-k heap.** To track the k *largest*, keep a **min**-heap of size k (the root is your "bar to clear"); to track the k *smallest*, keep a **max**-heap of size k. Counter-intuitive but correct: see the streaming top-k mini-project for why.
- **The two-heaps trick.** To track a *median* (or any middle statistic) of a stream, keep a **max-heap of the lower half** and a **min-heap of the upper half**, balanced in size. The two roots straddle the median.

Common questions, with the reasoning you'd give out loud:

1. **Kth largest element in an array/stream.** Keep a **min-heap of size k**. Push each element; if the heap exceeds k, pop the min. After processing, the root *is* the kth largest (it's the smallest of the k largest). **O(n log k)** time, **O(k)** space, beats sorting's O(n log n) when k ≪ n, and it streams. (Quickselect gives O(n) average but doesn't stream and is harder to get right.)

2. **Merge k sorted lists.** Put the **head of each list** into a min-heap (k items). Repeatedly pop the smallest, append it to the output, and push the *next* element from the list that smallest came from. The heap always holds at most k candidates, one frontier per list. **O(N log k)** for N total elements, vs. O(Nk) for naive repeated scanning. This is the canonical "heap of heads" pattern.

3. **Top-k frequent elements.** First count frequencies with a hash map (O(n)). Then either (a) push `(count, item)` into a **min-heap of size k** to keep the k most frequent, O(n log k); or (b) **bucket sort** by frequency for O(n). Mention both: the heap answer is the expected one, the bucket answer is the "can you do better than n log k" follow-up.

4. **Find median from a data stream.** The flagship **two-heaps** problem. Max-heap `lo` holds the smaller half, min-heap `hi` holds the larger half; keep their sizes within 1 of each other. The median is `lo`'s root (odd total) or the average of the two roots (even total). Each `addNum` is **O(log n)**, `findMedian` is **O(1)**. You build exactly this in the Extra practice section below.

5. **Task scheduler / "least interval."** Given tasks with a cooldown, greedily run the **most frequent** available task each tick, a **max-heap on remaining counts**, plus a short cooldown queue holding tasks that are temporarily benched. The heap surfaces the highest-count task that's eligible. It's a greedy + heap combo; the heap is what makes "most frequent eligible task" cheap.

6. **Meeting rooms II (minimum rooms needed).** Sort meetings by start time, then sweep with a **min-heap of end times** of currently-running meetings. For each new meeting, if the earliest-ending meeting (the heap root) finishes by the time this one starts, pop it (reuse that room); always push the current meeting's end. The heap's **size** at any moment is the number of concurrent rooms; the max size is the answer. **O(n log n)**.

7. **Dijkstra / Prim framing.** Both are "repeatedly pull the cheapest frontier edge/vertex," textbook priority-queue jobs. Dijkstra pops the closest unsettled vertex and relaxes its edges (`decrease_key` or lazy-deletion pushes); Prim pops the cheapest edge crossing the cut. Knowing the heap is the engine, and that the lazy `(dist, node)` push pattern is what real code uses, is the expected depth.

8. **K closest points to the origin.** Same size-k pattern: keep a **max-heap of size k** keyed by (squared) distance, so the root is the *current worst* of your k closest; evict it whenever a closer point arrives. **O(n log k)**, **O(k)** space. The squared-distance trick (skip the `sqrt`) is a nice detail to mention.

The meta-skill the interviewer is checking: **can you spot when "k best" or "running middle" is the hidden structure of the problem?** If you find yourself about to sort repeatedly, or scan for a min/max inside a loop, that's the tell: reach for a heap.

```python
import heapq
import re
from collections import Counter


# 1. Valid-heap checker -------------------------------------------------
def is_min_heap(arr):
    """True iff arr is a valid 0-indexed min-heap. O(n) time, O(1) space.
    Only need to check each PARENT vs its (up to two) children."""
    n = len(arr)
    for i in range((n - 2) // 2 + 1):       # all internal nodes
        l, r = 2 * i + 1, 2 * i + 2
        if l < n and arr[i] > arr[l]:
            return False
        if r < n and arr[i] > arr[r]:
            return False
    return True

assert is_min_heap([1, 3, 2, 7, 4, 5, 9])      # the chapter's valid heap
assert not is_min_heap([1, 3, 2, 7, 4, 1, 9])  # 2 > child 1 -> invalid
assert is_min_heap([]) and is_min_heap([42])   # trivial cases


# 2. Kth largest (size-k MIN-heap) -------------------------------------
def kth_largest(nums, k):
    """Kth largest value. O(n log k) time, O(k) space.
    Keep a min-heap of the k largest; its root is the kth largest."""
    if k <= 0 or k > len(nums):
        raise ValueError("k out of range")
    heap = []
    for x in nums:
        if len(heap) < k:
            heapq.heappush(heap, x)
        elif x > heap[0]:
            heapq.heapreplace(heap, x)   # pop min, push x: one O(log k) op
    return heap[0]                        # smallest of the k largest

assert kth_largest([3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4], 2) == 5
assert kth_largest([3, 2, 3, 1, 2, 4, 5, 5, 6], 4) == 4


# 3. Kth smallest (size-k MAX-heap via negation) -----------------------
def kth_smallest(nums, k):
    """Kth smallest value. O(n log k), O(k) space.
    Mirror of #2: keep the k SMALLEST in a max-heap so the root is the
    largest of them -- which is exactly the kth smallest. heapq is min-only,
    so negate to simulate a max-heap."""
    if k <= 0 or k > len(nums):
        raise ValueError("k out of range")
    heap = []  # stores negated values; -heap[0] is the current largest kept
    for x in nums:
        if len(heap) < k:
            heapq.heappush(heap, -x)
        elif x < -heap[0]:               # x smaller than our worst-kept
            heapq.heapreplace(heap, -x)
    return -heap[0]

assert kth_smallest([3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4], 2) == 2
assert kth_smallest([7, 10, 4, 3, 20, 15], 3) == 7


# 4. Merge k sorted lists (heap of heads) ------------------------------
def merge_k_sorted(lists):
    """Merge k sorted lists into one. O(N log k) for N total elements.
    Heap entry: (value, list_index, elem_index) -- indices break ties and
    avoid comparing values that may be equal-but-not-further-comparable."""
    heap = []
    for li, lst in enumerate(lists):
        if lst:                              # seed with each list's head
            heapq.heappush(heap, (lst[0], li, 0))
    out = []
    while heap:
        val, li, ei = heapq.heappop(heap)
        out.append(val)
        if ei + 1 < len(lists[li]):          # push the next from that list
            heapq.heappush(heap, (lists[li][ei + 1], li, ei + 1))
    return out

assert merge_k_sorted([[1, 4, 5], [1, 3, 4], [2, 6]]) == [1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6]
assert merge_k_sorted([[], [], [1]]) == [1]
assert merge_k_sorted([]) == []


# 5. Top-k frequent words (ties: alphabetical) -------------------------
def top_k_words(text, k):
    """k most frequent words; ties broken alphabetically. O(n log k)-ish.
    Trick: we want HIGH count but, on a tie, LOW (earlier) word. A single
    size-k heap must put the 'weakest' candidate at the root, so order by
    (count ASC, word DESC) -> negate the word's sort key. Simpler & clear:
    count, then sort the distinct words by (-count, word) and slice."""
    words = re.findall(r"[a-z']+", text.lower())
    counts = Counter(words)
    # distinct words is usually small; full sort is fine and unambiguous
    ranked = sorted(counts.items(), key=lambda kv: (-kv[1], kv[0]))
    return [w for w, _ in ranked[:k]]

txt = "the cat the dog the bird a cat a dog a"
assert top_k_words(txt, 2) == ["a", "the"]   # both 3; 'a' < 'the'
assert top_k_words(txt, 3) == ["a", "the", "cat"]  # cat & dog tie at 2 -> cat


# 6. Running median (two heaps) ----------------------------------------
def running_medians(stream):
    """Median after each element. Each step O(log n); total O(n log n).
    lo = max-heap (negated) of the smaller half; hi = min-heap of larger.
    Keep len(lo) == len(hi) or len(lo) == len(hi)+1."""
    lo, hi = [], []   # lo holds negatives; -lo[0] is the max of lower half
    out = []
    for x in stream:
        if not lo or x <= -lo[0]:
            heapq.heappush(lo, -x)
        else:
            heapq.heappush(hi, x)
        # rebalance so sizes differ by at most 1, lo never smaller than hi
        if len(lo) > len(hi) + 1:
            heapq.heappush(hi, -heapq.heappop(lo))
        elif len(hi) > len(lo):
            heapq.heappush(lo, -heapq.heappop(hi))
        # read the median
        if len(lo) > len(hi):
            out.append(float(-lo[0]))
        else:
            out.append((-lo[0] + hi[0]) / 2)
    return out

assert running_medians([2, 1, 5, 7, 2, 0, 5]) == [2.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.5, 2.0, 2.0, 2.0]


# 7. Heapify-and-verify -------------------------------------------------
def heapify_and_check(arr):
    """Floyd build-heap in place, then assert validity & sorted drain."""
    a = list(arr)
    n = len(a)
    def sift_down(i):
        while True:
            l, r, sm = 2 * i + 1, 2 * i + 2, i
            if l < n and a[l] < a[sm]: sm = l
            if r < n and a[r] < a[sm]: sm = r
            if sm == i: break
            a[i], a[sm] = a[sm], a[i]
            i = sm
    for i in range((n - 2) // 2, -1, -1):
        sift_down(i)
    assert is_min_heap(a), a
    # draining a copy via heapq must equal sorted(arr)
    drain = a[:]
    out = [heapq.heappop(drain) for _ in range(len(drain))]
    assert out == sorted(arr)
    return a

heapify_and_check([5, 3, 8, 1, 2, 7])
print("exercise 7 OK")


# 8. Convert min-heap to max-heap --------------------------------------
def min_to_max_heap(arr):
    """Given a valid MIN-heap array, rearrange into a valid MAX-heap.
    KEY INSIGHT: an arbitrary array (even a min-heap) carries no useful
    head-start for the opposite order, so we just run Floyd build-heap with
    the comparison flipped. That's O(n) -- the same linear cost as any
    build-heap, NOT something cheaper just because it was already a heap."""
    a = list(arr)
    n = len(a)
    def sift_down_max(i):
        while True:
            l, r, lg = 2 * i + 1, 2 * i + 2, i
            if l < n and a[l] > a[lg]: lg = l
            if r < n and a[r] > a[lg]: lg = r
            if lg == i: break
            a[i], a[lg] = a[lg], a[i]
            i = lg
    for i in range((n - 2) // 2, -1, -1):
        sift_down_max(i)
    return a

def is_max_heap(a):
    n = len(a)
    for i in range((n - 2) // 2 + 1):
        for c in (2 * i + 1, 2 * i + 2):
            if c < n and a[i] < a[c]:
                return False
    return True

m = min_to_max_heap([1, 3, 2, 7, 4, 5, 9])
assert is_max_heap(m) and sorted(m) == [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9]


# 9. K closest numbers to a target -------------------------------------
def k_closest(nums, target, k):
    """k values nearest `target` by |x-target|. Ties prefer the SMALLER
    number. Size-k MAX-heap keyed by (distance, value) so the root is the
    current WORST kept; evict it when a better candidate arrives.
    O(n log k) time, O(k) space. Result sorted ascending."""
    if k <= 0:
        return []
    heap = []  # (-distance, -value) so heapq's MIN gives us the WORST kept
    for x in nums:
        key = (-abs(x - target), -x)   # bigger dist = worse; on tie, bigger x = worse
        if len(heap) < k:
            heapq.heappush(heap, key)
        elif key > heap[0]:            # x is BETTER than our current worst
            heapq.heapreplace(heap, key)
    return sorted(-v for _, v in heap)

assert k_closest([1, 2, 3, 4, 5], 3, 3) == [2, 3, 4]
assert k_closest([1, 2, 3, 4, 5], 3.5, 2) == [3, 4]
assert k_closest([1, 5, 5], 3, 2) == [1, 5]   # |1-3|=2 ties |5-3|=2 -> prefer 1
print("all exercise solutions passed ✓")
```

Every assertion holds when you run the block. The two patterns you'll want to be able to reproduce cold are #2/#3 (the size-k heap, and *which* polarity to use for largest vs. smallest) and #6 (the two-heap median balance), because those generalize to the largest cluster of interview questions.

## Extra practice: a `MedianFinder` stream class (two heaps)

The exercises had a *functional* running median; here let's build the **object** version, the exact shape a "design a data structure" interview asks for: a `MedianFinder` you feed one number at a time and query for the current median in O(1). It's the cleanest showcase of the **two-heaps** invariant, and it's distinct from both prior mini-projects (no Dijkstra, no fixed-size top-k).

The invariant you maintain at all times:

- `lo` is a **max-heap** (stored negated, since `heapq` is min-only) holding the **smaller half** of all numbers seen.
- `hi` is a **min-heap** holding the **larger half**.
- Every element of `lo` is ≤ every element of `hi` (the halves don't overlap).
- Sizes are balanced: `len(lo) == len(hi)` or `len(lo) == len(hi) + 1`.

With that invariant, the median is trivially readable: if `lo` is bigger, it's `lo`'s root; otherwise it's the average of the two roots. The work is all in `add_num`, which inserts then rebalances, each step touching only heap roots, so **O(log n)** per add, **O(1)** per query.

```python
import heapq


class MedianFinder:
    """Streaming median via two balanced heaps.
       add_num: O(log n)   find_median: O(1)   space: O(n)."""

    def __init__(self):
        self._lo = []  # max-heap (negated): the smaller half
        self._hi = []  # min-heap: the larger half

    def add_num(self, x):
        # 1. tentatively place x into the correct half
        if not self._lo or x <= -self._lo[0]:
            heapq.heappush(self._lo, -x)
        else:
            heapq.heappush(self._hi, x)
        # 2. rebalance so |len(lo) - len(hi)| <= 1, with lo >= hi in size
        if len(self._lo) > len(self._hi) + 1:
            heapq.heappush(self._hi, -heapq.heappop(self._lo))
        elif len(self._hi) > len(self._lo):
            heapq.heappush(self._lo, -heapq.heappop(self._hi))

    def find_median(self):
        if not self._lo:
            raise IndexError("median of empty stream")
        if len(self._lo) > len(self._hi):
            return float(-self._lo[0])           # odd count: middle is lo's max
        return (-self._lo[0] + self._hi[0]) / 2  # even count: average of straddlers

    # handy for tests: confirm the invariant actually holds
    def _invariant_ok(self):
        size_ok = len(self._lo) in (len(self._hi), len(self._hi) + 1)
        order_ok = (not self._lo or not self._hi) or (-self._lo[0] <= self._hi[0])
        return size_ok and order_ok
```

### Tests for `MedianFinder`

```python
def test_median_finder():
    mf = MedianFinder()

    # odd then even insertions, checking median + invariant each step
    seq      = [5,   3,    8,   1,    9,   2]
    expected = [5.0, 4.0,  5.0, 4.0,  5.0, 4.0]
    #            5   (3,5) (3,5,8) ...
    for x, want in zip(seq, expected):
        mf.add_num(x)
        assert mf._invariant_ok(), "two-heap invariant broke"
        assert mf.find_median() == want, (x, mf.find_median(), want)

    # cross-check against a brute-force median over a random stream
    import random, statistics
    mf = MedianFinder()
    seen = []
    for _ in range(500):
        x = random.randint(-1000, 1000)
        mf.add_num(x)
        seen.append(x)
        assert mf._invariant_ok()
        assert mf.find_median() == statistics.median(seen)

    # empty-stream guard
    try:
        MedianFinder().find_median()
        assert False, "expected IndexError on empty stream"
    except IndexError:
        pass

    print("test_median_finder: all passed ✓")


test_median_finder()
```


The 500-element cross-check against `statistics.median(seen)` is the real proof, for *every* prefix of a random stream, the two-heap median matches the textbook definition exactly. Let's trace the first few `add_num` calls to see the invariant doing its job:

```
add 5:  lo=[5]            hi=[]        -> median 5.0   (lo bigger)
add 3:  lo=[3]            hi=[5]       -> median 4.0   (avg of 3,5)
        (3 <= 5 so it went to lo's spot; rebalance moved 5 to hi)
add 8:  lo=[5,3]          hi=[8]       -> median 5.0   (lo bigger, root 5)
add 1:  lo=[3,1]          hi=[5,8]     -> median 4.0   (avg of 3,5)
add 9:  lo=[5,3,1]        hi=[8,9]     -> median 5.0   (lo bigger, root 5)
add 2:  lo=[3,2,1]        hi=[5,8,9]   -> median 4.0   (avg of 3,5)
```

(The lists show heap order, not sorted order; remember mistake #2. `lo` is negated internally; the logical values are shown.) Each step, the two roots `-lo[0]` and `hi[0]` are the two values straddling the middle of everything seen, which is exactly what a median needs. The trick: don't sort the stream, just keep the two halves balanced around the middle.

_Recap: a heap is a **complete binary tree** flattened into an **array**, where the **heap property** keeps the extreme value at the root.
