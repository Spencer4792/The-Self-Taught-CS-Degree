## Start here: cities, roads, and friendships

Before any formality, here's the picture in the plainest possible terms.

Imagine a map. Dots are cities; the lines connecting them are roads. That's a graph. The cities are things, the roads are relationships between things. You already navigate graphs every day: when Google Maps routes you home, it's walking a graph of intersections and streets.

Now swap the labels. Make the dots people and the lines friendships. Same structure, totally different domain: a social network. "Who are Alice's friends?" is just "which dots is Alice's dot connected to?" "Are these two people connected through a chain of mutual friends?" is "is there a path between their dots?"

This is why graphs matter so much: **a graph is the data structure for relationships.** The moment your problem is about how things connect (not a flat list of items, not a strict parent-child tree, but a messy web of connections) you're holding a graph, whether you named it or not.

Where do they show up? Honestly, everywhere once you start looking:

- **Maps & navigation**, cities/intersections (vertices) joined by roads (edges), often *weighted* by distance or travel time.
- **Social networks**, people (vertices) joined by friendships or follows (edges). Friendships go both ways (undirected); follows don't (directed).
- **The web**, pages (vertices) joined by hyperlinks (edges). Always directed: page A linking to B doesn't mean B links back. Google's original PageRank was pure graph math.
- **Dependencies**, tasks, software packages, or course prerequisites (vertices) joined by "must-come-before" arrows (edges). Directed, and ideally with no cycles.
- **Anything with a "connected to" relationship**, flights between airports, pipes in a network, neurons, molecules, family trees.

The mental model to keep is simple:

> **Vertices = the things. Edges = the relationships between the things.** Everything else in this chapter is precise vocabulary for what kind of things and what kind of relationships, plus two ways to store them in a computer.

With that picture locked in, let's make it precise.

## What even is a graph?

A graph is, in a sense, the most honest data structure. A list pretends everything is in a line. A tree pretends everything has exactly one parent. A graph drops the pretense: things connect to other things, in whatever pattern reality actually has.

Formally, a graph is a pair `G = (V, E)`:

- **V** is a set of **vertices** (also called **nodes**). Think of them as the "things": people, cities, web pages, neurons.
- **E** is a set of **edges**. Each edge is a connection between two vertices. Think of them as the "relationships": friendships, roads, hyperlinks, synapses.

All of graph theory is built on those two sets. Everything else is vocabulary describing *what kind* of vertices and edges we have.

Here's a handy picture to keep in mind. Five friends, some of whom know each other:

```
        Alice
       /     \
   Bob ------ Carol
     \         /
      Dave -- Eve
```

Here `V = {Alice, Bob, Carol, Dave, Eve}` and the edges are the lines: `{Alice-Bob, Alice-Carol, Bob-Carol, Bob-Dave, Carol-Eve, Dave-Eve}`. Six edges, five vertices.

---

## The vocabulary (learn this once, use it forever)

We'll front-load the terminology because every later chapter assumes it. Skim it now, and it'll click harder once we build code.

**Adjacency.** Two vertices are **adjacent** (or **neighbors**) if there's an edge between them. Alice and Bob are adjacent; Alice and Dave are not.

**Incidence.** An edge is **incident** to the two vertices it connects. The edge Alice-Bob is incident to Alice and to Bob.

**Degree.** The **degree** of a vertex is the number of edges touching it. `deg(Bob) = 3` (Alice, Carol, Dave). `deg(Alice) = 2`. There's a neat little theorem here: if you sum the degrees of *all* vertices, you get exactly `2|E|`, because every edge contributes +1 to each of its two endpoints. This is the **Handshaking Lemma**. We'll actually verify it in a test.


> **Intuition for the Handshaking Lemma:** imagine counting handshakes at a party by asking each person "how many hands did you shake?" and summing. You've counted every handshake *twice*, once from each side. So the total is even, and equals twice the number of handshakes.

**Path.** A **path** is a sequence of vertices where each consecutive pair is connected by an edge: `Alice → Bob → Dave → Eve`. The **length** of a path is the number of edges in it (3, here).

**Cycle.** A **cycle** is a path that starts and ends at the same vertex without repeating any edge: `Bob → Carol → Eve → Dave → Bob`. A graph with no cycles is **acyclic** (a tree is exactly a connected acyclic graph, that's why trees were a "special case" all along).

**Connected.** An undirected graph is **connected** if there's a path between *every* pair of vertices. Our friend graph is connected: you can reach anyone from anyone. If you removed Bob-Dave and Carol-Eve, then Dave and Eve (still joined to each other by Dave-Eve) would be cut off from the rest, and the graph would have two **connected components**.

**Dense vs sparse.** This one drives every performance decision later, so internalize it:

- The **maximum** number of edges in a simple undirected graph on `V` vertices is `V·(V−1)/2`, every vertex paired with every other. That's roughly `V²/2`.
- A graph is **dense** if `E` is close to that maximum (`E ≈ V²`).
- A graph is **sparse** if `E` is much smaller, often `E ≈ V` or `E ≈ V·log(V)`.

Real-world graphs are usually sparse. You have ~1000 Facebook friends, not 3 billion. Cities connect to a handful of neighbors, not every other city. This single fact is why adjacency lists win most of the time, but we're getting ahead of ourselves.

## The flavors of graphs

A graph isn't one thing; it's a family. Four dimensions matter:

### Directed vs undirected

In an **undirected** graph, edges have no direction. Friendship is symmetric: if Alice is friends with Bob, Bob is friends with Alice. The edge `{Alice, Bob}` is an unordered pair.

In a **directed** graph (a **digraph**), edges have direction. They're ordered pairs `(u, v)`, drawn as arrows `u → v`. "Alice follows Bob" on a social network does *not* imply Bob follows Alice. Web links, Twitter follows, task dependencies, one-way streets, all directed.

In a digraph, "degree" splits into two:
- **in-degree**: how many edges point *into* the vertex.
- **out-degree**: how many edges point *out of* it.

### Weighted vs unweighted

In an **unweighted** graph, all edges are equal, an edge either exists or it doesn't.

In a **weighted** graph, each edge carries a number (a **weight** or **cost**): the distance between cities, the latency of a network link, the price of a flight. Dijkstra's algorithm (Part 3.4) lives entirely in weighted-graph land.

### Self-loops and multigraphs (brief, but worth knowing)

- A **self-loop** is an edge from a vertex to itself: `(u, u)`. Useful for modeling states that can stay put. (Note: a self-loop adds **2** to a vertex's degree in undirected graphs, by convention, because both endpoints are the same vertex.)
- A **multigraph** allows **multiple edges** (parallel edges) between the same pair of vertices, e.g., two different flights between the same two cities. A **simple graph** forbids self-loops *and* parallel edges. We'll build a simple-graph class by default, but we'll point out exactly where the assumption lives.

## Representations: the central tradeoff

We've got the math. Now: how do you store a graph in memory? There are three classic answers. The choice is the single most consequential design decision you'll make, so let's reason through it carefully.

### Edge list

The simplest: just store a list of edges.

```
edges = [(A, B), (A, C), (B, C), (B, D), (C, E), (D, E)]
```

- **Space:** `O(E)`. Very compact.
- **"Does edge (u,v) exist?":** `O(E)`, scan the whole list. Slow.
- **"Who are u's neighbors?":** `O(E)`, scan again. Slow.

Edge lists are great for *some* algorithms (Kruskal's MST sorts edges by weight) and for serialization, but they're terrible for the queries you run constantly. So you mostly use them as input/output format, not the working representation.

### Adjacency matrix

Number the vertices `0 … V−1`. Make a `V × V` grid `M` where `M[i][j] = 1` if there's an edge from `i` to `j`, else `0`. (For weighted graphs, store the weight instead of `1`, and use a sentinel like `∞` or `None` for "no edge".)

For our friend graph (undirected → the matrix is **symmetric** across the diagonal):

```
       A  B  C  D  E
   A [ 0  1  1  0  0 ]
   B [ 1  0  1  1  0 ]
   C [ 1  1  0  0  1 ]
   D [ 0  1  0  0  1 ]
   E [ 0  0  1  1  0 ]
```

- **Space:** `O(V²)`, always, regardless of how few edges you actually have. This is the killer for large sparse graphs.
- **"Does edge (u,v) exist?":** `O(1)`, just look at `M[u][v]`. This is the matrix's big win.
- **"Who are u's neighbors?":** `O(V)`, scan the whole row `u`, even if the vertex has 2 neighbors.
- **Add / remove edge:** `O(1)`.

### Adjacency list

For each vertex, keep a list (or set/dict) of its neighbors.

```
A → [B, C]
B → [A, C, D]
C → [A, B, E]
D → [B, E]
E → [C, D]
```

- **Space:** `O(V + E)`, one slot per vertex, plus one entry per edge endpoint. For sparse graphs (`E ≈ V`), this is enormously smaller than `O(V²)`.
- **"Does edge (u,v) exist?":** `O(deg(u))`, scan u's neighbor list. If you use a `set` or `dict` per vertex instead of a plain list, this drops to `O(1)` average.
- **"Who are u's neighbors?":** `O(deg(u))`, just iterate the list. Optimal: you only touch the neighbors that exist.
- **Add edge:** `O(1)` (append / set insert).
- **Remove edge:** `O(deg(u))` for a list, `O(1)` for a set/dict.

### The tradeoff table (tape this to your monitor)

| Operation | Edge list | Adjacency matrix | Adjacency list (sets) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Space | `O(E)` | `O(V²)` | `O(V + E)` |
| `has_edge(u, v)` | `O(E)` | `O(1)` | `O(1)` avg |
| Iterate neighbors of `u` | `O(E)` | `O(V)` | `O(deg(u))` |
| Add edge | `O(1)` | `O(1)` | `O(1)` |
| Remove edge | `O(E)` | `O(1)` | `O(1)` avg |
| Iterate **all** edges | `O(E)` | `O(V²)` | `O(V + E)` |
| Good when… | tiny / serialization | **dense**, fast `has_edge` | **sparse** (the usual case) |

**The decision rule worth memorizing:**

> If the graph is **dense** (`E` near `V²`) and you query `has_edge` constantly, reach for the **matrix**. Otherwise, and this is the vast majority of real graphs, reach for the **adjacency list**. When in doubt, list.

Why does dense favor the matrix? Because when `E ≈ V²`, the list's `O(V+E)` space *equals* `O(V²)` anyway, so you're paying the same memory, and the matrix gives you `O(1)` edge checks and cache-friendly contiguous rows on top. The list only wins when `E ≪ V²`.

## Build it: a Graph class supporting both representations

Let's build a single `Graph` class with a `representation` flag (`"list"` or `"matrix"`). It handles **directed/undirected** (via a `directed` flag) and **weighted/unweighted** (weights default to `1`). Same public API regardless of the underlying storage: that's the point of an abstraction.

### Design decisions & invariants

Before code, let's pin down the invariants we're committing to. Writing these down first is the habit that saves you from 2am debugging:

1. **Vertices are hashable labels** (strings, ints, anything you can put in a dict). We map them to integer indices internally for the matrix.
2. **Simple graph by default:** no parallel edges (adding the same edge twice just updates the weight). We'll allow self-loops because they're cheap and sometimes useful, but we'll note it.
3. **Undirected symmetry invariant:** in undirected mode, `has_edge(u, v) == has_edge(v, u)` *always*. Every mutation must preserve this. This is the invariant most likely to break, so the tests hammer it.
4. **Weight invariant:** an edge's stored weight is whatever was last set; `None`/absent means "no edge."

```python
class Graph:
    """A graph supporting adjacency-list OR adjacency-matrix storage,
    directed or undirected, weighted or unweighted.

    Invariants:
      - Vertex labels are unique and hashable.
      - Undirected mode keeps edges symmetric at all times.
      - No parallel edges (re-adding an edge updates its weight).
    """

    def __init__(self, directed=False, representation="list"):
        if representation not in ("list", "matrix"):
            raise ValueError("representation must be 'list' or 'matrix'")
        self.directed = directed
        self.representation = representation

        # Shared: label <-> index bookkeeping (needed for the matrix;
        # harmless and handy for the list too).
        self._index = {}        # label -> int index
        self._labels = []       # int index -> label

        if representation == "list":
            # label -> {neighbor_label: weight}
            self._adj = {}
        else:  # matrix
            # _matrix[i][j] = weight, or None if no edge
            self._matrix = []   # list of lists, grown as vertices are added

    # ---------- size ----------

    def __len__(self):
        """Number of vertices, so len(graph) just works."""
        return len(self._labels)

    def num_edges(self):
        """Count edges. Undirected edges are counted once."""
        if self.representation == "list":
            total = sum(len(nbrs) for nbrs in self._adj.values())
        else:
            total = sum(
                1
                for i in range(len(self))
                for j in range(len(self))
                if self._matrix[i][j] is not None
            )
        # In undirected mode every edge was stored twice (u->v and v->u),
        # EXCEPT self-loops which are stored once. Correct for that.
        if not self.directed:
            self_loops = sum(1 for v in self._labels if self.has_edge(v, v))
            total = (total - self_loops) // 2 + self_loops
        return total

    # ---------- vertices ----------

    def add_vertex(self, label):
        """Add a vertex. Idempotent: re-adding an existing vertex is a no-op."""
        if label in self._index:
            return
        idx = len(self._labels)
        self._index[label] = idx
        self._labels.append(label)

        if self.representation == "list":
            self._adj[label] = {}
        else:
            # Grow every existing row by one column, then add a new row.
            for row in self._matrix:
                row.append(None)
            self._matrix.append([None] * (idx + 1))

    def vertices(self):
        """Return the list of vertex labels (a snapshot copy)."""
        return list(self._labels)

    # ---------- edges ----------

    def add_edge(self, u, v, weight=1):
        """Add (or update) an edge u->v with the given weight.
        Auto-creates vertices if they don't exist yet."""
        self.add_vertex(u)
        self.add_vertex(v)

        if self.representation == "list":
            self._adj[u][v] = weight
            if not self.directed and u != v:
                self._adj[v][u] = weight
        else:
            i, j = self._index[u], self._index[v]
            self._matrix[i][j] = weight
            if not self.directed and u != v:
                self._matrix[j][i] = weight

    def remove_edge(self, u, v):
        """Remove edge u->v (and v->u if undirected). No-op if absent."""
        if self.representation == "list":
            self._adj.get(u, {}).pop(v, None)
            if not self.directed and u != v:
                self._adj.get(v, {}).pop(u, None)
        else:
            if u in self._index and v in self._index:
                i, j = self._index[u], self._index[v]
                self._matrix[i][j] = None
                if not self.directed and u != v:
                    self._matrix[j][i] = None

    def has_edge(self, u, v):
        """True iff edge u->v exists. O(1) for both representations here."""
        if self.representation == "list":
            return v in self._adj.get(u, {})
        else:
            if u not in self._index or v not in self._index:
                return False
            i, j = self._index[u], self._index[v]
            return self._matrix[i][j] is not None

    def weight(self, u, v):
        """Weight of edge u->v, or None if no such edge."""
        if not self.has_edge(u, v):
            return None
        if self.representation == "list":
            return self._adj[u][v]
        i, j = self._index[u], self._index[v]
        return self._matrix[i][j]

    def neighbors(self, u):
        """Yield the neighbor labels of u (out-neighbors if directed)."""
        if u not in self._index:
            raise KeyError(f"no such vertex: {u!r}")
        if self.representation == "list":
            yield from self._adj[u].keys()
        else:
            i = self._index[u]
            for j in range(len(self)):
                if self._matrix[i][j] is not None:
                    yield self._labels[j]

    # ---------- degree ----------

    def degree(self, u):
        """Degree of u. For directed graphs this is the OUT-degree."""
        return sum(1 for _ in self.neighbors(u))

    def in_degree(self, u):
        """In-degree (number of edges pointing INTO u)."""
        return sum(1 for v in self._labels if self.has_edge(v, u))
```

Notice how `neighbors`, `has_edge`, and friends present an *identical interface* whether you're backed by a dict-of-dicts or a 2D grid. Callers, including the algorithms in Part 3.4, never need to know. That's the abstraction earning its keep.

> **One subtlety on `has_edge` being O(1) for the list:** we chose a **dict** (`{neighbor: weight}`) per vertex instead of a plain Python `list`. A list would make `has_edge` cost `O(deg(u))`. The dict buys us `O(1)` average membership tests *and* stores the weight in the same place. This is the representation to reach for in practice. If you used a list-of-neighbors, the complexity table's `has_edge` row would say `O(deg)` instead of `O(1)`.

### Traced walkthrough - same graph, two representations

Abstractions hide the mechanics, so let's crank the handle by hand once. We'll build this tiny undirected graph one edge at a time and watch both storages fill in:

```
   A --- B
   |
   C
```

That's three vertices and two edges: `A-B`, then `A-C`. We'll show the dict-of-dicts (`_adj`) on the left and the grid (`_matrix`) on the right after each step.

**Step 0, empty graph.** Nothing exists yet:

```
LIST  _adj = {}                MATRIX  _matrix = []  (0x0)
```

**Step 1, `add_edge("A", "B")`.** First `add_vertex("A")` and `add_vertex("B")` fire (they auto-create). Then we write the edge. Because we're **undirected** and `A != B`, we write it in *both directions*:

```
LIST                           MATRIX        A  B
  _adj = {                                A [ 0  1 ]
    "A": {"B": 1},   <- A->B           B [ 1  0 ]
    "B": {"A": 1},   <- B->A   (the symmetric twin)
  }                            indices: A=0, B=1
```

Notice the two highlighted entries are mirror images. In the matrix, that's the symmetry across the diagonal: `M[0][1] == M[1][0] == 1`. This double-write is the symmetry invariant in action, and it's exactly the line beginners forget (see Common Mistakes).

**Step 2, `add_edge("A", "C")`.** Now `add_vertex("C")` fires. For the **matrix** this is the expensive bit: every existing row grows a new column, then we append a fresh row of `None`s (drawn as `0`). Then we write `A-C` symmetrically:

```
LIST                           MATRIX        A  B  C
  _adj = {                                A [ 0  1  1 ]   <- A->C added
    "A": {"B": 1, "C": 1},             B [ 1  0  0 ]
    "B": {"A": 1},                     C [ 1  0  0 ]   <- C->A (twin)
    "C": {"A": 1},                indices: A=0, B=1, C=2
  }
```

Stare at the two side by side and the tradeoff becomes physical:

- The **list** only stored what *exists*: 4 entries total (each edge twice). Empty space costs nothing. `A`'s neighbors are right there: `{"B", "C"}`.
- The **matrix** is a full 3×3 grid = 9 cells, of which only 4 are non-zero. Every cell exists whether or not there's an edge. On 3 vertices that's fine; on 3 million sparse vertices it's a catastrophe (that's the `O(V²)` blowup).
- The **symmetry** is visible in both: in the list, every `u: {v: w}` has a matching `v: {u: w}`; in the matrix, the grid is a mirror image across the top-left-to-bottom-right diagonal.

If we had passed `directed=True`, we'd write *only* `_adj["A"]["B"]` (no twin) and *only* `M[0][1]` (the mirror cell stays `0`). That single `if not self.directed` branch is the entire difference between the two flavors.

#### Tracing the minimal reachability DFS

Now the same "make it concrete" treatment for traversal. Take the directed chain `A → B → C` and ask `is_reachable(g, "A", "C")`. Here's the loop's state, `stack` (we `pop()` from the right end) and `seen`, at the top of each iteration:

```
iter | node popped | seen before        | stack after this iter        | note
-----+-------------+--------------------+------------------------------+----------------------
init |     -       | {}                 | ["A"]                        | seed with start
  1  |   "A"       | {}                 | ["B"]      (pushed A's nbr)  | A != C; mark A seen
  2  |   "B"       | {"A"}              | ["C"]      (pushed B's nbr)  | B != C; mark B seen
  3  |   "C"       | {"A","B"}          | []                           | C == target -> return True
```

Walk it slowly: we pop a node, check if it's the target, and if not we mark it `seen` and push its unseen neighbors onto the stack. The `seen` set is what stops us from looping forever on a cycle: if `C` looped back to `A`, iteration would just find `A in seen` and `continue` instead of revisiting it. **That `seen` set is not optional**; drop it on a cyclic graph and you get an infinite loop (again, see Common Mistakes). The `stack.pop()` from the end is what makes this depth-*first*; swap it for `pop(0)` and you'd have breadth-first, but that, and the full analysis, is Part 3.4 territory.

## Tests: prove the invariants hold

Code without tests is a hypothesis. Let's turn it into a fact. We run the same suite against both representations and both directedness modes; if the abstraction is real, the behavior must be identical.

```python
def run_tests():
    # --- build the friend graph in all four configs ---
    edges = [("A", "B"), ("A", "C"), ("B", "C"),
             ("B", "D"), ("C", "E"), ("D", "E")]

    for rep in ("list", "matrix"):
        g = Graph(directed=False, representation=rep)
        for u, v in edges:
            g.add_edge(u, v)

        # size
        assert len(g) == 5, f"[{rep}] vertex count"
        assert g.num_edges() == 6, f"[{rep}] edge count, got {g.num_edges()}"

        # has_edge + symmetry invariant (undirected)
        assert g.has_edge("A", "B") and g.has_edge("B", "A"), f"[{rep}] symmetry"
        assert not g.has_edge("A", "D"), f"[{rep}] no A-D edge"

        # neighbors
        assert set(g.neighbors("B")) == {"A", "C", "D"}, f"[{rep}] B neighbors"

        # degrees + Handshaking Lemma: sum of degrees == 2|E|
        total_degree = sum(g.degree(v) for v in g.vertices())
        assert total_degree == 2 * g.num_edges(), \
            f"[{rep}] handshaking: {total_degree} != {2 * g.num_edges()}"
        assert g.degree("B") == 3, f"[{rep}] deg(B)"

        # remove_edge preserves symmetry
        g.remove_edge("A", "B")
        assert not g.has_edge("A", "B") and not g.has_edge("B", "A"), \
            f"[{rep}] remove symmetry"
        assert g.num_edges() == 5, f"[{rep}] edges after removal"

    # --- directed behavior ---
    for rep in ("list", "matrix"):
        d = Graph(directed=True, representation=rep)
        d.add_edge("X", "Y")            # X -> Y only
        assert d.has_edge("X", "Y"), f"[{rep}] directed forward"
        assert not d.has_edge("Y", "X"), f"[{rep}] directed NOT backward"
        assert d.degree("X") == 1 and d.degree("Y") == 0, f"[{rep}] out-degree"
        assert d.in_degree("Y") == 1 and d.in_degree("X") == 0, f"[{rep}] in-degree"

    # --- weighted behavior ---
    for rep in ("list", "matrix"):
        w = Graph(directed=False, representation=rep)
        w.add_edge("P", "Q", weight=7)
        assert w.weight("P", "Q") == 7, f"[{rep}] weight stored"
        assert w.weight("Q", "P") == 7, f"[{rep}] weight symmetric"
        assert w.weight("P", "Z") is None, f"[{rep}] missing weight is None"
        w.add_edge("P", "Q", weight=3)   # update, not duplicate
        assert w.weight("P", "Q") == 3, f"[{rep}] weight updated"
        assert w.num_edges() == 1, f"[{rep}] no parallel edge created"

    # --- self-loop sanity ---
    for rep in ("list", "matrix"):
        s = Graph(directed=False, representation=rep)
        s.add_edge("A", "A")
        assert s.has_edge("A", "A"), f"[{rep}] self-loop exists"
        assert s.num_edges() == 1, f"[{rep}] self-loop counted once"

    print("All graph tests passed ✓")


run_tests()
```

```text
All graph tests passed ✓
```

Every assertion fires against the dict-backed list and the grid-backed matrix, directed and undirected, weighted and unweighted. The symmetry invariant survives every mutation. The Handshaking Lemma checks out (`sum of degrees == 2·|E|`). That's the foundation locked in.

## A tiny traversal teaser (motivating Part 3.4)

We promised not to build the real algorithms here: they deserve their own chapter with proper complexity analysis. But here's a taste of why we built all this machinery: reachability via a dead-simple depth-first search. It's short precisely because the `Graph` abstraction did the hard work.

```python
def is_reachable(graph, start, target):
    """Can we get from `start` to `target` following edges?
    A minimal DFS. Full treatment (BFS, DFS, shortest paths) is Part 3.4."""
    seen = set()
    stack = [start]
    while stack:
        node = stack.pop()
        if node == target:
            return True
        if node in seen:
            continue
        seen.add(node)
        for nbr in graph.neighbors(node):   # <-- the abstraction pays off here
            if nbr not in seen:
                stack.append(nbr)
    return False
```

That `graph.neighbors(node)` call works identically whether the graph is a matrix or a list, directed or undirected. We wrote reachability once and it runs on every representation. That is the dividend of a clean interface, and it's exactly what we'll exploit when we build BFS, DFS, topological sort, and Dijkstra in Part 3.4.

## Mini-project: model a road network and answer reachability

Let's put it all together. We'll model a small directed, weighted road network (some roads are one-way), then answer real questions: Is town B reachable from town A? How many roads leave each town? Who are a town's direct neighbors?

```python
def build_road_network():
    """A directed, weighted road network. Weights = distance in km.
    Note: NORTHGATE -> EASTON is one-way (no return road)."""
    roads = Graph(directed=True, representation="list")
    segments = [
        ("Harbor",    "Midtown",   4),
        ("Midtown",   "Northgate", 6),
        ("Midtown",   "Westend",   3),
        ("Northgate", "Easton",    5),   # one-way!
        ("Westend",   "Easton",    8),
        ("Easton",    "Harbor",    7),   # closes a big loop
        ("Westend",   "Lakeside",  2),
        # Lakeside is a dead-end here (no outgoing roads).
    ]
    for a, b, dist in segments:
        roads.add_edge(a, b, weight=dist)
    return roads


def reachable_set(graph, start):
    """All vertices reachable from `start` (inclusive). DFS again -
    a preview of Part 3.4 traversal."""
    seen, stack = set(), [start]
    while stack:
        node = stack.pop()
        if node in seen:
            continue
        seen.add(node)
        for nbr in graph.neighbors(node):
            stack.append(nbr)
    return seen

    print(f"Towns: {len(roads)}")
    print(f"Road segments (directed): {roads.num_edges()}\n")

    # Degree / neighbor queries
    print("Outgoing roads per town:")
    for town in sorted(roads.vertices()):
        outs = sorted(roads.neighbors(town))
        print(f"  {town:9} out-degree {roads.degree(town)} -> {outs}")
    print()

    # Reachability questions
    questions = [
        ("Harbor", "Easton"),
        ("Easton", "Lakeside"),
        ("Lakeside", "Harbor"),   # Lakeside is a dead-end -> should be False
    ]
    for src, dst in questions:
        ans = is_reachable(roads, src, dst)
        print(f"  Reachable {src} -> {dst}? {ans}")
    print()

    # Full reachable set from Harbor
    rs = reachable_set(roads, "Harbor")
    print(f"Reachable from Harbor: {sorted(rs)}")

    return roads
```

## Complexity recap for our implementation

Let's tabulate exactly what our class costs, so the Big-O isn't hand-wavy. `deg(u)` is u's degree; `V = len(graph)`.

| Method | List backing (dict-of-dicts) | Matrix backing |
|---|---|---|
| `add_vertex` | `O(1)` | `O(V)` (grow every row) |
| `add_edge` | `O(1)` | `O(1)` |
| `remove_edge` | `O(1)` avg | `O(1)` |
| `has_edge` | `O(1)` avg | `O(1)` |
| `weight` | `O(1)` avg | `O(1)` |
| `neighbors` | `O(deg(u))` | `O(V)` |
| `degree` | `O(deg(u))` | `O(V)` |
| `in_degree` | `O(V + E)` | `O(V)` |
| Space | `O(V + E)` | `O(V²)` |

Two things jump out:

1. `add_vertex` is `O(V)` for the matrix because we resize every existing row. If you knew the vertex count up front, you'd preallocate the `V×V` grid once and make it `O(1)`, a classic time/space tradeoff.
2. `in_degree` is cheap on the matrix (`O(V)`, just scan a column) but pricier on the list (`O(V+E)`, since we don't store reverse edges). If you query in-degrees constantly on a list-backed digraph, you'd maintain a second reverse adjacency structure. Design follows access pattern.

## Common mistakes

These are the bugs you'll likely write, probably more than once. Graphs have a small number of very repeatable traps; internalize these and you'll skip a lot of debugging.

1. **Forgetting the symmetry update in undirected mode.** You write `_adj[u][v] = w` and call it a day. Now `has_edge(u, v)` is `True` but `has_edge(v, u)` is `False`: the graph is silently half-directed. Every undirected mutation needs the twin write (`_adj[v][u] = w`), guarded by `if not self.directed`. This is the most common graph bug there is.

2. **Double-counting undirected edges.** Because each undirected edge is stored *twice* (`u→v` and `v→u`), naively summing entries gives you `2|E|`, not `|E|`. Our `num_edges` divides by 2, but watch out: self-loops are stored *once*, so blindly halving undercounts them. (That's why our code handles self-loops separately.)

3. **Off-by-one when growing the matrix.** When you `add_vertex`, the *order matters*: append a column to every **existing** row first, then append the new row sized to the **new** count (`idx + 1`). Get the sizing wrong and you'll index a row that's one column too short and get an `IndexError`, or worse, a row that's too long and reads garbage. Rows and the index map must stay in lockstep.

4. **Using a `list` instead of a `set`/`dict` for neighbors.** A list-of-neighbors looks fine until `has_edge` shows up: membership in a list is `O(deg(u))`, so a hot `has_edge` loop quietly becomes quadratic. A `set` (or our `dict` keyed by neighbor) makes it `O(1)` average. Same idea, wildly different performance.

5. **`O(V²)` matrix memory blowup on sparse graphs.** The matrix always allocates `V×V` cells regardless of edge count. Build an adjacency matrix for a 1-million-vertex social graph and you've asked for a trillion cells: gigabytes for a graph the list would store in megabytes. Default to the list; only reach for the matrix when the graph is actually dense or `V` is small.

6. **Forgetting that self-loops count differently.** In an undirected graph, a self-loop `(u, u)` adds 2 to `deg(u)` by convention (both endpoints are `u`), and it's stored once, not twice. Edge-counting and degree code that assumes "every edge has two distinct endpoints" will be subtly wrong the moment a self-loop appears.

7. **Not marking visited (infinite loop in traversal).** Any traversal on a graph with cycles must track a `seen`/`visited` set and skip already-visited nodes. Skip the bookkeeping and a cycle `A → B → A` loops forever. Trees can sometimes get away without it (no cycles by definition); general graphs never can.

8. **Confusing in-degree vs out-degree in digraphs.** In a directed graph, `degree(u)` in our class means out-degree (edges leaving `u`); `in_degree(u)` counts edges entering. A "sink" has out-degree 0; a "source" has in-degree 0. Mix them up and your reachability/dependency logic inverts, a classic source of "why is everything backwards?" bugs.

## On the job / interview angle

Graphs are, by a wide margin, one of the most common interview topics, and frequently the hardest, because graph problems are rarely labeled "graph problem." The interviewer describes a maze, a network of friends, a set of build dependencies, a word-ladder puzzle, and the first skill they're testing is whether you can recognize "oh, this is a graph" and model it correctly. Once you've named it as a graph, the algorithm is often a standard one. The modeling is where candidates win or lose.

This chapter is the foundation that modeling stands on. The flashy algorithms come next, but you can't apply BFS to a maze if you can't see the maze as a graph in the first place.

**How to recognize a graph problem in disguise:**

- **Grids as graphs.** A 2D grid (maze, image, board) is a graph where each cell is a vertex adjacent to its up/down/left/right neighbors (sometimes diagonals too). "Count the islands," "flood fill," "shortest path through a maze": all grid-as-graph. No one will tell you it's a graph.
- **Dependencies / ordering.** "Course A requires B," "task X must finish before Y," "package P depends on Q": directed edges, where the question is usually about reachability or valid ordering.
- **Relationships / connections.** Friends, follows, routes, links, "are these two related?" If the data is about connections between entities, it's a graph.

**The modeling questions to ask out loud (interviewers love this):**

1. **What are the vertices? What are the edges?** Name them explicitly. For a maze: vertices = open cells, edges = adjacency between open cells. Getting this wrong dooms everything downstream.
2. **Directed or undirected?** Friendship is undirected; "depends on" is directed. This decides whether you write the symmetric twin.
3. **Weighted or unweighted?** Distances and costs mean weighted (→ Dijkstra-flavored, Part 3.4); plain "connected or not" means unweighted.
4. **List or matrix?** Sparse and big → adjacency list. Dense or tiny, with constant `has_edge` checks → matrix. Say why in terms of `O(V+E)` vs `O(V²)`.

The tools from this chapter already answer a surprising number of interview questions: counting connected components (how many separate groups?), checking whether a path exists between two nodes (reachability via the minimal DFS), computing degrees, and using the Handshaking Lemma as a sanity check (the sum of degrees must equal `2|E|` and must be even, a fast way to catch a malformed graph or a bug). The classic follow-ups (BFS for shortest unweighted paths, DFS for cycle detection, topological sort for dependency ordering, and Dijkstra for weighted shortest paths) all build directly on the `neighbors()` interface and are covered in Part 3.4. Knowing they're coming, and being able to say "this is a topological-sort problem," is itself worth points.

**Practice prompts (with reasoning):**

1. *"You're given a list of friendships. How many separate friend-groups are there?"* This is counting connected components in an undirected graph: repeatedly pick an unvisited vertex, flood-fill everything reachable from it, and increment a counter. Each flood-fill consumes exactly one component.

2. *"Given a 5×5 grid where some cells are walls, can you get from the top-left to the bottom-right?"* Model each open cell as a vertex with up/down/left/right edges; then it's plain reachability (the DFS we wrote). The insight is the grid-as-graph mapping; the algorithm is already in your pocket.

3. *"Here's a course catalog: each course lists its prerequisites. Which courses must I take before Course X?"* Directed graph, edges = "is prerequisite of." The answer is the set of vertices that can reach X (or that X depends on), pure reachability. (Ordering them into a valid schedule is topological sort, Part 3.4, but knowing which courses are required is foundation-level.)

4. *"Should you use an adjacency matrix or list for a 2-billion-user social network?"* List, decisively. About 200 friends each means `E ≈ 200 billion` but `V² ≈ 4×10^18`: the matrix wants quintillions of cells for a sparse graph. State it in `O(V+E)` vs `O(V²)` terms; that's the answer they want.

5. *"How would you sanity-check that a graph you just parsed isn't corrupt?"* Handshaking Lemma: sum every vertex's degree; it must equal `2|E|` and must be even. An odd degree sum means you've miscounted or built a half-directed graph. It's cheap (`O(V+E)`) and catches real bugs.

6. *"What's the difference between in-degree and out-degree, and when do you care?"* Only meaningful in digraphs. A vertex with out-degree 0 is a **sink** (dead-end); in-degree 0 is a **source** (nothing points to it). You care for dependency graphs (sources = things with no prerequisites, a starting point for ordering) and for spotting dead-ends in routing.

```python
# --- 1. Build from an edge list ---
def from_edge_list(edges, directed=False, representation="list"):
    g = Graph(directed=directed, representation=representation)
    for u, v in edges:
        g.add_edge(u, v)
    return g
# Complexity: O(E) edges, each add_edge is O(1) on the list backing -> O(V + E).


# --- 2. Degree sequence + Handshaking check ---
def degree_sequence(g):
    return sorted(g.degree(v) for v in g.vertices())
    # O(V + E): one neighbor-scan per vertex sums to touching every edge endpoint.

def verify_handshaking(g):
    # Only meaningful for undirected graphs (directed: sum of out-degrees == |E|).
    total = sum(g.degree(v) for v in g.vertices())
    return total == 2 * g.num_edges()
    # O(V + E).


# --- 3. Neighbors-of-neighbors (exactly two steps away) ---
def neighbors_of_neighbors(g, u):
    direct = set(g.neighbors(u))
    two_step = set()
    for n in direct:
        for nn in g.neighbors(n):
            two_step.add(nn)
    return two_step - direct - {u}
    # O(sum of deg over u's neighbors) <= O(V + E) worst case.


# --- 4. Isolated vertices (no edges at all) ---
def isolated_vertices(g):
    result = []
    for v in g.vertices():
        out = g.degree(v)
        inc = g.in_degree(v) if g.directed else 0
        if out == 0 and inc == 0:
            result.append(v)
    return result
    # O(V * (deg + V)) on the list for digraphs because in_degree scans;
    # O(V + E) for undirected. Fine for foundation work.


# --- 5. Self-loop detector ---
def has_self_loop(g):
    return any(g.has_edge(v, v) for v in g.vertices())
    # O(V) average: has_edge is O(1) on both backings.


# --- 6. Densest vertex (highest degree, ties by label) ---
def densest_vertex(g):
    if len(g) == 0:
        return None
    # max by (degree, then smaller label): rank ties break toward
    # the lexicographically smaller label.
    return max(g.vertices(), key=lambda v: (g.degree(v), -_rank(v, g)))

def _rank(v, g):
    # stable tie-break by label order; small helper so ties go to the
    # lexicographically smaller label.
    return sorted(g.vertices()).index(v)
    # Simpler, clearer alternative below if labels are comparable:

def densest_vertex_simple(g):
    if len(g) == 0:
        return None
    best = None
    best_deg = -1
    for v in sorted(g.vertices()):      # sorted -> first max wins ties
        d = g.degree(v)
        if d > best_deg:
            best_deg, best = d, v
    return best
    # O(V * deg) = O(V + E) on the list. Use the *_simple version in practice.


# --- 7. Path exists (named wrapper over reachability) ---
def path_exists(g, a, b):
    if a not in g.vertices() or b not in g.vertices():
        return False
    return is_reachable(g, a, b)
    # O(V + E): a single DFS in the worst case.


# --- 8. Count connected components (undirected, flood-fill DFS) ---
def count_components(g):
    assert not g.directed, "components defined here for undirected graphs"
    seen = set()
    count = 0
    for start in g.vertices():
        if start in seen:
            continue
        count += 1
        # flood-fill everything reachable from `start`
        stack = [start]
        while stack:
            node = stack.pop()
            if node in seen:
                continue
            seen.add(node)
            for nbr in g.neighbors(node):
                if nbr not in seen:
                    stack.append(nbr)
    return count
    # O(V + E): every vertex and edge is touched once across all flood-fills.


# --- 9. Convert representations ---
def to_matrix(g):
    """Return (matrix, labels): matrix[i][j] = weight or None, in `labels` order."""
    labels = g.vertices()                    # fix a row/column order
    idx = {lab: i for i, lab in enumerate(labels)}
    n = len(labels)
    M = [[None] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for u in labels:
        for v in g.neighbors(u):
            M[idx[u]][idx[v]] = g.weight(u, v)
    return M, labels
    # O(V + E) to read every edge; O(V^2) space for the grid itself.

def from_matrix(matrix, labels, directed=False):
    g = Graph(directed=directed, representation="list")
    for lab in labels:
        g.add_vertex(lab)
    n = len(labels)
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            w = matrix[i][j]
            if w is not None:
                g.add_edge(labels[i], labels[j], weight=w)
    return g
    # O(V^2) because we must scan every matrix cell.


# --- 10. Grid as a graph + reachable-cell count ---
def grid_to_graph(grid):
    """grid[r][c] == 0 means open, 1 means wall. Undirected, 4-neighbor."""
    rows, cols = len(grid), len(grid[0]) if grid else 0
    g = Graph(directed=False, representation="list")
    for r in range(rows):
        for c in range(cols):
            if grid[r][c] != 0:
                continue
            g.add_vertex((r, c))             # ensure even isolated open cells exist
            for dr, dc in ((1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)):
                nr, nc = r + dr, c + dc
                if 0 <= nr < rows and 0 <= nc < cols and grid[nr][nc] == 0:
                    g.add_edge((r, c), (nr, nc))
    return g
    # O(R*C): constant work per cell (4 neighbors). Edges added twice are
    # deduped by the dict, so no parallel edges.

def count_reachable(grid, start):
    g = grid_to_graph(grid)
    if start not in g.vertices():
        return 0
    seen, stack = set(), [start]
    while stack:
        node = stack.pop()
        if node in seen:
            continue
        seen.add(node)
        for nbr in g.neighbors(node):
            stack.append(nbr)
    return len(seen)
    # O(R*C): flood-fill over reachable open cells.
```

## Extra practice - a tiny social-network analyzer

Different domain, same machinery: that's the payoff of the abstraction. We'll model friendships as an undirected graph and answer the questions a real social product asks: Who are two people's mutual friends? Who should we recommend you befriend? Who's the most connected person? Friend recommendations here are just neighbors-of-neighbors (people your friends know that you don't), no fancy algorithms needed.

```python
def build_social_network():
    """Undirected friendships. Edges are symmetric (friendship goes both ways)."""
    net = Graph(directed=False, representation="list")
    friendships = [
        ("Alice", "Bob"),
        ("Alice", "Carol"),
        ("Bob", "Carol"),
        ("Bob", "Dave"),
        ("Carol", "Eve"),
        ("Dave", "Eve"),
        ("Eve", "Frank"),
        # Grace knows nobody yet -> she'll be isolated.
    ]
    for a, b in friendships:
        net.add_edge(a, b)
    net.add_vertex("Grace")     # a lonely new signup
    return net


def mutual_friends(net, a, b):
    """People who are friends with BOTH a and b."""
    return set(net.neighbors(a)) & set(net.neighbors(b))


def friend_recommendations(net, person):
    """Recommend friends-of-friends the person isn't already friends with
    (and isn't themselves). Pure neighbors-of-neighbors."""
    friends = set(net.neighbors(person))
    recs = set()
    for f in friends:
        for fof in net.neighbors(f):
            if fof != person and fof not in friends:
                recs.add(fof)
    return recs


def most_connected(net):
    """The person with the most friends (highest degree), ties by name."""
    people = sorted(net.vertices())
    if not people:
        return None
    best, best_deg = people[0], -1
    for p in people:
        d = net.degree(p)
        if d > best_deg:
            best_deg, best = d, p
    return best, best_deg


def social_report():
    net = build_social_network()

    print(f"People: {len(net)}   Friendships: {net.num_edges()}\n")

    print("Friend counts:")
    for p in sorted(net.vertices()):
        print(f"  {p:6} has {net.degree(p)} friend(s): {sorted(net.neighbors(p))}")
    print()

    print(f"Mutual friends of Alice & Dave: {sorted(mutual_friends(net, 'Alice', 'Dave'))}")
    print(f"Mutual friends of Alice & Carol: {sorted(mutual_friends(net, 'Alice', 'Carol'))}")
    print()

    print(f"Recommendations for Alice: {sorted(friend_recommendations(net, 'Alice'))}")
    print(f"Recommendations for Frank: {sorted(friend_recommendations(net, 'Frank'))}")
    print()

    person, deg = most_connected(net)
    print(f"Most connected: {person} ({deg} friends)")

    return net
```

Read the results like a product analyst:

- Alice & Dave share exactly one mutual friend, Bob: the set intersection of their neighbor lists. This is the engine behind "you have N mutual friends" on every social app.
- Alice's recommendations are Dave and Eve: friends-of-friends she doesn't already know. Bob is friends with Dave; Carol is friends with Eve. This is the classic "People You May Know," built from nothing but neighbors-of-neighbors.
- Grace got no recommendations and has 0 friends: an isolated vertex. A real product would special-case the cold-start problem; the graph makes her isolation obvious.
- Bob is the most connected with 3 friends (tied with Carol and Eve on degree; ties break to the alphabetically first name). Degree is the crudest first approximation of influence.

```python
def test_social_network():
    net = build_social_network()

    assert len(net) == 7
    assert net.num_edges() == 7
    assert not net.directed

    # symmetry: friendship goes both ways
    assert net.has_edge("Alice", "Bob") and net.has_edge("Bob", "Alice")

    # degrees
    assert net.degree("Bob") == 3
    assert net.degree("Grace") == 0
    assert isolated_vertices(net) == ["Grace"]     # reusing the exercise helper

    # mutual friends
    assert mutual_friends(net, "Alice", "Dave") == {"Bob"}
    assert mutual_friends(net, "Bob", "Carol") == {"Alice"}
    assert mutual_friends(net, "Alice", "Grace") == set()   # Grace knows no one

    # friend recommendations (neighbors-of-neighbors, excluding self & friends)
    assert friend_recommendations(net, "Alice") == {"Dave", "Eve"}
    assert friend_recommendations(net, "Frank") == {"Carol", "Dave"}
    assert friend_recommendations(net, "Grace") == set()    # no friends -> no FoF

    # most connected (Bob, Carol, Eve all deg 3; alphabetical -> Bob)

    assert verify_handshaking(net)
```
