## Start here

Before any math, let's build the picture with something you already understand: a coffee shop.

Picture your favorite café. One barista, one espresso machine. You walk up, order, and a few minutes later you have your latte.

- **Latency** is how long your single order takes, from "I'll have a latte" to "here's your latte." Say 3 minutes. That's your personal experience. You don't care how many other people are in line; you care that your drink takes 3 minutes.
- **Throughput** is how many drinks the shop makes per hour: 20, say, at 3 minutes each. That's the shop owner's number. The owner doesn't care that your specific drink took 3 minutes; they care that the shop pumps out enough drinks to pay rent.

The part that trips everyone up: these two numbers are different, and you can't fix one by tuning the other.

- If you want *your* latte faster (lower latency), the barista has to work faster, or use a faster machine. Speed limit goes up.
- If the owner wants *more drinks per hour* (higher throughput), they hire a second barista and buy a second machine. More lanes.

A second barista does not make your individual latte come out faster; it makes more lattes happen at once. Two baristas working in parallel, each taking 3 minutes per drink, produce ~40 drinks/hour together, but your drink still takes 3 minutes. Throughput scales by adding parallel workers; latency improves only by making each unit of work faster.

```
   THE COFFEE SHOP

   Latency  = time for ONE customer's drink   (3 min)   ← the customer cares
   Throughput = drinks made per hour           (20/hr)  ← the owner cares

   Add a barista  → throughput up,  latency unchanged
   Faster machine → latency down,   throughput up a bit
   Line out the door at 8am → that's PEAK, not average
```

Why should a beginner care? Because every computer system is a coffee shop. A website is a barista taking requests. A database is the espresso machine.

When NYX Finance has 10 users, one server (one barista) is fine. When it has 10 million, you need to know: do you make the barista faster (a bigger machine), or hire more baristas (more machines)? How many baristas? What happens when the morning rush hits and the line goes out the door? If the espresso machine breaks, does the whole shop close, or do you have a backup machine?

That last question, "what happens when something breaks?", is **availability**, and it's why a system with *one* perfect barista is more fragile than a system with *two* mediocre ones.

**Scalability** is just the art of answering "what happens when 100× more people show up?" *before* they show up, with numbers instead of hope.

By the end of this chapter you'll be able to:
- Tell latency and throughput apart in any conversation and never confuse them again.
- Take a product idea ("a chat app with 50M users") and, in five minutes of arithmetic on a napkin, estimate how many servers, how much storage, and how much bandwidth it needs.
- Reason about uptime ("how many nines?") with real failure/repair numbers instead of vibes.
- Spot the classic scaling mistakes that sink real systems, and avoid them.

Everything below is this coffee shop, made precise.

## Theory

### Latency vs Throughput - they are not the same thing

These two get conflated constantly, and it leads to bad designs. Let's nail them down.

- **Latency** = how long *one* operation takes, end to end. Units: time (ms, µs, s). "This request took 80 ms."
- **Throughput** = how many operations complete *per unit time*. Units: ops/time (requests/sec, MB/s). "This service handles 12,000 req/s."

The classic analogy: a highway.
- Latency = how long it takes one car to drive from A to B.
- Throughput = how many cars pass a point per hour.

You can have low latency and low throughput (one fast car on an empty road), high latency and high throughput (a packed but moving freeway, where every car is slow but tons of cars get through), and every combination. Widening the road (more lanes / more servers) raises throughput without changing how long one car's trip takes. Raising the speed limit (faster CPU) lowers latency.

```
Latency  = time per request                (lower is better)
Throughput = requests per second completed   (higher is better)
```

One gotcha: throughput is not 1 / latency in a concurrent system. If a request takes 100 ms but you can run 50 in parallel, throughput is 50 / 0.1 s = 500 req/s, not 10 req/s. The relationship between them is governed by concurrency, which is exactly what Little's Law captures.

### Little's Law: L = λW (and where it comes from)

Little's Law is the single most useful equation in capacity planning. For *any* stable system (arrivals = departures over the long run), with no assumptions about distributions:

```
L = λ · W

L = average number of items in the system (concurrency / in-flight requests)
λ = average arrival rate (items per unit time)  - this is throughput when stable
W = average time an item spends in the system (latency)
```

**Derivation (intuitive area argument).** Watch the system over a long window of length T. Let each request `i` enter and leave, spending time `W_i` inside. Draw a graph of "number in system" over time; the area under that curve is the total "request-seconds" accumulated, which equals Σ W_i (each request contributes its own residence time as area). 

- The *time-average* number in system is that area divided by T: `L = (Σ W_i) / T`.
- Total arrivals in T is `N`, so arrival rate `λ = N / T`.
- Average time in system is `W = (Σ W_i) / N`.

Now substitute:
```
L = (Σ W_i)/T = (N/T) · (Σ W_i / N) = λ · W   ∎
```

No probability distributions, no Poisson assumption: it holds for any stable queueing system. That's why it's so useful.

**Why you care.** It lets you convert between the three quantities you can measure:

> *Example (NYX Finance API).* Suppose you measure average latency W = 200 ms = 0.2 s, and you observe L = 80 requests in flight on average (concurrent connections). Then throughput λ = L / W = 80 / 0.2 = **400 req/s**. If you want to push to 1000 req/s while keeping latency at 200 ms, you need L = λW = 1000 × 0.2 = **200 concurrent in-flight requests**, so your thread pool / connection pool / worker count must support ~200 in flight. If it caps at 80, you *cannot* hit 1000 req/s no matter how you tune; you'll just queue and latency will balloon.

This is the math behind "add more workers." More on the queueing blowup below.

> *Worked example, sizing a thread pool with Little's Law (fully stepped).* Say you're building NYX's "portfolio sync" worker that calls a slow downstream broker API. You need to decide how many threads (or async tasks) the worker pool should hold. Here's the chain of reasoning, one step at a time:
>
> 1. **What do you know?** The broker API call takes on average W = 250 ms = 0.25 s per request (mostly waiting on the network: the thread is blocked, not burning CPU). The product team says you must sustain λ = 600 sync requests/sec at peak.
> 2. **What do you want?** The number of requests that will be in flight simultaneously. That's exactly L, and it's how many threads/tasks the pool needs.
> 3. **Apply L = λ·W:**
>    ```
>    L = λ · W = 600 req/s × 0.25 s = 150 in-flight requests
>    ```
> 4. **Interpret:** you need a pool that can keep ~150 requests in flight at once. If you size the pool at the naive "one thread per CPU core" (say 16 cores → 16 threads), you can sustain only `λ = L/W = 16 / 0.25 = 64 req/s`. That's roughly 10× short of your 600 target: the threads spend 240 of every 250 ms just waiting on the network, idle but occupied.
> 5. **The fix and the catch.** Because this work is I/O-bound (blocked on the network, not CPU), you can run far more in-flight tasks than you have cores; 150 blocked sockets cost almost no CPU. So you bump the pool to ~180 (150 plus ~20% headroom for jitter and the queueing blowup discussed next). If this were CPU-bound work, the opposite holds: more threads than cores just thrashes the scheduler, and the answer is more machines, not more threads.
> 6. **Sanity check with the law backwards:** pool of 180, W = 0.25 s → max sustainable `λ = 180 / 0.25 = 720 req/s`. Comfortably above 600. 
>
> The lesson: Little's Law turns a latency measurement (250 ms) plus a throughput target (600 req/s) directly into a concurrency-sizing decision (150 slots). This calculation is also why "we added more threads and it didn't help" is so common: people add threads without checking whether the bottleneck is concurrency (fixable with threads) or CPU/downstream (not).

### The danger zone: utilization and queueing

When a server approaches 100% utilization, latency does not rise linearly; it explodes. For an M/M/1 queue (single server, Poisson arrivals, exponential service), the average time in system is:

```
W = (1/µ) / (1 - ρ)      where ρ = λ/µ = utilization (0..1), µ = service rate
```

Plug in numbers with service time 1/µ = 10 ms:
```
ρ = 0.5  → W = 10/0.5  = 20 ms
ρ = 0.8  → W = 10/0.2  = 50 ms
ρ = 0.9  → W = 10/0.1  = 100 ms
ρ = 0.95 → W = 10/0.05 = 200 ms
ρ = 0.99 → W = 10/0.01 = 1000 ms   ← 100x the service time!
```

**Lesson:** never run a latency-sensitive service near 100% utilization. Plan for ~50-70% steady-state so spikes have headroom. This is why "we have spare CPU" can still mean "we're falling over": the queue, not the CPU, is the bottleneck.

### QPS / RPS and how to estimate it

QPS (queries per second) = RPS (requests per second), same thing, different word depending on whether you're DB- or web-flavored. To estimate it from product metrics:

```
Daily Active Users (DAU)  ────┐
Requests per user per day     │──→  total requests/day
                              ┘
QPS_avg  = total requests/day / 86,400 s
QPS_peak = QPS_avg · peak_factor   (peak_factor typically 2-5×)
```

There are 86,400 seconds in a day; memorize that. A handy shortcut: **1 million events/day ≈ 11.6 QPS** (1e6 / 86400). So 100M/day ≈ 1160 QPS average.

> *Worked example.* NYX has 500k DAU, each loads a dashboard that fires ~20 API calls, and they do this ~5 sessions/day.
> - Requests/day = 500,000 × 20 × 5 = 50,000,000 = 50M/day
> - QPS_avg = 50e6 / 86400 ≈ **579 QPS**
> - Markets open at a fixed time → spiky. Use peak_factor 4× → QPS_peak ≈ **2,300 QPS**.

Always design for peak, not average. Averages get you paged at 9:30am when the market opens.

### Percentiles, and why the average lies

If you report only the *average* latency, you are hiding the experience of your worst-served users. Consider these 10 request latencies (ms):

```
10, 12, 11, 13, 10, 12, 11, 14, 12, 900
```
- Mean = (105 + 900)/10 = **100.5 ms**, sounds bad but vague.
- Median (p50) = 12 ms, most requests are snappy.
- p99 ≈ 900 ms: 1% of users wait nearly a second.

The average (100 ms) describes *nobody*: no single request took ~100 ms. The truth is "almost everyone is fast, but the tail is terrible." That's why you always look at **percentiles**:

- **p50 (median)**, the typical experience.
- **p95**, 95% of requests are at least this fast; the start of "unlucky."
- **p99 / p99.9**, the tail. This is what power users and high-traffic pages hit constantly.

**Why the tail dominates at scale (tail-at-scale).** If a single backend call has p99 = 1% chance of being slow, and rendering one page fans out to **100** backend calls, the probability that *at least one* of them is slow is:
```
P(page slow) = 1 - (1 - 0.01)^100 = 1 - 0.99^100 ≈ 1 - 0.366 = 0.634
```
63% of page loads hit a slow call. Your p99 backend becomes your median page, which is why Google obsesses over p99 of leaf services. At scale, tail latency is not an edge case; it's the common case.

### Numbers Every Engineer Should Know (latency table)

Burn these orders of magnitude into memory. They tell you instantly whether a design is sane.

```
Operation                              Latency        Notes / scale-up
-------------------------------------- -------------- ------------------------
L1 cache reference                     ~0.5 ns
Branch mispredict                      ~5   ns
L2 cache reference                     ~7   ns        14× L1
Mutex lock/unlock                      ~25  ns
Main memory (RAM) reference            ~100 ns        200× L1
Compress 1 KB (Snappy-ish)             ~2   µs
Send 1 KB over 1 Gbps network          ~10  µs
Read 1 MB sequentially from RAM        ~50  µs (DDR4 faster)
SSD random read                        ~150 µs        ~1,500× RAM read
Read 1 MB sequentially from SSD        ~1   ms
Round trip within same datacenter      ~0.5 ms
Read 1 MB sequentially from disk (HDD) ~20  ms
Disk seek (HDD)                        ~10  ms
Round trip CA ↔ Netherlands            ~150 ms        speed of light tax
```

Two mental anchors worth keeping:
- RAM is ~1.5 million× faster than a network round trip across the world. Caching in memory near the user is the single biggest lever (Chapter 9.2).
- A cross-country round trip is ~150 ms. No amount of CPU fixes geography, which is why CDNs and edge exist.

If you scale all these up by ~1 billion (ns → s) to make them human:
- L1 reference = 0.5 s, RAM = 100 s (~1.5 min), SSD random read = ~1.7 days, datacenter round trip = ~6 days, disk seek = ~4 months, CA↔NL round trip = ~5 years. That intuition (memory = minutes, disk = months, intercontinental = years) is worth keeping.

### Vertical vs Horizontal Scaling

```
VERTICAL (scale up)              HORIZONTAL (scale out)
bigger machine                   more machines
   ┌────────────┐                ┌────┐ ┌────┐ ┌────┐ ┌────┐
   │  64 cores  │                │ 8c │ │ 8c │ │ 8c │ │ 8c │
   │  512 GB    │                └────┘ └────┘ └────┘ └────┘
   └────────────┘                       behind a load balancer
```

| | Vertical (up) | Horizontal (out) |
|---|---|---|
| How | bigger CPU/RAM/disk | add more nodes |
| Ceiling | hard hardware limit | ~unlimited (in theory) |
| Complexity | simple, no code change | needs LB, statelessness, coordination |
| Failure blast radius | one big box = one big outage | one node out of N |
| Cost curve | superlinear (big boxes pricey) | ~linear (commodity boxes) |
| Best for | databases (hard to shard), early stage | stateless web/app tiers |

**Rule of thumb:** scale *up* first because it's free engineering-wise, until you hit a wall (cost, single-point-of-failure risk, or the biggest box isn't enough). Then scale *out*. Horizontal scaling is the only path to high availability, because a single big box is a single point of failure.

### Stateless vs Stateful - the key to horizontal scaling

A service is **stateless** if any instance can handle any request because it keeps no per-client data in local memory between requests. State lives in a shared store (DB, Redis, etc.).

```
STATELESS (good for scaling)          STATEFUL (hard to scale)
request → any app node → shared DB    request must hit the SAME node
          ↑ ↑ ↑                        (session stored in node memory)
   LB can send anywhere                 LB needs sticky sessions
   add/remove nodes freely              node death = lost sessions
```

Why you care: **horizontal scaling basically requires statelessness.** If a node holds user session state in local RAM, the load balancer must pin that user to that node ("sticky sessions"), and if the node dies, the user is logged out. Push state to Redis or the DB and any node can serve any request: nodes are cattle, not pets, and autoscaling just works. You'll keep NYX's app tier stateless and put session/JWT and cache state in Redis (see 9.3 sticky sessions discussion).

### Availability math - the "nines"

Availability = fraction of time the system is up:
```
A = uptime / (uptime + downtime) = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)
```
where MTBF = mean time between failures, MTTR = mean time to repair. So you raise availability by failing less often and by recovering faster. MTTR matters as much as MTBF, which is why fast rollback and good alerting are availability features.

> *Worked example, computing availability from real failure/repair numbers (fully stepped).* The NYX data-ingest service has a year of incident history. Let's turn it into an availability number, then into a target.
>
> 1. **Gather the raw history.** Over the last 365 days, the service had 6 outages. Total time it was down and being fixed across those 6 incidents was 9 hours.
> 2. **Compute MTTR (mean time to repair):**
>    ```
>    MTTR = total downtime / number of failures = 9 hours / 6 = 1.5 hours per incident
>    ```
> 3. **Compute MTBF (mean time between failures).** Total operating time in a year ≈ 365 × 24 = 8,760 hours. Uptime = 8,760 − 9 = 8,751 hours. Spread across 6 failures:
>    ```
>    MTBF = uptime / number of failures = 8,751 / 6 ≈ 1,458.5 hours between failures (~61 days)
>    ```
> 4. **Compute availability:**
>    ```
>    A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR) = 1458.5 / (1458.5 + 1.5) = 1458.5 / 1460 = 0.99897 ≈ 99.90%
>    ```
>    So you're running at almost exactly three nines (99.9%): 9 hours down/year, which matches 0.1% × 8,760 h ≈ 8.76 h. 
> 5. **Now decide where to invest.** Suppose the business wants 99.99% (four nines, ~52 min/year). Two independent levers:
>    - **Fail less often (raise MTBF).** Cut failures from 6/year to 3/year (better testing, canary deploys) → MTBF ≈ 2,917 h, A = 2917/(2917+1.5) = 99.949%. Better, but not four nines.
>    - **Recover faster (lower MTTR).** Keep 6 failures but cut MTTR from 1.5 h to 6 minutes (0.1 h) via automated rollback + auto-failover → A = 1458.5/(1458.5+0.1) = 99.9931%. Almost four nines from MTTR alone.
> 6. **The takeaway:** for this service, MTTR is the bigger lever: slashing repair time from 90 min to 6 min buys more availability than halving the failure rate. That's not always true, but it's why mature shops obsess over fast detection (alerting), fast diagnosis (observability), and fast recovery (one-click rollback, automated failover). Outages are inevitable; long outages are a choice.

What the nines actually buy you (per year, where 1 year ≈ 525,600 min):
```
Availability   "nines"   Downtime / year      Downtime / month   Downtime / day
99%            two        3.65 days            7.2 hours          14.4 min
99.9%          three      8.76 hours           43.8 min           1.44 min
99.95%         -          4.38 hours           21.9 min           43.2 s
99.99%         four       52.6 min             4.38 min           8.6 s
99.999%        five       5.26 min             26.3 s             0.86 s
```

Each extra nine is ~10× less downtime and dramatically more expensive to reach. For NYX you'd target 99.9% for the dashboard (8.8 hours/year is acceptable for analytics) but push the trade-execution / data-ingest path to 99.99%, because lost market data during open is unacceptable.

**Components in series (a request must pass through all of them):** availabilities *multiply*.
```
A_total = A1 · A2 · A3 · ...
```
If a request passes through LB (99.99%), app (99.95%), DB (99.9%), cache (99.9%):
```
A = 0.9999 · 0.9995 · 0.999 · 0.999 = 0.9974 ≈ 99.74%
```
That's worse than the worst component (99.9%). Series dependencies erode availability: every hop you add subtracts nines. This is the hidden cost of microservices and deep call chains.

**Components in parallel / redundancy (system is up if *any* replica is up):** *un*availabilities multiply.
```
A_total = 1 - (1 - A)^N      (N independent redundant replicas)
```
Two replicas at 99% each:
```
A = 1 - (1 - 0.99)^2 = 1 - 0.01^2 = 1 - 0.0001 = 0.9999 = 99.99%
```
Two cheap 99% boxes in parallel give four nines; redundancy is how you buy availability you can't get from a single component. Three replicas: `1 - 0.01^3 = 99.9999%`. (Caveat: this assumes *independent* failures. Correlated failures, like the same datacenter power loss or the same bad deploy, break the math. Real redundancy means spreading across failure domains: zones, regions, deploy waves.)

**Design takeaway:** keep critical paths short (fewer series hops), and make each hop redundant (parallel replicas across failure domains). That is the core discipline of HA architecture.

### Back-of-the-envelope capacity estimation - a methodology

Follow a fixed recipe so you never freeze in a design interview or planning meeting:

1. **Clarify scope & assumptions.** DAU, actions/user/day, read:write ratio, object sizes, retention.
2. **QPS.** total ops/day → /86,400 → average; ×peak_factor → peak. Split reads vs writes.
3. **Storage.** writes/day × bytes/write × retention (× replication factor). Project growth (×3 for years).
4. **Bandwidth.** QPS × payload size, in and out, in MB/s and Gbps.
5. **Memory / cache.** Apply 80/20: ~20% of data serves ~80% of reads → cache that hot set; size it.
6. **Servers.** peak QPS / per-server capacity, then add redundancy headroom (N+1 or N+2).

Round aggressively. The goal is the right *order of magnitude*, not a spreadsheet. Useful constants to memorize:
```
seconds/day  = 86,400  (≈ 1e5)
1 KB = 1e3 B, 1 MB = 1e6 B, 1 GB = 1e9 B, 1 TB = 1e12 B
1 char ≈ 1 byte (ASCII); a UUID ≈ 16 B raw / 36 chars
1 Gbps ≈ 125 MB/s
1 million/day ≈ 11.6 per second
```

### Common mistakes

These are the scalability sins you'll catch yourself (and everyone else) committing. Each one has burned a real production system somewhere.

1. **Designing for the average, not the peak.** Average QPS is comforting and irrelevant. The market opens at 9:30am and everyone hits the dashboard at once; the Super Bowl ad airs; the cron job fires at midnight. If you provision for average and traffic spikes 4×, you fall over exactly when it matters most. *Always size for peak = average × peak_factor (typically 2-5×), then add headroom on top of that.*

2. **Ignoring tail latency (optimizing the mean).** A green "avg = 12 ms" dashboard while p99 = 900 ms means 1% of users are suffering, often your most active users, the ones who make the most requests. At scale, the tail *is* the common experience (see the fan-out math: 100 calls × 1% slow ≈ 63% of pages slow). *Watch p99/p99.9, not the mean.*

3. **Running at 90-95% utilization "to save money."** Queueing theory is unforgiving: at ρ = 0.95, latency is ~20× the service time; at 0.99 it's ~100×. "We have spare CPU" is a lie when the queue is exploding. *Target 50-70% steady-state so spikes have somewhere to go.*

4. **Conflating latency and throughput.** "Make it faster" is ambiguous. Do you want each request quicker (latency) or more requests served (throughput)? They have *different fixes* (faster work vs. more parallel workers). Saying which one you mean prevents building the wrong thing.

5. **Assuming throughput = 1 / latency.** Only true for a single, serial worker. In any concurrent system, `throughput = concurrency / latency` (Little's Law). A 100 ms request with 200-way concurrency does 2,000 req/s, not 10. Forgetting concurrency leads to wildly under-provisioned pools.

6. **Assuming failures are independent.** The parallel-redundancy math (`A = 1 − (1−A)^N`) assumes replicas fail independently. But two replicas in the same rack share a power supply; two in the same region share a fiber cut; two on the same deploy share the same bad release. *Correlated failures collapse your nines.* Real redundancy means spreading across failure domains (zones, regions, deploy waves).

7. **Forgetting the replication factor (and overhead) in storage math.** "50 TB/day of photos" is the *logical* size. With 3× replication for durability it's 150 TB/day on disk, and that's before indexes, compression ratios, write amplification, and backups. *Storage estimates that skip the replication factor are off by 3× or more.* Same trap with bandwidth: count *both* ingress and egress.

8. **Treating availability as a single number for the whole system.** Series dependencies *multiply* (each hop subtracts nines), so a chain of "99.9% each" services is worse than 99.9%. Conversely, not every path needs the same nines: NYX's analytics dashboard can be 99.9% while the trade-execution path is 99.99%. *Budget availability per critical path, and shorten the critical paths.*

### On the job / interview angle

Capacity estimation is the single most common opening of a system-design interview, and a daily reflex on the job. Here's how it shows up and how to talk about it.

**In the interview.** The interviewer says "Design Twitter / a URL shortener / Instagram." The strongest candidates don't jump to boxes-and-arrows; they spend the first 5-10 minutes doing exactly the back-of-envelope from this chapter, out loud. Interviewers want to see you:

- **Drive the scope yourself.** "Let me assume 100M DAU, each posting ~0.5×/day and reading ~100×/day. Read-heavy, roughly 100:1. Sound right?" You state assumptions, then invite correction. This shows you know real systems are read-heavy and that numbers come from product behavior, not thin air.
- **Walk the recipe.** QPS (avg → ×peak), then storage (× replication, × retention), then bandwidth (Gbps), then servers (peak / per-node, + headroom). Narrate each step. The structure itself is the signal: it says "I've done this before."
- **Let the numbers drive the architecture.** The most senior move is to say "egress is ~2 Tbps from origin, that's impossible, so a **CDN is mandatory**," or "410 PB doesn't go in Postgres, so blobs go to object storage and only metadata goes in a sharded DB." The estimate *discovers* the design. Interviewers love this because it shows the math isn't ceremony; it's load-bearing.
- **Round aggressively and say so.** "Call it ~580 QPS, round to 600." Precision is a tell that you're missing the point; the goal is the right *order of magnitude*. Memorized constants (86,400 s/day; 1M/day ≈ 11.6 QPS; 1 Gbps ≈ 125 MB/s) make you fast and credible.

**Phrases interviewers reward:** "Let me design for *peak*, not average." "I'll watch p99, not the mean, because of tail-at-scale." "These are in *series* so availabilities multiply, I'll make the weak link redundant." "I'll keep the app tier *stateless* so I can scale horizontally." "Let me add N+2 headroom and run at ~60% utilization."

**On the job.** Same skill, lower stakes per use but higher frequency: sizing a new service before launch, deciding how many pods to autoscale to, justifying a cloud bill, doing a "can our current fleet survive the holiday spike?" capacity review, or writing the back-of-envelope at the top of a design doc so reviewers can sanity-check it. The engineers who get trusted with big systems are the ones who reach for a number *first* and can defend where it came from.

## Build it

A load-tester that reports p50/p95/p99 + throughput, plus availability calculators. Pure standard library so you can drop it anywhere.

```python
# perf_toolkit.py
import time
import random
import math
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor
from dataclasses import dataclass


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Percentiles done right (nearest-rank, no interpolation surprises)
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
def percentile(sorted_samples, p):
    """p in [0,100]. Nearest-rank method on an already-sorted list."""
    if not sorted_samples:
        raise ValueError("no samples")
    # rank = ceil(p/100 * N), clamped to [1, N]
    rank = max(1, math.ceil((p / 100.0) * len(sorted_samples)))
    return sorted_samples[rank - 1]


@dataclass
class LoadResult:
    count: int
    wall_seconds: float
    throughput: float          # completed ops / sec
    p50: float
    p95: float
    p99: float
    p999: float
    mean: float
    max: float

    def report(self):
        print(f"  requests      : {self.count}")
        print(f"  wall time     : {self.wall_seconds*1000:8.1f} ms")
        print(f"  throughput    : {self.throughput:8.1f} req/s")
        print(f"  mean latency  : {self.mean*1000:8.2f} ms   <- often misleading")
        print(f"  p50  (median) : {self.p50*1000:8.2f} ms")
        print(f"  p95           : {self.p95*1000:8.2f} ms")
        print(f"  p99           : {self.p99*1000:8.2f} ms")
        print(f"  p99.9         : {self.p999*1000:8.2f} ms")
        print(f"  max           : {self.max*1000:8.2f} ms")


def load_test(fn, n=2000, concurrency=20):
    """Fire `fn` n times across `concurrency` workers; measure per-call latency."""
    latencies = []

    def timed_call(_):
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        fn()
        return time.perf_counter() - t0

    wall0 = time.perf_counter()
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=concurrency) as pool:
        for dt in pool.map(timed_call, range(n)):
            latencies.append(dt)
    wall = time.perf_counter() - wall0

    latencies.sort()
    return LoadResult(
        count=n,
        wall_seconds=wall,
        throughput=n / wall,
        p50=percentile(latencies, 50),
        p95=percentile(latencies, 95),
        p99=percentile(latencies, 99),
        p999=percentile(latencies, 99.9),
        mean=sum(latencies) / len(latencies),
        max=latencies[-1],
    )

# 2. A "service" with a nasty tail: fast most of the time, slow 1%.
#    This demonstrates exactly why averages lie.
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
def flaky_service():
    if random.random() < 0.01:        # 1% of calls hit a slow path (GC, cache miss, etc.)
        time.sleep(0.150)
    else:
        time.sleep(0.010)


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Availability calculators
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
def downtime_per_year(availability):
    """Return (days, hours, minutes) of downtime for a given availability."""
    minutes_year = 365 * 24 * 60
    down_min = (1 - availability) * minutes_year
    return {
        "downtime_min_per_year": round(down_min, 2),
        "downtime_hours_per_year": round(down_min / 60, 2),
        "downtime_min_per_month": round(down_min / 12, 2),
        "downtime_sec_per_day": round((1 - availability) * 86400, 2),
    }


def availability_series(*components):
    """A request must pass through ALL components -> multiply availabilities."""
    a = 1.0
    for c in components:
        a *= c
    return a


def availability_parallel(component_a, n):
    """System up if ANY of n identical redundant replicas is up."""
    return 1 - (1 - component_a) ** n


def littles_law(L=None, lam=None, W=None):
    """Solve L = lam * W for whichever argument is None."""
    if L is None:
        return lam * W
    if lam is None:
        return L / W
    if W is None:
        return L / lam
    raise ValueError("leave exactly one of L, lam, W as None")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("=== Load test of a service with a 1% slow tail ===")
    res = load_test(flaky_service, n=3000, concurrency=50)
    res.report()
    print("  Note: mean ~", round(res.mean*1000,1),
          "ms but p99 ~", round(res.p99*1000,1),
          "ms. The tail is 15x the median.\n")

    print("=== Availability: the nines ===")
    for a in (0.99, 0.999, 0.9999, 0.99999):
        print(f"  {a*100:8.3f}% ->", downtime_per_year(a))

    print("\n=== Series (LB -> app -> DB -> cache) ===")
    a_series = availability_series(0.9999, 0.9995, 0.999, 0.999)
    print(f"  combined = {a_series*100:.3f}%  (worse than the worst link 99.9%)")

    print("\n=== Parallel redundancy (two 99% boxes) ===")
    print(f"  1 box  = 99.000%")
    print(f"  2 box  = {availability_parallel(0.99, 2)*100:.4f}%")
    print(f"  3 box  = {availability_parallel(0.99, 3)*100:.6f}%")

    print("\n=== Little's Law ===")
    print(f"  L=80 in-flight, W=0.2s  -> throughput lam = {littles_law(L=80, W=0.2):.0f} req/s")
    print(f"  want lam=1000, W=0.2s   -> need L = {littles_law(lam=1000, W=0.2):.0f} in flight")
```

Run it and the numbers make the point: the mean hovers near 11-12 ms while p99 sits near 150 ms. If you only watched the mean dashboard, you'd swear the service was healthy while 1% of NYX traders saw a frozen screen.

### A worked availability design

Say NYX's read path is `CDN → LB → app → cache → DB`. Component availabilities: CDN 99.99%, LB 99.99%, app tier 99.95%, cache 99.95%, DB 99.9%. Naively in series:
```
0.9999 · 0.9999 · 0.9995 · 0.9995 · 0.999 = 0.99780 ≈ 99.78%  →  ~19 hours down/year
```
To fix it, make the *weak link* (DB at 99.9%) redundant with a replica and failover. Two independent DBs in parallel: `1 - (1-0.999)^2 = 99.9999%`. Now the series product is dominated by the app/cache tier; replicate those too (they're stateless / clustered):
```
app:   1-(1-0.9995)^2 = 99.999975%
cache: 1-(1-0.9995)^2 = 99.999975%
DB:    1-(1-0.999)^2  = 99.9999%
series with CDN/LB ≈ 99.98%   →  still bounded by single-instance CDN/LB
```
The lesson repeats: **the system is only as available as its least-redundant series hop.** Find the single points of failure and replicate them across failure domains.

## Mini-project: full capacity estimate for a realistic service

Let's size a **photo-sharing / media feed service** end to end (a generic but realistic target). You'll state every assumption.

**Assumptions**
- 100M DAU.
- Each user posts 1 photo every other day → 0.5 uploads/user/day → 50M uploads/day (writes).
- Read:write ratio = 100:1 (people view far more than they post) → 5B reads/day.
- Average photo stored size (after compression + thumbnails) = 1.5 MB.
- Metadata per photo ≈ 1 KB.
- Retention: keep forever; project 5 years.
- Replication factor for storage = 3 (durability).

**1. QPS**
```
Writes/day = 50,000,000
QPS_write_avg = 50e6 / 86400 ≈ 579 write QPS
Reads/day  = 5,000,000,000
QPS_read_avg  = 5e9 / 86400 ≈ 57,870 read QPS
Peak factor ≈ 3x:
  peak write QPS ≈ 1,740
  peak read  QPS ≈ 173,600   (~174k QPS reads - heavy!)
```

**2. Storage**
```
Per day (raw) = 50e6 uploads × 1.5 MB = 75e6 MB = 75 TB/day
With 3x replication = 225 TB/day
Per year ≈ 225 TB × 365 ≈ 82 PB/year
Over 5 years ≈ 410 PB.   (round: ~0.4 exabyte) -> object storage / blob store territory, NOT a SQL DB.
Metadata: 50e6 × 1 KB = 50 GB/day -> ~18 TB/year -> fits a sharded DB easily.
```
Insight: photos belong in blob storage (S3-like), metadata in a database. The back-of-envelope already forced the right architecture.

**3. Bandwidth**
```
Egress (reads): peak 173,600 read QPS × 1.5 MB
  = 260,400 MB/s = 260 GB/s ≈ 2,080 Gbps   <- huge; this screams CDN.
Ingress (writes): peak 1,740 × 1.5 MB = 2,610 MB/s ≈ 21 Gbps.
```
Egress at ~2 Tbps is impossible/ruinous from origin servers, so **a CDN is mandatory** (Chapter 9.2). With a 95% CDN hit rate, origin egress drops to ~104 Gbps, manageable.

**4. Memory / cache (80/20)**
```
Hot working set = ~20% of recent metadata reads. Cache feed metadata, not blobs.
If active feed metadata ~ 200 GB hot, a Redis cluster of a few hundred GB covers it.
```

**5. Servers (app tier, read path)**
```
Assume one app node serves ~5,000 req/s (light metadata + redirect to CDN URLs).
Read nodes needed = 173,600 / 5,000 ≈ 35 nodes.
Add N+2 redundancy and headroom (run at 60% util): 35 / 0.6 ≈ 58 → ~60 read nodes.
Write nodes: 1,740 / 2,000 (writes heavier) ≈ 1 → run 3 for redundancy.
```

**Summary table**
```
Metric            Value
----------------- -----------------------------
Write QPS (peak)  ~1,740
Read QPS (peak)   ~174,000
Storage (5 yr)    ~410 PB (blob store, 3x repl)
Metadata storage  ~18 TB/year (sharded DB)
Egress (peak)     ~2 Tbps -> CDN mandatory (95% offload)
App nodes         ~60 read + 3 write (with redundancy)
Cache             few hundred GB Redis (hot feed metadata)
```

That whole estimate took five minutes of arithmetic and surfaced the three load-bearing decisions: blob store for photos, CDN for egress, sharded DB + Redis for metadata. The back-of-envelope is an architecture-discovery tool, not a finance exercise.


### A second estimate: a real-time chat service (different shape, same recipe)

The media feed was read-heavy and storage-dominated. A chat app has a totally different profile: it's write-heavy, the payloads are tiny, and the hard part is the persistent connections, not the bytes. Running the same recipe surfaces a different architecture, which is why you practice on contrasting services.

**Assumptions**
- 50M DAU.
- Each active user sends **40 messages/day** (chat is chatty).
- Each message is read by ~**2 recipients** on average (mix of 1:1 and small groups) → reads ≈ 2× writes.
- Average message size = **200 bytes** (short text + metadata overhead).
- Retention: keep messages **1 year**, then archive.
- Replication factor = **3** for the message store.
- ~**10%** of DAU are *connected simultaneously* at peak (holding a live socket).

**1. QPS (write-heavy this time)**
```
Messages sent/day = 50,000,000 × 40 = 2,000,000,000 = 2B writes/day
QPS_write_avg = 2e9 / 86400 ≈ 23,150 write QPS
Reads/day = 2× writes = 4B/day
QPS_read_avg = 4e9 / 86400 ≈ 46,300 read QPS
Peak factor ≈ 3× (evenings cluster):
  peak write QPS ≈ 69,000
  peak read  QPS ≈ 139,000
  total peak     ≈ 208,000 QPS
```

**2. Storage**
```
Per day (logical) = 2e9 msgs × 200 B = 4e11 B = 400 GB/day
With 3× replication        = 1.2 TB/day
Per year (1 yr retention)  = 400 GB × 365 ≈ 146 TB logical → ~438 TB on disk (3×)
```
Insight: tiny per-message, but 2B/day adds up. 438 TB fits comfortably in a **sharded NoSQL store** (Cassandra/DynamoDB-style, partitioned by conversation_id), *not* object storage (objects too small) and *not* a single SQL box (too many writes). The write-heaviness pushes you toward a log-structured, append-friendly store.

**3. Bandwidth**
```
Ingress (writes): peak 69,000 × 200 B = 13.8 MB/s ≈ 0.11 Gbps
Egress  (reads):  peak 139,000 × 200 B = 27.8 MB/s ≈ 0.22 Gbps
Total ≈ 0.33 Gbps of message payload.
```
Insight: bandwidth is a non-issue here, just a third of a gigabit, the opposite of the photo service. No CDN needed for messages (they're tiny and personalized, so not cacheable anyway). The bytes are not the bottleneck.


**4. The real bottleneck: persistent connections**
```
Concurrent live sockets at peak = 10% × 50M DAU = 5,000,000 open WebSocket connections.
A well-tuned connection-gateway node holds ~50,000-100,000 sockets.
Gateway nodes = 5,000,000 / 75,000 ≈ 67 nodes.
Add N+2 + headroom (run at 60%): 67 / 0.6 ≈ 112 → ~115 gateway nodes.
```
Insight: the recipe forces the realization that this is a connection-management problem, not a throughput or storage problem. The expensive resource is open sockets and the memory/fan-out to push messages to them, which is why real chat systems (WhatsApp famously) obsess over connections-per-server, not GB/s.

**5. Memory / fan-out**
```
Need a presence/routing layer mapping user_id → which gateway holds their socket.
50M users × ~100 B presence record ≈ 5 GB → trivially fits in a Redis cluster.
Pub/sub (Redis or a message bus like Kafka) fans a new message to the recipients' gateways.
```

**6. App/write servers**
```
Assume one write-path node ingests ~3,000 msg/s (durable write + fan-out enqueue).
Write nodes = 69,000 / 3,000 ≈ 23 → with headroom (÷0.6) ≈ 38 → ~40 write nodes.
```

**Summary table**
```
Metric              Value
------------------- ----------------------------------------
Write QPS (peak)    ~69,000      (write-heavy! opposite of media)
Read QPS (peak)     ~139,000
Total QPS (peak)    ~208,000
Storage (1 yr)      ~438 TB on disk (3× repl) → sharded NoSQL
Bandwidth (peak)    ~0.33 Gbps   → bytes are NOT the bottleneck
Concurrent sockets  ~5,000,000   → THE real constraint
Gateway nodes       ~115         (WebSocket fan-out)
Write nodes         ~40
Presence/routing    ~5 GB Redis + pub/sub bus
```

Side-by-side, the two estimates explain why "it depends" is the honest answer in system design:

```
                Media feed                Chat app
Shape           read-heavy (100:1)        write-heavy (1:2)
Bottleneck      egress bandwidth (CDN)    open connections (gateways)
Storage         410 PB blobs (S3-like)    438 TB messages (sharded NoSQL)
Bandwidth       ~2 Tbps  → CDN mandatory  ~0.33 Gbps → trivial
Hardest part    moving big bytes cheaply  holding millions of sockets
```

Same six-step recipe, radically different architectures, because the numbers are different. That's what the back-of-envelope buys you: you don't memorize architectures, you derive them from the workload.

## Exercises

Work these on paper first (napkin arithmetic is the whole skill), then check against the solutions. The constants to keep handy: 86,400 s/day, 1M/day ≈ 11.6 QPS, 1 Gbps ≈ 125 MB/s, 1 year ≈ 8,760 h ≈ 525,600 min.

1. **Little's Law, forward and backward.** A payments service measures average latency **W = 40 ms** and observes **L = 240** requests in flight on average. (a) What is its throughput? (b) The business wants to triple throughput while *keeping latency at 40 ms*, how many in-flight requests must the system support, and what concretely has to change?

2. **Thread-pool sizing.** A worker calls a downstream API that takes **W = 500 ms** (purely I/O-blocked) and must sustain **λ = 300 req/s**. How many concurrent tasks does the pool need (add ~20% headroom)? If the box has 8 CPU cores, explain why "8 threads" is the wrong answer here but might be right for CPU-bound work.

3. **Utilization danger zone.** A single server has service time **1/µ = 8 ms**. Using the M/M/1 formula `W = (1/µ)/(1−ρ)`, compute the average time-in-system at ρ = 0.5, 0.8, 0.9, and 0.98. By what factor does latency grow going from 80% to 98% utilization, and what steady-state utilization would you target?

4. **Tail at scale.** A leaf service has **p99 = 1%** chance of being slow on any call. (a) If a page fans out to **30** such calls, what fraction of page loads hit at least one slow call? (b) How many fan-out calls before *more than half* of all pages are slow? (Solve `1 − 0.99^n > 0.5`.)

5. **Availability from incidents.** A service had **4 outages** last year totaling **20 hours** of downtime. Compute MTTR, MTBF, and the availability (in nines). If the target is 99.99%, which lever (fewer failures vs. faster repair) gets you there with less change, justify with a number.

6. **Series vs. parallel.** A request flows through CDN (99.99%) → LB (99.99%) → app (99.9%) → DB (99.5%). (a) Compute the combined series availability. (b) The DB is the weak link, put it behind two independent replicas (`A = 1−(1−A)^N`) and recompute the chain. (c) State the one assumption that, if false, breaks your parallel-redundancy gain.

7. **Mini back-of-envelope: a ride-share service.** Assume **5M DAU**, each requesting **2 rides/day**. Each ride generates **~100 location pings** during the trip, each ping **100 bytes**. Pings are stored with **3× replication**, retained **90 days**. Estimate: (a) ride-request peak QPS (peak factor 3×), (b) location-ping peak QPS, (c) ping storage over 90 days, (d) peak ping ingress bandwidth in Gbps. What's the bottleneck, ride requests or pings?

8. **Capacity-to-architecture.** From your answers in #7, name the *one* architectural decision the numbers force (think about which subsystem dominates), and say why a single SQL database is or isn't the right home for the location pings.

### Solutions

**1. Little's Law.**
(a) `λ = L / W = 240 / 0.040 s = 6,000 req/s.`
(b) Triple throughput at constant latency: `L = λ·W = 18,000 × 0.040 = 720 in-flight requests.` So the connection/thread/worker pool must support 720 concurrent in-flight requests (up from 240). Concretely: bigger worker/connection pools *and* enough downstream capacity (DB connections, etc.) to actually run 720 at once, otherwise requests just queue and latency blows past 40 ms, violating the constraint.

**2. Thread-pool sizing.**
`L = λ·W = 300 × 0.5 = 150` concurrent tasks; +20% headroom → **~180 tasks**. Eight threads is wrong because the work is **I/O-blocked**: each task spends ~all 500 ms waiting on the network, using almost no CPU, so 8 threads cap throughput at `8 / 0.5 = 16 req/s`, 19× short. Since blocked sockets are cheap, you can hold ~180 in flight on 8 cores. For **CPU-bound** work the logic flips: tasks burn the core the whole time, so more tasks than cores just thrash the scheduler, there the answer is ~one worker per core and *more machines* to scale out.

**3. Utilization danger zone.**
```
ρ = 0.50 → W = 8 / 0.50 = 16 ms
ρ = 0.80 → W = 8 / 0.20 = 40 ms
ρ = 0.90 → W = 8 / 0.10 = 80 ms
ρ = 0.98 → W = 8 / 0.02 = 400 ms
```
From 80% → 98%, latency grows 40 ms → 400 ms = **10×**. Target **~50-70%** steady-state so traffic spikes have headroom before the queue explodes.

**4. Tail at scale.**
(a) `P(page slow) = 1 − 0.99^30 = 1 − 0.7397 ≈ 0.260 → ~26% of pages hit a slow call.`
(b) Solve `1 − 0.99^n > 0.5` → `0.99^n < 0.5` → `n > ln(0.5)/ln(0.99) = (−0.6931)/(−0.01005) ≈ 68.97`. So at **n = 69** fan-out calls, *more than half* of all pages contain at least one slow call, from a "rare" 1% leaf event. That's tail-at-scale.

**5. Availability from incidents.**
`MTTR = 20 h / 4 = 5 h per incident.` Uptime = 8,760 − 20 = 8,740 h → `MTBF = 8,740 / 4 = 2,185 h (~91 days).`
`A = MTBF/(MTBF+MTTR) = 2185/(2185+5) = 2185/2190 = 0.99772 ≈ 99.77%` (just under three nines; ~20 h/yr down, as given).
To reach **99.99%** (~52 min/yr):
- *Faster repair:* cut MTTR to **0.1 h (6 min)** → `A = 2185/2185.1 = 99.9954%`, basically there, with no change to failure rate.
- *Fewer failures:* even halving to 2 failures/yr → MTBF ≈ 4,370 h, but with MTTR still 5 h, `A = 4370/4375 = 99.886%`, *not* four nines.
**MTTR is the cheaper lever here:** automated rollback/failover (6-min recovery) beats halving the failure count. (Generally true when MTTR is large relative to the gap you need to close.)

**6. Series vs. parallel.**
(a) `A = 0.9999 × 0.9999 × 0.999 × 0.995 = 0.99381 ≈ 99.38%` (~54 h/yr, dragged down by the 99.5% DB).
(b) Two independent DB replicas: `A_db = 1 − (1−0.995)^2 = 1 − 0.005^2 = 1 − 0.000025 = 0.999975 ≈ 99.9975%.` New chain: `0.9999 × 0.9999 × 0.999 × 0.999975 = 0.99878 ≈ 99.88%`, the DB is no longer the weak link; the app tier (99.9%) now dominates, so it'd be the next thing to replicate.
(c) The gain assumes the two DB replicas **fail independently**. If they share a failure domain (same rack/power/zone, same bad deploy, same poison query), failures correlate and the `(1−A)^2` term doesn't hold, spread replicas across zones/regions to keep them independent.

**7. Ride-share back-of-envelope.**
```
(a) Ride requests:
    rides/day = 5M × 2 = 10,000,000/day
    avg QPS = 10e6 / 86400 ≈ 116 QPS
    peak (×3) ≈ 347 QPS                      ← tiny
(b) Location pings:
    pings/day = 10e6 rides × 100 = 1,000,000,000 = 1B/day
    avg QPS = 1e9 / 86400 ≈ 11,574 QPS
    peak (×3) ≈ 34,700 ping QPS              ← 100× the ride QPS
(c) Ping storage (90 days, 3× repl):
    per day logical = 1e9 × 100 B = 100 GB/day
    × 3 replication = 300 GB/day
    × 90 days       = 27 TB on disk
(d) Peak ping ingress:
    34,700 QPS × 100 B = 3.47 MB/s ≈ 0.028 Gbps   ← negligible bandwidth
```
Bottleneck: the location pings, not ride requests. Pings outnumber rides 100:1 in QPS (~35k vs ~350). Bandwidth is trivial; the pressure is write throughput (34,700 small writes/sec) and the growing time-series store.

**8. Capacity-to-architecture.**
The numbers force a high-write-throughput, time-series / geospatial store for the pings, partitioned by ride_id or by geo-cell + time, ideally append-only (Cassandra/Dynamo-style, or a purpose-built time-series DB), with TTL-based expiry at 90 days. A single SQL database is the wrong home: 35k small writes/sec with continuous 100 GB/day ingest will saturate one box's write path and churn its b-tree indexes, and the data is write-once/read-rarely with a natural expiry, exactly the access pattern relational engines handle worst and log-structured stores handle best. (The ride *requests* at ~350 QPS, by contrast, are happily a normal SQL workload.)
