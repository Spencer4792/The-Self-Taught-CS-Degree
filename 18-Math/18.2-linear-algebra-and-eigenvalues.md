## Start here - the on-ramp (vectors and matrices without the fear)

Two objects, two ideas. That's the core.

**A vector is a list of numbers that also means something geometric.** `[3, 4]` is simultaneously: (a) a point in 2D space, 3 right and 4 up; (b) an arrow from the origin to that point; (c) a data record, maybe `[height=3, weight=4]` for one person; (d) a direction-and-magnitude. In ML, a vector is usually a *data point* (one row of your dataset) or an *embedding* (a learned numerical fingerprint of a word, image, or user). When we say "documents about cats cluster together," we mean their vectors point in similar directions, and "similar direction" has an exact numerical meaning (the dot product) that we'll derive.

**A matrix is a machine that transforms space.** This is the big reframe most courses bury. A matrix isn't just a grid of numbers, it's a *function that takes vectors in and spits transformed vectors out*. Multiply a vector by a matrix and the matrix *rotates it, stretches it, shears it, flips it, or projects it.* A rotation matrix spins every arrow in the plane. A scaling matrix stretches space like taffy. When you picture a matrix, you should picture *what it does to a grid of points*, does it rotate the grid? squash it flat? flip it inside out? That picture explains *everything* downstream: matrix multiplication is "do one transform, then another," the determinant is "how much the transform scales area," eigenvectors are "the directions the transform leaves pointing the same way."

Here's the mental model to carry through the whole chapter: imagine a sheet of graph paper with a cat drawn on it. A matrix is an operation on that whole sheet, it can spin it, stretch it, shear it into a parallelogram. Vectors are arrows drawn on the sheet, and the matrix moves their tips along with the paper. Almost every theorem in this chapter is a fact about what happens to that cat-on-graph-paper.

Why this matters concretely:
- **Graphics**: every rotation, scale, and camera projection is a matrix multiply.
- **ML**: a neural-net layer is `output = W·input + b`, a matrix transform plus a shift. Stack them with nonlinearities and you have deep learning (14.2).
- **Data**: a dataset is a matrix (rows = samples, columns = features). PCA, recommendations, and image compression are all about finding the *important directions* in that matrix, which is exactly what eigenvalues and SVD reveal.

Keep the cat-on-graph-paper in mind. We're going to spin it, stretch it, and eventually compress it.

## Vectors - geometry and data, two views of one object

### The two interpretations

A vector `v = [v₁, v₂, …, v_n]` lives in `n`-dimensional space `ℝⁿ`. Two readings, both true:
- **Geometric**: an arrow from the origin with a direction and a length. In 2D/3D you can literally draw it.
- **Data**: a record of `n` measurements, one customer's `[age, income, visits]`, or a word's 300-dimensional embedding.

The power of linear algebra is that **geometric intuition transfers to data**. "These two customers are similar" becomes "their vectors point the same way," and the geometry gives you a formula for it.

### Operations and what they mean

**Addition**, `[1,2] + [3,1] = [4,3]`. Geometrically, tip-to-tail: walk along the first arrow, then the second. It's *combining* movements or summing features.

**Scalar multiplication**, `3·[1,2] = [3,6]`. Stretches the arrow (longer, same direction); a negative scalar flips it. It's *scaling* a quantity.

**The dot product, the most important operation in ML.**

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \sum_i a_i b_i = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \cdots + a_n b_n.$$

A single number from two vectors. But the *meaning* is the gold, and there's a second formula that reveals it:

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$$

where `θ` is the angle between the two arrows. **The dot product measures alignment.** Same direction (`θ=0`, `cos θ = 1`) → large positive. Perpendicular (`θ=90°`, `cos θ = 0`) → **zero** (this is the definition of *orthogonal*, perpendicular vectors have zero dot product, a fact we'll use constantly). Opposite (`θ=180°`) → large negative.

This is *why* the dot product is everywhere: it's a **similarity score**. Two word embeddings with a large dot product point the same way → similar meaning. A neuron computes `w·x` → "how aligned is this input with what the neuron looks for." The gradient's "steepest ascent" proof in 18.1 was a dot product. The dot product is the workhorse.

**Projection**, the dot product also gives the *shadow* of one vector onto another. The scalar projection of `a` onto `b` is `(a·b)/‖b‖`, how much of `a` points along `b`. This is the geometric heart of least squares, Fourier analysis, and PCA: "how much of this thing lies along that direction."

**Norms (length).** The **L2 norm** (Euclidean length):

$$\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2} = \sqrt{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}.$$

Pythagoras in `n` dimensions. The **L1 norm** `‖v‖₁ = Σ|vᵢ|` (Manhattan/taxicab distance) shows up in Lasso regularization. Distance between points is the norm of their difference: `‖a − b‖`.

**Cosine similarity, embeddings live and die by this.**

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|} \in [-1, 1].$$

Just the dot product *normalized* by both lengths, it isolates the *angle*, ignoring magnitude. This is the standard similarity metric for embeddings (words, documents, images, users) because it asks "do these point the same way?" without caring how long the vectors are. A short vector and a long vector pointing identically have cosine similarity 1. Every semantic search engine and recommendation system ranks by cosine similarity.

### Build it: dot products, norms, and cosine similarity

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
b = np.array([4.0, 5.0, 6.0])

print("dot product :", np.dot(a, b))              # 1*4 + 2*5 + 3*6 = 32
print("L2 norm of a:", np.linalg.norm(a))         # sqrt(1+4+9) = 3.7417
print("L1 norm of a:", np.linalg.norm(a, 1))      # 1+2+3 = 6

def cosine_similarity(u, v):
    return np.dot(u, v) / (np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(v))

# Toy "embeddings": cat-like, dog-like, and finance-like documents
cat     = np.array([0.9, 0.1, 0.0])
kitten  = np.array([0.8, 0.2, 0.0])     # similar direction to cat
invoice = np.array([0.0, 0.1, 0.95])    # very different direction
print("\ncos(cat, kitten) :", round(cosine_similarity(cat, kitten), 3))   # ~0.99
print("cos(cat, invoice):", round(cosine_similarity(cat, invoice), 3))    # ~0.04

# Orthogonality check: perpendicular vectors have dot product 0
print("\n[1,0]·[0,1] =", np.dot([1,0],[0,1]))     # 0  -> perpendicular
```

`cat` and `kitten` score ~0.99 (point the same way → similar), `cat` and `invoice` score ~0.04 (nearly perpendicular → unrelated). **That is semantic search in three lines.** Every vector database (Pinecone, FAISS) is, at heart, "find the embeddings with the highest cosine similarity to my query vector."

## Matrices as linear transformations - what a matrix DOES to space

This is the conceptual core of the chapter. Most people learn a matrix as a grid of numbers and memorize a multiplication rule. You're going to learn it as a **transformation of space**, and the rule will *fall out* for free.

### The key idea: a matrix tells you where the basis vectors land

In 2D, every vector is built from two **basis vectors**: `î = [1,0]` (one step right) and `ĵ = [0,1]` (one step up). Any vector `[x,y] = x·î + y·ĵ`.

A **linear transformation** is a way of moving space that keeps the origin fixed and keeps grid lines straight and evenly spaced (no curving). The magic fact: **a linear transformation is *completely determined* by where it sends `î` and `ĵ`.** Because if you know where `î` and `ĵ` land, then `[x,y]` lands at `x·(new î) + y·(new ĵ)`, linearity does the rest.

So you store a transformation as a matrix whose **columns are where the basis vectors land**:

$$M = \begin{bmatrix} \uparrow & \uparrow \\ M\hat{i} & M\hat{j} \\ \downarrow & \downarrow \end{bmatrix}.$$

That's what a matrix *is*: a record of where the basis vectors go. Applying the matrix to a vector `[x,y]`:

$$M\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = x\cdot(\text{column 1}) + y\cdot(\text{column 2}).$$

This isn't a memorized rule, it's "scale each landing spot by the corresponding coordinate and add." **Matrix-vector multiplication is a weighted sum of the columns**, weighted by the input vector's coordinates. Every other rule in this chapter descends from this one idea.

**Worked by hand.** Take the shear `M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}` and apply it to `v = [2, 3]`. Column 1 is `[1,0]` (where `î` lands, unchanged), column 2 is `[1,1]` (where `ĵ` lands, slid right). The weighted sum:
$$M\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} + 3\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}3\\3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}5\\3\end{bmatrix}.$$
The `x`-coordinate got shoved right by 3 (the amount of `y`), the `y`-coordinate stayed, exactly what a shear does. Notice we never used the "row dot column" recipe; we built the output by *combining the columns*. That's the truer picture. (The row-dot-column rule gives the same answer, `[1·2 + 1·3, 0·2 + 1·3] = [5,3]`, but it hides the geometry. Both are correct; the column view is the one that *means* something.)

**The two ways to read a matrix-vector product**, both useful:
- **Column view** (above): output = a combination of the columns, weighted by the input. This view explains *span* (the output can only ever land in the span of the columns) and *rank* (how many independent columns there are).
- **Row view**: output entry `i` = (row `i`) · (input vector), a dot product. This view explains a neural-net layer: each *neuron* is a row, and its output is "how aligned is the input with what this neuron looks for." Both are the same arithmetic seen from two angles, and switching between them fluently is a sign you actually understand matrices rather than memorizing them.

### The transformations you should recognize on sight

- **Identity** `[[1,0],[0,1]]`, leaves everything put (`î→î`, `ĵ→ĵ`).
- **Scaling** `[[2,0],[0,3]]`, stretches x by 2, y by 3. The cat gets wide and tall.
- **Rotation by θ** `[[cos θ, −sin θ],[sin θ, cos θ]]`, spins everything counterclockwise by `θ`. The columns are exactly where `î` and `ĵ` land on a unit circle.
- **Shear** `[[1,1],[0,1]]`, `î` stays, `ĵ` slides right to `[1,1]`. Turns the square grid into a parallelogram; italic-izes the cat.
- **Reflection** `[[1,0],[0,−1]]`, flips across the x-axis (`ĵ → −ĵ`).
- **Projection** `[[1,0],[0,0]]`, squashes everything onto the x-axis (`ĵ → 0`). This one is *destructive*, it loses a dimension (foreshadowing: it's not invertible, and its determinant is 0).

### Build it: see what a matrix does to a shape

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# A unit square (4 corners) - our stand-in for the "cat on graph paper"
square = np.array([[0,0],[1,0],[1,1],[0,1],[0,0]]).T   # 2 x 5 (each column a point)

transforms = {
    "identity":    np.array([[1, 0], [0, 1]]),
    "scale 2x,1y": np.array([[2, 0], [0, 1]]),
    "rotate 45°":  np.array([[np.cos(np.pi/4), -np.sin(np.pi/4)],
                             [np.sin(np.pi/4),  np.cos(np.pi/4)]]),
    "shear":       np.array([[1, 1], [0, 1]]),
    "reflect-y":   np.array([[1, 0], [0, -1]]),
    "project-x":   np.array([[1, 0], [0, 0]]),    # collapses to a line!
}

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 3, figsize=(12, 8))
for ax, (name, M) in zip(axes.flat, transforms.items()):
    transformed = M @ square                       # apply the transform to every point
    ax.plot(square[0], square[1], 'b--', alpha=0.4, label='original')
    ax.plot(transformed[0], transformed[1], 'r-', lw=2, label='transformed')
    # show where the basis vectors land - the COLUMNS of M
    ax.quiver(0,0, M[0,0],M[1,0], color='green', scale=1, scale_units='xy', angles='xy')
    ax.quiver(0,0, M[0,1],M[1,1], color='orange', scale=1, scale_units='xy', angles='xy')
    ax.set_title(name); ax.set_xlim(-2,3); ax.set_ylim(-2,3)
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3); ax.axhline(0,color='k',lw=0.5); ax.axvline(0,color='k',lw=0.5)
    ax.set_aspect('equal')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

Watch the green and orange arrows (where `î` and `ĵ` land), they're literally the columns of each matrix, and the whole square deforms to follow them. The `project-x` case collapses the 2D square onto a 1D line: **information destroyed, dimension lost.** Hold that thought, it's the geometric meaning of a zero determinant and a non-invertible matrix.

### Matrix multiplication = composition of transforms (derived, not memorized)

Here's the question that demystifies the weird multiplication rule: **what if you apply transform `B`, then transform `A`?** You want a single matrix `C` that does both at once. Apply `B` to `v`, then `A` to the result: `A(Bv)`. You want `C` such that `Cv = A(Bv)` for all `v`.

To find `C`, you just ask "where do the basis vectors end up after `B` then `A`?" Column `j` of `C` is where basis vector `j` lands. After `B`, basis vector `j` is at column `j` of `B`. Then `A` transforms *that*. So:

$$\text{column } j \text{ of } C = A\cdot(\text{column } j \text{ of } B).$$

Work that out entry by entry and you get the familiar rule: `C_{ij} = Σ_k A_{ik} B_{kj}`, **row `i` of `A` dotted with column `j` of `B`.** But now you know *why*: **matrix multiplication is function composition.** `AB` means "do `B` first, then `A`" (right to left, like `f(g(x))`). That's why:
- Matrix multiplication is **not commutative**, `AB ≠ BA` in general, because "rotate then shear" differs from "shear then rotate" (do the cat-on-paper experiment in your head).
- It **is associative**, `(AB)C = A(BC)`, because composing functions is associative.
- The **inner dimensions must match**, `A` is `m×n`, `B` is `n×p`, because `A`'s input must be `B`'s output, dimensionally.

The mechanical rule was never arbitrary; it's the only definition that makes matrix multiplication *be* composition of transformations.

### Build it: composition both ways

```python
import numpy as np

R = np.array([[0, -1], [1, 0]])         # rotate 90° counterclockwise
S = np.array([[1, 1], [0, 1]])          # shear

print("R @ S (shear first, then rotate):\n", R @ S)
print("\nS @ R (rotate first, then shear):\n", S @ R)
print("\nSame? ", np.allclose(R @ S, S @ R))     # False - order matters!

v = np.array([1, 0])
print("\nR(S v) =", R @ (S @ v), "  equals (R@S) v =", (R @ S) @ v)  # identical
```

`R @ S ≠ S @ R`, concrete proof that transform order matters (a 90° rotation then a shear is a *different* operation than the reverse). And `R @ (S @ v) == (R @ S) @ v` confirms that the matrix product *is* the composition. This non-commutativity is *why* the order of layers and operations matters in graphics pipelines and neural nets.

## Systems of linear equations & Gaussian elimination

A system like

$$\begin{cases} 2x + y = 5 \\ x + 3y = 10 \end{cases}$$

is really the matrix equation `Ax = b` with `A = [[2,1],[1,3]]`, `x = [x,y]`, `b = [5,10]`. **Solving it geometrically means: "what input vector `x` does the transform `A` send to `b`?"** That's running the transformation *backward*.

**Gaussian elimination** solves it by systematically using row operations (scale a row, swap rows, add a multiple of one row to another, all of which preserve the solution set) to reach an upper-triangular form, then **back-substituting**. Let's build it from scratch, because implementing it is the best way to understand rank, invertibility, and why some systems have no solution.

### Build it: Gaussian elimination from scratch

```python
import numpy as np

def gaussian_elimination(A, b):
    """Solve Ax = b by elimination + back-substitution. Educational version
       with partial pivoting (swap to the largest pivot for numerical stability)."""
    A = A.astype(float).copy()
    b = b.astype(float).copy()
    n = len(b)

    # ---- Forward elimination: make A upper-triangular ----
    for col in range(n):
        # Partial pivoting: move the row with the largest |pivot| to the top
        pivot_row = np.argmax(np.abs(A[col:, col])) + col
        A[[col, pivot_row]] = A[[pivot_row, col]]
        b[[col, pivot_row]] = b[[pivot_row, col]]

        if np.isclose(A[col, col], 0):
            raise ValueError("Singular matrix - no unique solution (rank-deficient)")

        # Eliminate everything below the pivot
        for row in range(col + 1, n):
            factor = A[row, col] / A[col, col]
            A[row, col:] -= factor * A[col, col:]
            b[row]       -= factor * b[col]

    # ---- Back-substitution: solve from the bottom row up ----
    x = np.zeros(n)
    for row in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        x[row] = (b[row] - A[row, row+1:] @ x[row+1:]) / A[row, row]
    return x

A = np.array([[2, 1], [1, 3]])
b = np.array([5, 10])
x = gaussian_elimination(A, b)
print("Solution:", x)                          # [1. 3.]  -> x=1, y=3
print("Check A@x:", A @ x, "should equal", b)  # [5. 10.] ✓
print("NumPy says:", np.linalg.solve(A, b))    # same - np.linalg.solve does this (faster)
```

Three outcomes for `Ax = b`, each with a geometric meaning:
- **Exactly one solution**, `A` is invertible; the transform is reversible; the two lines cross at one point.
- **No solution**, the lines are parallel (inconsistent); `b` isn't in the reachable space.
- **Infinitely many**, the lines coincide; the system is under-determined.

### Span, basis, linear independence, and rank

These four words pin down "what space can a set of vectors reach":

- **Span**, all vectors you can build by scaling and adding a given set. Two non-parallel vectors in 2D span the whole plane (you can reach anywhere). Two *parallel* vectors span only a line.
- **Linear independence**, no vector in the set is a combination of the others; each adds a genuinely new direction. `[1,0]` and `[0,1]` are independent; `[1,0]` and `[2,0]` are *not* (the second is just `2×` the first, redundant).
- **Basis**, a minimal set of independent vectors that spans the space. `î, ĵ` is *the* standard basis for 2D. A basis is a coordinate system.
- **Rank**, the number of linearly independent columns (= independent rows) of a matrix; the dimension of the space the transform's output can reach. A `2×2` matrix of rank 2 maps the plane to the plane (full); rank 1 collapses the plane onto a line (the `project-x` case, that lost dimension is exactly a rank drop). **Rank = how many dimensions survive the transformation.**

These tie directly to ML: a feature matrix with **low rank** has redundant features (columns that are combinations of others, multicollinearity), which is *why* dimensionality reduction works and why `(XᵀX)` becomes non-invertible in regression when features are collinear.

## The determinant - how much a transform scales area/volume

The **determinant** `det(A)` answers one beautiful geometric question: **by what factor does this transformation scale area (in 2D) or volume (in 3D)?**

- `det = 2` → the transform doubles all areas. The unit square (area 1) becomes a parallelogram of area 2.
- `det = 1` → area-preserving (rotations have `det = 1`, spinning doesn't change size).
- `det = 0` → **the transform collapses space to a lower dimension** (squashes 2D onto a line, area → 0). This is the `project-x` case.
- `det < 0` → the transform *flips orientation* (a reflection, the cat becomes its mirror image), with `|det|` still the area factor.

For a 2×2: `det([[a,b],[c,d]]) = ad − bc`. That's literally the (signed) area of the parallelogram spanned by the two columns.

**The determinant is the invertibility detector.** `det(A) ≠ 0` ⟺ `A` is invertible. *Why?* If `det = 0`, the transform squashed space flat, it crushed a whole dimension to zero, and you *cannot un-squash* (you can't recover the dimension you destroyed; infinitely many inputs mapped to the same output). No determinant, no inverse. This single fact governs when `Ax = b` has a unique solution, when a covariance matrix can be inverted, and when a regression has a unique answer.

### Build it: determinant as area scaling

```python
import numpy as np

M = np.array([[2, 1], [1, 3]])
print("det(M) =", np.linalg.det(M))            # 2*3 - 1*1 = 5  -> areas scale by 5x

# Verify: the unit square (area 1) maps to a parallelogram of area |det|
square = np.array([[0,0],[1,0],[1,1],[0,1]]).T
mapped = M @ square
# Shoelace area of the transformed parallelogram
x, y = mapped[0], mapped[1]
area = 0.5*abs(x[0]*(y[1]-y[3]) + x[1]*(y[2]-y[0]) + x[2]*(y[3]-y[1]) + x[3]*(y[0]-y[2]))
print("Area of transformed square:", round(area, 4))   # 5.0  ✓ matches det

singular = np.array([[1, 2], [2, 4]])          # row 2 = 2*row 1 -> rank 1
print("\ndet(singular) =", np.linalg.det(singular))    # 0  -> collapses to a line
print("Invertible?", not np.isclose(np.linalg.det(singular), 0))  # False
```

The transformed square's area equals `|det|` exactly, the determinant *is* the area-scaling factor, not a memorized formula. And the singular matrix (whose second row is twice the first → rank 1) has `det = 0`: it crushes the plane onto a line, so it has no inverse.

### The inverse

The **inverse** `A⁻¹` undoes `A`: `A⁻¹A = I`. Geometrically it's the transformation that puts space back exactly as it was. It exists **iff `det(A) ≠ 0`** (the transform didn't destroy a dimension). Solving `Ax = b` is `x = A⁻¹b` in principle, though in practice you solve via elimination (`np.linalg.solve`), never by explicitly computing the inverse (it's slower and numerically worse).

### The special matrices worth knowing by name

A few matrix *types* recur so often they deserve names and one-line meanings, they're the vocabulary of the eigenvalue and SVD sections coming up:

- **Symmetric** (`A = Aᵀ`), mirror-image across the diagonal. *Covariance matrices, Gram matrices, and the Hessian (18.1) are all symmetric.* Their gift: **real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors, always.** This is *the* reason PCA's principal axes come out perpendicular. Use `np.linalg.eigh` for these.
- **Orthogonal** (`QᵀQ = I`, so `Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ`), a *rigid* transformation: pure rotation/reflection, no stretching. Lengths and angles are preserved (`‖Qv‖ = ‖v‖`). Its inverse is just its transpose, free to compute. The `U` and `V` in SVD are orthogonal.
- **Diagonal**, scaling along each axis independently; trivial to invert (reciprocate the diagonal) and to raise to powers. The `Σ` in SVD and the `Λ` in eigendecomposition are diagonal.
- **Identity** `I`, the do-nothing transform; the `1` of matrix multiplication.

**Transpose `Aᵀ`** (flip rows and columns) shows up everywhere, `XᵀX` is the covariance-shaped Gram matrix at the heart of regression and PCA, and it's *always* symmetric and positive semi-definite, which is why its eigenvalues (the variances) are real and non-negative. Keep that fact handy; it's why everything in the PCA derivation below behaves.

## Eigenvalues & eigenvectors - the directions a transform leaves alone

Now the centerpiece, and we're deriving it carefully because it powers PCA, PageRank, stability analysis, and vibration modes.

### The core idea and the defining equation

Apply a transformation `A` to a bunch of vectors and most of them change direction (the cat-on-paper gets rotated and sheared). But for *special* directions, the transform only **stretches or shrinks** the vector, it stays on its own line, never rotating off it. Those special directions are the **eigenvectors**, and the stretch factor is the **eigenvalue**. The defining equation:

$$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}.$$

"Applying `A` to `v` is the same as just scaling `v` by the number `λ`." `v` is an eigenvector (its direction is *invariant* under `A`), `λ` is its eigenvalue (how much it stretches: `λ=2` doubles, `λ=0.5` halves, `λ=−1` flips, `λ=1` unchanged). **Eigenvectors are the axes the transformation is "built around", the skeleton of what the matrix does.**

### Deriving them - the characteristic polynomial

Rearrange `Av = λv`:

$$A\mathbf{v} - \lambda\mathbf{v} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = 0.$$

(We insert the identity `I` so we can factor out `v`, `λv = λIv`.) This says the matrix `(A − λI)` sends the *nonzero* vector `v` to the zero vector. A matrix can only crush a nonzero vector to zero if it's **singular**, if it collapses a dimension, which means its **determinant is zero**:

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0.$$

This is the **characteristic equation**. Expanding the determinant gives a polynomial in `λ` (the characteristic polynomial); its roots are the eigenvalues. Then for each `λ`, solve `(A − λI)v = 0` for the eigenvector. That's the whole recipe, and it follows logically from "we need a nonzero `v` in the null space, so the matrix must be singular, so its determinant is zero."

### Worked by hand: a 2×2

Let `A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}`.

**Step 1, characteristic polynomial.** `A − λI = \begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix}`.

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = (2-\lambda)^2 - (1)(1) = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 4 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3.$$

**Step 2, solve for `λ`.** `λ² − 4λ + 3 = (λ−1)(λ−3) = 0`, so **`λ₁ = 3`, `λ₂ = 1`.**

**Step 3, eigenvector for `λ₁ = 3`.** Solve `(A − 3I)v = 0`:
$$\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; -v_1 + v_2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; v_1 = v_2.$$
So `v₁ = [1, 1]` (any scalar multiple). Along the `[1,1]` direction, `A` stretches by 3.

**Step 4, eigenvector for `λ₂ = 1`.** Solve `(A − I)v = 0`:
$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; v_1 + v_2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; v_1 = -v_2.$$
So `v₂ = [1, −1]`. Along the `[1,−1]` direction, `A` leaves length unchanged (`λ=1`).

**Sanity check:** `A[1,1] = [2+1, 1+2] = [3,3] = 3·[1,1]` ✓. And note the eigenvectors `[1,1]` and `[1,−1]` are *perpendicular* (dot product 0), a guaranteed bonus for symmetric matrices, which is exactly why PCA's axes come out orthogonal.

### Build it: eigenvalues by NumPy, and *power iteration* from scratch

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2, 1], [1, 2]])

# NumPy confirms our hand-derivation
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)
print("Eigenvalues:", vals)                       # [3. 1.]  ✓
print("Eigenvectors (columns):\n", vecs)          # normalized [1,1]/√2 and [1,-1]/√2

# --- Power iteration: find the DOMINANT eigenvalue from scratch ---
# Intuition: repeatedly applying A amplifies the largest-eigenvalue direction
# most, so any random vector converges toward the top eigenvector.
def power_iteration(A, n_iter=100):
    v = np.random.default_rng(0).random(A.shape[0])
    v = v / np.linalg.norm(v)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        Av = A @ v
        v = Av / np.linalg.norm(Av)               # renormalize each step
    eigenvalue = v @ A @ v                          # Rayleigh quotient: vᵀAv
    return eigenvalue, v

lam, vec = power_iteration(A)
print("\nPower iteration dominant eigenvalue:", round(lam, 4))   # ~3.0
print("Dominant eigenvector:", np.round(vec, 4))                 # ~[0.707, 0.707] = [1,1]/√2
```

**Power iteration** is profound in its simplicity: keep multiplying a random vector by `A` and renormalizing, and it converges to the eigenvector with the *largest* eigenvalue, because each multiply amplifies that direction the most, drowning out the others. **This is literally how Google's original PageRank works**: the web is a giant matrix (page `i` links to page `j`), and the dominant eigenvector of that matrix *is* the PageRank, the stationary distribution of a random surfer. You just implemented the core of a billion-dollar algorithm in six lines.

### Eigendecomposition and why eigenvalues matter

If `A` has a full set of eigenvectors, you can write `A = V Λ V⁻¹`, where `V`'s columns are eigenvectors and `Λ` is diagonal with the eigenvalues. This **eigendecomposition** says: *in the coordinate system of its eigenvectors, the transform is just independent scaling along each axis.* The eigenvectors are the "natural axes" of the matrix; in those axes the complicated transform becomes simple stretching. (This makes `Aⁿ` trivial: `Aⁿ = VΛⁿV⁻¹`, you just raise the eigenvalues to the `n`, which is how you analyze long-run behavior of Markov chains and recurrence relations.)

Where eigenvalues run the show:
- **PCA**, eigenvectors of the covariance matrix are the principal directions of the data (derived below).
- **PageRank**, dominant eigenvector of the link matrix ranks the web.
- **Stability**, in a dynamical system `x_{t+1} = Ax_t`, eigenvalues with `|λ| > 1` mean blow-up, `|λ| < 1` mean decay. This governs whether your RNN's gradients explode or vanish (14.2).
- **Vibration modes**, eigenvectors of a structure's stiffness matrix are its natural modes of vibration; eigenvalues are the resonant frequencies (why bridges have dangerous resonances).

## Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) - the most useful factorization in data science

Eigendecomposition only works for square matrices (and not all of them). **SVD works for *any* matrix**, and it's the engine behind dimensionality reduction, recommender systems, and image compression. Every matrix `A` (`m×n`) factors as:

$$A = U\Sigma V^\top.$$

- `U` (`m×m`), orthonormal columns (the **left singular vectors**): an output rotation.
- `Σ` (`m×n`), diagonal, with non-negative **singular values** `σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0` on the diagonal, sorted largest first: a scaling.
- `Vᵀ` (`n×n`), orthonormal rows (the **right singular vectors**): an input rotation.

**Geometric meaning: *every* linear transformation is a rotation, then a scaling along axes, then another rotation.** That's a stunningly clean statement, no matter how complicated the matrix looks, it's only ever "rotate, stretch, rotate." The singular values `σᵢ` tell you *how much* the transform stretches along each principal axis.

**Why it's the dimensionality-reduction workhorse, the truncation idea.** The singular values are sorted biggest-first, and each `σᵢ` measures how much "energy" / variance lives in direction `i`. Keep only the top `k` singular values (and their vectors) and you get the **best possible rank-`k` approximation** of `A` (this is the Eckart-Young theorem, truncated SVD is *provably* the optimal low-rank compression). The small singular values you throw away were carrying little information. So:

$$A \approx U_k \Sigma_k V_k^\top \quad(\text{the top } k \text{ components}).$$

This *one idea* is image compression (an image is a matrix; keep the top singular values), recommender systems (the user×item ratings matrix is low-rank, a few latent "taste" factors explain most ratings, which is the heart of the Netflix-prize approach), latent semantic analysis, and noise reduction.

### Mini-project part A - compress an image with truncated SVD

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Use a built-in grayscale-able image; fall back to a synthetic one if SciPy absent.
try:
    from scipy.datasets import face
    img = face(gray=True).astype(float)
except Exception:
    # Synthetic structured image (low-rank-ish) so the demo still runs anywhere
    xx, yy = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-3, 3, 512), np.linspace(-3, 3, 512))
    img = (np.sin(xx) * np.cos(yy) + 0.3*xx).astype(float)

print("Image shape:", img.shape)

# THE compression: full SVD, then keep only the top-k singular values
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(img, full_matrices=False)
print("Number of singular values:", len(S))

def reconstruct(U, S, Vt, k):
    """Rank-k approximation: keep the top k singular triplets."""
    return (U[:, :k] * S[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]

ranks = [5, 20, 50, 150]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, len(ranks) + 1, figsize=(16, 4))
axes[0].imshow(img, cmap='gray'); axes[0].set_title(f"original\n(rank {min(img.shape)})")
axes[0].axis('off')

m, n = img.shape
for ax, k in zip(axes[1:], ranks):
    approx = reconstruct(U, S, Vt, k)
    # Storage: instead of m*n numbers, we store k*(m + n + 1) numbers
    stored = k * (m + n + 1)
    ratio  = stored / (m * n)
    err    = np.linalg.norm(img - approx) / np.linalg.norm(img)
    ax.imshow(approx, cmap='gray')
    ax.set_title(f"k={k}\n{ratio:.1%} of storage\n{err:.1%} error")
    ax.axis('off')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

With `k=50` singular values out of ~500, the image is visually almost perfect while storing a fraction of the data. **That's lossy compression, and it works because real images are approximately low-rank**, most of the "energy" lives in the first few singular values (smooth regions, big structures), while the long tail of tiny singular values encodes fine noise you can discard. This is conceptually the same reason JPEG works.

### Mini-project part A2 - the quality/size tradeoff plot

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(img, full_matrices=False)
m, n = img.shape
ks = range(1, min(150, len(S)) + 1)

errors  = [np.linalg.norm(img - (U[:, :k]*S[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]) / np.linalg.norm(img)
           for k in ks]
ratios  = [k * (m + n + 1) / (m * n) for k in ks]

fig, ax1 = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 5))
ax1.plot(list(ks), errors, 'b-', label='reconstruction error')
ax1.set_xlabel('k (singular values kept)'); ax1.set_ylabel('relative error', color='b')
ax2 = ax1.twinx()
ax2.plot(list(ks), ratios, 'r--', label='storage ratio')
ax2.set_ylabel('storage vs. original', color='r')
ax1.set_title('SVD compression: quality vs. size tradeoff')
fig.tight_layout(); plt.show()

# The "energy captured" curve - why a few components suffice
energy = np.cumsum(S**2) / np.sum(S**2)

```

The error curve drops steeply then flattens, the classic *diminishing returns* shape. The first handful of components buy almost all the quality; beyond that you pay storage for little gain. **Picking `k` is exactly the elbow of this curve**, the same judgment call as choosing the number of PCA components.

## PCA derived as eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix

You use `PCA(n_components=2)` constantly (14.1, 16.2). Here's what it *is*, derived. **Principal Component Analysis finds the directions of maximum variance in the data**, the axes along which the data spreads out most, and projects onto them, reducing dimensions while keeping the most information.

### The derivation

Center the data (subtract the mean of each feature). The **covariance matrix** `C = (1/n) XᵀX` (with `X` centered) captures how features vary together: `C_{ij}` is the covariance of feature `i` and feature `j` (16.1). You want the unit direction `w` along which the projected data `Xw` has **maximum variance**. The variance of the projection is:

$$\text{Var}(Xw) = w^\top C\, w \quad\text{subject to } \|w\| = 1.$$

Maximize `wᵀCw` subject to `‖w‖² = 1`. Using a Lagrange multiplier (constrained optimization, 18.1), set the gradient of `wᵀCw − λ(wᵀw − 1)` to zero:

$$2Cw - 2\lambda w = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; Cw = \lambda w.$$

**That's the eigenvector equation!** The direction of maximum variance is the **eigenvector of the covariance matrix with the largest eigenvalue**, and the eigenvalue `λ` *is* the variance captured along that direction (since `wᵀCw = wᵀλw = λ`). So:

- **Principal components = eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.**
- **Variance explained by each = its eigenvalue.**
- To reduce to `k` dimensions, project onto the top `k` eigenvectors (largest eigenvalues).

The Lagrange multiplier *is* the eigenvalue, the constrained optimization *becomes* the eigenvalue problem. No black box, PCA is calculus (18.1) plus eigenvectors, fused.

### Mini-project part B - PCA from scratch via eigendecomposition

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Real dataset: the Iris flowers (4 features, 3 species)
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
data = load_iris()
X, y, names = data.data, data.target, data.target_names

def pca_from_scratch(X, n_components=2):
    # 1. Center the data (PCA needs zero-mean features)
    X_centered = X - X.mean(axis=0)
    # 2. Covariance matrix (features x features)
    C = np.cov(X_centered, rowvar=False)
    # 3. Eigendecomposition - the heart of PCA (C is symmetric -> real eigenvalues)
    eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(C)        # eigh: for symmetric matrices
    # 4. Sort eigenvectors by eigenvalue, largest first
    order = np.argsort(eigvals)[::-1]
    eigvals, eigvecs = eigvals[order], eigvecs[:, order]
    # 5. Project onto the top components
    components = eigvecs[:, :n_components]
    projected  = X_centered @ components
    explained  = eigvals / eigvals.sum()
    return projected, explained, eigvals

proj, explained, eigvals = pca_from_scratch(X, 2)
print("Variance explained by PC1, PC2:", np.round(explained[:2], 3))
print("Total captured by 2 of 4 dims :", f"{explained[:2].sum():.1%}")

# Plot the 4D data squashed to 2D - species separate cleanly
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 6))
for cls, name in enumerate(names):
    mask = y == cls
    plt.scatter(proj[mask, 0], proj[mask, 1], label=name, alpha=0.7)
plt.xlabel('PC1 (most variance)'); plt.ylabel('PC2'); plt.legend()
plt.title('Iris in 2D via PCA-from-scratch (eigendecomposition of covariance)')
plt.grid(alpha=0.3); plt.show()

# Confirm against scikit-learn (signs may differ - eigenvectors are unique up to ±)
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
sk = PCA(n_components=2).fit(X)
print("\nsklearn explained variance ratio:", np.round(sk.explained_variance_ratio_, 3))
print("Matches our from-scratch version ✓")
```

Two principal components, the top two eigenvectors of the 4×4 covariance matrix, capture ~98% of the variance in the four-dimensional Iris data, letting you plot it in 2D with the species still cleanly separated. And it matches scikit-learn exactly (up to a sign flip, since an eigenvector and its negative point along the same axis). **You just built PCA from the math up**: center, covariance, eigendecompose, sort, project. The thing you'd been calling as a one-liner is an eigenvalue problem, and you derived *why*.

> **Note on SVD and PCA being the same thing.** Running SVD directly on the centered data matrix gives the principal components without ever forming the covariance matrix, the right singular vectors `V` *are* the eigenvectors of `XᵀX`, and `σᵢ²` are proportional to the eigenvalues. That's why scikit-learn's PCA uses SVD internally: it's more numerically stable than building and eigendecomposing the covariance matrix. SVD and PCA are two faces of one idea.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting matrix multiplication isn't commutative.** `AB ≠ BA`. Transform order matters; "rotate then shear" ≠ "shear then rotate." This bites in graphics pipelines and when composing transformations.
- **Dimension mismatch.** To multiply `A (m×n)` by `B (p×q)`, you need `n = p`. The inner dimensions must agree because `B`'s output feeds `A`'s input.
- **Confusing the dot product (→ scalar) with element-wise multiply (→ vector).** In NumPy, `a @ b` / `np.dot` is the dot product; `a * b` is element-wise (Hadamard). Mixing them silently breaks ML code.
- **Not centering data before PCA.** PCA finds directions of variance *around the mean*; skip the centering and the first component just points at the mean, garbage results.
- **Computing the inverse to solve `Ax = b`.** Use `np.linalg.solve(A, b)`, not `np.linalg.inv(A) @ b`, solving is faster and far more numerically stable than inverting.
- **Expecting eigenvalues to be real for any matrix.** General matrices can have complex eigenvalues (a pure rotation has them, it leaves *no* real direction unchanged). *Symmetric* matrices (like covariance matrices) are guaranteed real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors, use `np.linalg.eigh` for those.
- **Thinking `det = 0` is a numerical edge case.** It's the fundamental signal that a transform destroyed a dimension, non-invertible, rank-deficient, collinear features. Respect it.

## On the job / interview angle

- **"Explain PCA."** *It finds the directions of maximum variance by computing the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix; the top-`k` eigenvectors give the best low-dimensional projection, and each eigenvalue is the variance captured.* Offer the Lagrange-multiplier derivation if they push, it shows you know it's not magic.
- **"What does a matrix *do*?"** "It's a linear transformation of space, its columns are where the basis vectors land; it can rotate, scale, shear, reflect, or project." This framing instantly signals depth.
- **"Why use cosine similarity for embeddings?"** It measures the angle (direction) between vectors, ignoring magnitude, so two embeddings that mean the same thing score high regardless of length. It's the normalized dot product.
- **"How does Google rank pages / what's PageRank?"** The dominant eigenvector of the web's link matrix, found by power iteration, the stationary distribution of a random web surfer.
- **"When is a matrix not invertible, and why does it matter?"** When `det = 0`, it collapsed a dimension. In ML this is collinear features making `XᵀX` singular, breaking the closed-form regression solution and forcing regularization.
- **SVD for recommenders / compression.** Be ready to say truncated SVD gives the optimal low-rank approximation (Eckart-Young) and that the ratings matrix being approximately low-rank is *why* collaborative filtering works.

## Exercises

**1. Dot product and angle.** For `a = [1, 0]` and `b = [1, 1]`, compute `a·b`, both norms, and the angle between them.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

`a·b = 1·1 + 0·1 = 1`. `‖a‖ = 1`, `‖b‖ = √2`. So `cos θ = 1/(1·√2) = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707`, giving `θ = 45°`. The vector `[1,1]` is indeed 45° above the x-axis. ✓
</details>

**2. Apply a transformation by hand.** Where does the rotation matrix `[[0,−1],[1,0]]` send the point `[2, 0]`? Describe geometrically.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

`[[0,−1],[1,0]] · [2,0] = [0·2 + (−1)·0, 1·2 + 0·0] = [0, 2]`. The point `[2,0]` (on the +x axis) rotates to `[0,2]` (on the +y axis), a **90° counterclockwise rotation**. (Its columns are where `î=[0,1]` and `ĵ=[−1,0]` land, confirming the spin.)
</details>

**3. Determinant and invertibility.** Compute `det([[3,1],[6,2]])`. Is it invertible? What does the value mean geometrically?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

`det = 3·2 − 1·6 = 6 − 6 = 0`. **Not invertible.** Geometrically, the transform collapses the plane onto a line (the second row `[6,2]` is `2×` the first `[3,1]` → rank 1 → area scaled to zero). No inverse exists because the lost dimension can't be recovered.
</details>

**4. Eigenvalues by hand.** Find the eigenvalues of `A = [[4, 0], [0, 3]]` (a diagonal matrix). Then of `[[2, 1],[0, 2]]`.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Diagonal matrix: eigenvalues are just the diagonal entries, **`λ = 4` and `λ = 3`** (eigenvectors `[1,0]` and `[0,1]`, it scales the axes directly).

For `[[2,1],[0,2]]`: `det(A−λI) = (2−λ)² − 0 = (2−λ)² = 0`, so `λ = 2` (repeated). This matrix is *defective*, it has a repeated eigenvalue but only one independent eigenvector (`[1,0]`), so it can't be diagonalized. A subtle but important edge case.
</details>

**5. Verify an eigenvector.** Is `[1, 1]` an eigenvector of `A = [[3, 1], [1, 3]]`? If so, what's its eigenvalue?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

`A[1,1] = [3·1 + 1·1, 1·1 + 3·1] = [4, 4] = 4·[1,1]`. Yes, `[1,1]` is an eigenvector with **eigenvalue `λ = 4`** (the transform stretches this direction by 4 without rotating it).
</details>

**6. PCA conceptual.** You run PCA and the eigenvalues are `[8.0, 1.5, 0.4, 0.1]`. How much variance do the first two components capture? Would you reduce to 2D?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Total variance = `8.0+1.5+0.4+0.1 = 10.0`. First two capture `(8.0+1.5)/10.0 = 9.5/10 = 95%`. Yes, reducing to 2D keeps 95% of the variance while halving (or more) the dimensions, a great tradeoff. The sharp drop after the second eigenvalue (the "elbow") is exactly the signal to keep two components.
</details>

**7. (Challenge) Power iteration intuition.** Explain why repeatedly applying `A` and renormalizing converges to the dominant eigenvector. (Hint: write the starting vector in the eigenvector basis.)

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Write the random start `v₀ = c₁e₁ + c₂e₂ + … ` in the eigenvector basis (eigenvalues `|λ₁| > |λ₂| > …`). Applying `A` `k` times scales each component by its eigenvalue: `Aᵏv₀ = c₁λ₁ᵏe₁ + c₂λ₂ᵏe₂ + …`. Factor out the dominant term: `= λ₁ᵏ(c₁e₁ + c₂(λ₂/λ₁)ᵏe₂ + …)`. Since `|λ₂/λ₁| < 1`, every ratio `(λᵢ/λ₁)ᵏ → 0` as `k` grows, so only the `e₁` term survives. Renormalizing each step keeps it from over/underflowing. The direction converges to `e₁`, the dominant eigenvector. **That decay of the sub-dominant ratios is exactly why PageRank converges.**
</details>
