## Start here - the on-ramp (read this even if calculus scared you in school)

Forget the textbook intimidation. Calculus is about exactly two questions, and they're mirror images of each other:

1. **How fast is something changing right now?** (the derivative), If you plot a model's loss against one weight, the derivative is the *steepness of that hill at a single point*. Steep downhill slope → you can make the loss drop a lot by nudging the weight. Flat → you're near the bottom, stop nudging. That's all gradient descent is: read the steepness, step downhill.

2. **If you know the rate, how much piles up over time?** (the integral), If you know your download speed at every instant, the integral tells you how many total megabytes arrived. Rate → accumulated total. Speed → distance. Probability density → actual probability.

Here's a picture to keep in mind. Imagine standing on a foggy hillside at night. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the slope under your feet. The **derivative is that slope**. To get to the bottom (minimum loss), you take a step in the steepest *downhill* direction, feel the slope again, step again. In one dimension the slope is a single number. In a million dimensions (a real neural net) it's a vector, the **gradient**, and it still points in the steepest *uphill* direction, so you step the opposite way. That single idea trains every model in this book's chapter 14.

The other half: if you record your *speed* at every moment of a road trip, you can recover the *total distance* even though you never looked at the odometer. Adding up rate-over-tiny-intervals to recover a total is **integration**. The astonishing fact, the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**, is that these two operations *undo each other*. Differentiation and integration are inverses, like `+` and `−`. That's the punchline of a whole semester, and we'll show you why it's true.

Two reassurances before the math:
- You will never compute a hard integral by hand on the job. You'll let SymPy or a numerical routine do it. What you need is the *intuition* for what it means, so you reach for the right tool and trust the answer.
- Every formula below gets *derived* and then *run in Python*, so nothing is "just memorize this." If a rule feels arbitrary, that's a bug in the explanation, not in you.

Keep the foggy hillside in mind. We'll come back to it constantly.

## Functions and limits - the foundation

Everything in calculus is built on **functions** and the idea of a **limit**. Let's make both precise without losing the intuition.

### Functions as machines

A **function** `f` takes an input `x` and produces exactly one output `f(x)`. Think of it as a machine: feed in `x`, get out `f(x)`. In ML the function might be "model weights → loss," and the goal is finding the input that minimizes the output.

- **Domain**, the set of legal inputs (you can't take `log(x)` for `x ≤ 0`).
- **Range**, the set of outputs the machine can produce.
- **Continuity** (intuitive version), you can draw the graph without lifting your pen. No sudden jumps, no holes, no teleporting. We'll sharpen this formally with limits in a moment, but the pen test is the right mental model.

### Limits - "what value is the function heading toward?"

A **limit** asks: as `x` gets arbitrarily close to some value `a`, what does `f(x)` get close to? We write:

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

and read it "the limit of `f(x)` as `x` approaches `a` is `L`." The crucial subtlety: **we don't care what happens *at* `a`, only what `f(x)` approaches as `x` sneaks up on `a`.** This is exactly the loophole that lets us define the derivative, where the function `0/0` shows up at the point itself but has a perfectly sensible limit *approaching* it.

The classic example that motivates everything:

$$f(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x}.$$

At `x = 0` this is literally `0/0`, undefined. But as `x → 0`, `f(x) → 1`. The function has a hole at `0` but is "heading toward" 1 from both sides. The limit sees the destination, not the pothole.

**The precise (ε-δ) definition**, for the rigor promised earlier. `lim_{x→a} f(x) = L` means: for *every* tolerance `ε > 0` you demand on the output, there *exists* a closeness `δ > 0` on the input such that whenever `0 < |x − a| < δ`, we have `|f(x) − L| < ε`. In English: "you can force `f(x)` as close to `L` as you like, just by making `x` close enough to `a`." You won't use ε-δ on the job, but it's the bedrock that makes "approaches" mean something exact rather than hand-wavy.

**Continuity, now precise:** `f` is continuous at `a` when `lim_{x→a} f(x) = f(a)`, the limit exists, the function is defined there, and they agree. The pen never lifts. ReLU (`max(0, x)`), the activation in chapter 14.2, is continuous everywhere but has a *kink* at 0, continuous yet not smooth, which matters when we differentiate it.

### Build it: watch a limit converge

```python
import numpy as np

def f(x):
    return np.sin(x) / x

# Sneak up on x = 0 from the right, halving the distance each time
for x in [1, 0.5, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 1e-6]:
    print(f"x={x:<8}  f(x)={f(x):.8f}")
# f(x) marches toward 1.0 even though f(0) is literally 0/0 (undefined)
```

```
x=1         f(x)=0.84147098
x=0.5       f(x)=0.95885108
x=0.1       f(x)=0.99833417
x=0.01      f(x)=0.99998333
x=0.001     f(x)=0.99999983
x=1e-06     f(x)=1.00000000
```

You just *watched* the limit. The function never has to be defined at 0 for the limit to exist, and that hole is precisely the door we walk through to define the derivative.

## The derivative from first principles

Here's the most important definition in this chapter, and we're going to build it from a picture rather than hand it over.

### The slope of a curve at a single point

The slope of a *straight line* is easy: rise over run, `Δy/Δx`. But a curve has a different slope at every point. How do you get the slope at *one* point `x`?

Trick: pick a second point a tiny distance `h` away, at `x + h`. Draw the line through `(x, f(x))` and `(x+h, f(x+h))`, a **secant line**. Its slope is:

$$\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

That's the *average* rate of change over the little interval `[x, x+h]`. Now shrink `h` toward 0. The second point slides toward the first, and the secant line pivots until it becomes the **tangent line**, the line that just kisses the curve at `x`. Its slope is the **instantaneous rate of change**: the derivative.

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$

Notice the `0/0` lurking: as `h → 0`, the top `f(x+h) − f(x) → 0` and the bottom `h → 0`. This is *exactly* why we needed limits, the ratio approaches a finite, meaningful value even though plugging in `h = 0` gives nonsense.

### Worked by hand: the derivative of x²

Let's grind through `f(x) = x²` with zero hand-waving, because doing it once makes the limit definition real.

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h}$$

Expand the numerator: `(x+h)² = x² + 2xh + h²`, so

$$= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h}.$$

The `x²` terms cancel. Now factor `h` from the top and cancel it (legal because `h ≠ 0` while we're approaching, which is exactly what a limit lets you do):

$$= \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h).$$

*Now* let `h → 0` safely, no division by zero anymore, and get:

$$f'(x) = 2x.$$

So the slope of `y = x²` at `x = 3` is `6`; at `x = 0` it's `0` (the bottom of the parabola, flat, a minimum, which is exactly what optimization looks for). You derived that from the definition, not a rule. Every differentiation rule below is this same limit, done once in general so you never have to repeat it.

### Notation

Three notations for the same object, and you should be fluent in all three because different fields use different ones:
- `f'(x)`, Lagrange/prime notation. Compact.
- `\frac{dy}{dx}` or `\frac{df}{dx}`, Leibniz notation. Reads as "the ratio of an infinitesimal change in `y` to an infinitesimal change in `x`." This notation *suggests* the chain rule "fractions cancel," which is a useful (if technically informal) mnemonic.
- `\frac{d}{dx}f`, the derivative as an *operator* applied to `f`.

## The differentiation rules (each one is just that limit, generalized)

Computing the limit every time is exhausting. Mathematicians did it once for common building blocks; here are the rules with the intuition for why each holds. These are the LEGO bricks, combine them to differentiate almost anything.

### Power rule

$$\frac{d}{dx} x^n = n\,x^{n-1}.$$

We derived the `n=2` case above (`2x¹`). The general case comes from the binomial expansion of `(x+h)ⁿ`: the leading term `xⁿ` cancels, the next term is `n·xⁿ⁻¹·h`, and everything else carries an `h²` or higher that dies in the limit. So `xⁿ → n·xⁿ⁻¹`. Examples: `x³ → 3x²`, `x → 1`, `√x = x^{1/2} → ½x^{−1/2}`, constant `c = c·x⁰ → 0` (a flat line has zero slope).

### Linearity (sum and constant-multiple rules)

$$\frac{d}{dx}\big[a\,f(x) + b\,g(x)\big] = a\,f'(x) + b\,g'(x).$$

Derivatives respect addition and scaling. This is why you can differentiate a polynomial term by term. It falls straight out of the limit because limits distribute over sums.

### Product rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)\,g(x)\big] = f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x).$$

Intuition: a rectangle with width `f` and height `g` has area `fg`. Grow both sides a hair. The new area added is (change in width)·(height) + (width)·(change in height) + a tiny corner that's second-order and vanishes. So the *rate* of area change is `f'g + fg'`. "Derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times derivative of the second."

### Quotient rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{f'(x)\,g(x) - f(x)\,g'(x)}{g(x)^2}.$$

Less intuitive; it's often easier to rewrite `f/g` as `f·g^{−1}` and use product + chain instead. But memorize the form, "low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared" is the classic chant.

### The chain rule - the most important rule in all of ML

$$\frac{d}{dx} f\big(g(x)\big) = f'\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x).$$

This is the one that *is* backpropagation, so let's dwell on it.

**Intuition, gears.** Suppose `y` depends on `u`, and `u` depends on `x`. If turning `x` makes `u` turn 3× as fast, and turning `u` makes `y` turn 2× as fast, then turning `x` makes `y` turn `3 × 2 = 6` times as fast. **Rates multiply along the chain.** That's it. In Leibniz notation it looks like fractions cancelling:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}.$$

**Worked example.** Differentiate `h(x) = sin(x²)`. The outer function is `sin(·)`, the inner is `u = x²`. Outer derivative is `cos(u) = cos(x²)`; inner derivative is `2x`. Multiply:

$$h'(x) = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x = 2x\cos(x^2).$$

**Why this is backprop.** A neural network is a *deeply nested* composition: `loss(activation(weights · activation(weights · … · input)))`. To know how the loss changes when you nudge a weight buried 10 layers deep, you apply the chain rule 10 times, multiplying the local derivative at each layer. Backpropagation is *exactly* the chain rule, organized cleverly so each local derivative is computed once and reused (that reuse is dynamic programming on the computation graph, see 03.5 for DP and 14.2 for the full backprop derivation). When chapter 14.2 multiplies "delta" terms backward through layers, every one of those multiplications is a chain-rule factor `g'(·)`. This is why ReLU beat sigmoid: sigmoid's derivative is `≤ 0.25`, so multiplying many of them through a deep chain drives the product to zero (the *vanishing gradient*), while ReLU's derivative is exactly 1 on the active path and the product survives. The chain rule literally explains a multi-billion-dollar architectural choice.

### Build it: numerical differentiation from scratch

Before SymPy, let's approximate the derivative *directly from its definition*, pick a small `h` and evaluate the difference quotient. This is "finite differences," and it's how you sanity-check an analytic gradient (something worth doing whenever you hand-derive backprop).

```python
import numpy as np

def numerical_derivative(f, x, h=1e-5):
    """Forward difference: the raw limit definition with a small h."""
    return (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h

def central_difference(f, x, h=1e-5):
    """Central difference: symmetric, far more accurate (error O(h^2) vs O(h))."""
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)

f = lambda x: x**2            # we proved f'(x) = 2x by hand above
for x in [0.0, 1.0, 3.0, -2.0]:
    fwd = numerical_derivative(f, x)
    ctr = central_difference(f, x)
    print(f"x={x:>4}:  forward={fwd:.6f}  central={ctr:.6f}  exact(2x)={2*x:.6f}")
```

```
x= 0.0:  forward=0.000010  central=0.000000  exact(2x)=0.000000
x= 1.0:  forward=2.000010  central=2.000000  exact(2x)=2.000000
x= 3.0:  forward=6.000010  central=6.000000  exact(2x)=6.000000
x=-2.0:  forward=-3.999990  central=-4.000000  exact(2x)=-4.000000
```

The central difference nails it. Notice the forward difference is off by `~h`: that's a real lesson, `(f(x+h)−f(x))/h` has error proportional to `h`, while the symmetric version has error proportional to `h²`. **The symmetric version is the gradient-checker every ML engineer should keep in their back pocket.**

> **The finite-difference trap:** you'd think tinier `h` is always better. It isn't. Too small and *floating-point round-off* dominates, you're subtracting two nearly-equal numbers and amplifying the noise. There's a sweet spot around `h ≈ 1e-5` to `1e-7` for `float64`. This catastrophic-cancellation issue is exactly why we use *analytic* gradients (autograd) in real training and reserve finite differences for *checking* them.

### SymPy does it symbolically

Now let SymPy compute the *exact* derivative algebraically, no `h`, no approximation. This is your derivation-checker.

```python
import sympy as sp

x = sp.Symbol('x')

# Confirm our hand-derivation of x^2
print(sp.diff(x**2, x))                    # 2*x   ✓ (matches our limit derivation)

# Chain rule example we did by hand: sin(x^2)
print(sp.diff(sp.sin(x**2), x))            # 2*x*cos(x**2)   ✓

# Something gnarly - product + chain + quotient all at once
expr = sp.exp(x) * sp.sin(x) / (x**2 + 1)
print(sp.simplify(sp.diff(expr, x)))

# Verify the limit definition itself, symbolically
h = sp.Symbol('h')
f = x**3
deriv = sp.limit((f.subs(x, x + h) - f) / h, h, 0)
print(deriv)                               # 3*x**2   ✓ the power rule, derived live
```

That last block is worth pausing on: SymPy evaluates `lim_{h→0} ((x+h)³ − x³)/h` and returns `3x²`. It ran the *exact* first-principles derivation you'd do by hand, symbolically, and you can always ask SymPy to show you the limit it's taking.

## What a derivative MEANS - three readings

The derivative `f'(x)` is one object with three interchangeable interpretations, and fluency means switching between them effortlessly:

1. **Slope**, geometrically, the steepness of the tangent line. Positive → going uphill, negative → downhill, zero → flat (a peak, valley, or plateau). This is the *foggy hillside*.

2. **Instantaneous rate of change**, physically, if `f(t)` is position, `f'(t)` is velocity (rate of position change); `f''(t)` is acceleration (rate of velocity change). If `f(t)` is total downloaded bytes, `f'(t)` is current download speed.

3. **Sensitivity**, this is the ML reading. `f'(x)` answers "if you nudge the input by a tiny `ε`, how much does the output change?" Specifically `Δf ≈ f'(x)·Δx`. When you compute `∂loss/∂weight`, you're asking *how sensitive the loss is to that weight*, which weights matter, and in which direction to push them. A weight with a large-magnitude gradient is one the loss cares about a lot; a near-zero gradient means that weight barely matters right now. **Gradient descent is just "follow the sensitivity."**

These aren't three different facts; they're three lenses on the same number. The slope view builds intuition, the rate view connects to physics and time-series, and the sensitivity view is what you're *actually doing* when you train a model.

## Higher derivatives and Taylor series

### Second derivative - curvature

The derivative of the derivative, `f''(x)`, measures how the slope itself is changing, the **curvature**:
- `f''(x) > 0` → slope increasing → curve bends *upward* → **convex** (cup that holds water). A critical point here is a **minimum**.
- `f''(x) < 0` → slope decreasing → curve bends *downward* → **concave**. A critical point here is a **maximum**.
- `f''(x) = 0` → possible inflection point (curvature changing sign).

This is the basis of the **second-derivative test** for classifying critical points, and it's why convexity is so prized in optimization (more below).

### Taylor series - approximating any function with polynomials

This idea is worth sitting with: near a point `a`, almost any smooth function can be approximated by a polynomial built from its derivatives at `a`:

$$f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 + \frac{f'''(a)}{3!}(x-a)^3 + \cdots$$

**Intuition, term by term:** start at the function's *value* `f(a)`. Add a correction using the *slope* `f'(a)`, that's the tangent line, the best straight-line approximation. Add another correction using the *curvature* `f''(a)`, now you're matching the bend, the best parabola. Each higher derivative refines the fit. With enough terms you reproduce the function exactly (within its radius of convergence).

**Why you should care in CS:**
- **First-order Taylor** `f(x) ≈ f(a) + f'(a)(x−a)` is *literally the update rule* of gradient descent: it says "locally, the function looks like its tangent line, so step along that line to reduce it."
- **Second-order Taylor** (adding the `f''` term) gives **Newton's method** and explains why curvature information lets optimizers like L-BFGS take smarter steps than plain gradient descent.
- It's how computers compute `sin`, `exp`, `log` under the hood, polynomial approximations.

### Build it: watch Taylor series converge to a function

```python
import numpy as np
import sympy as sp

x = sp.Symbol('x')
f = sp.exp(x)

# Build Taylor polynomials of e^x around a=0 (the "Maclaurin series")
for order in [1, 2, 4, 8]:
    taylor = sp.series(f, x, 0, order).removeO()   # truncate to 'order' terms
    approx = sp.lambdify(x, taylor, 'numpy')
    err_at_1 = abs(approx(1.0) - np.e)
    print(f"order {order}: {taylor}")
    print(f"           approx(1) = {approx(1.0):.6f}  vs  e = {np.e:.6f}  "
          f"(error {err_at_1:.2e})")
```

Each added term shrinks the error dramatically, by order 8 the polynomial approximation of `eˣ` at `x=1` is accurate to ~4 decimal places. This is *exactly* how your CPU computes `exp` (using a few carefully chosen terms), and it's why "everything looks linear up close" (the first-order term dominates near `a`), the foundational assumption of gradient descent.

## Optimization with calculus - finding minima

Now you cash in everything for the central task of ML: **finding the input that minimizes a function** (the weights that minimize the loss).

### Critical points

At a minimum or maximum, the tangent line is *horizontal*, the slope is zero. So:

$$f'(x) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{candidate min/max (a "critical point")}.$$

This is fundamental: **to optimize, set the derivative to zero and solve.** That's where the classic least-squares formula comes from (as referenced in 14.1, setting the derivative of squared error to zero gives the normal equations). It's where the maximum-likelihood estimates in 16.1 come from. "Take the derivative, set it to zero" is the single most-used move in all of applied math.

### Classifying critical points - the second-derivative test

A zero slope could be a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle/inflection. The second derivative disambiguates:
- `f'(x*) = 0` and `f''(x*) > 0` → **local minimum** (convex cup, the slope is rising through zero).
- `f'(x*) = 0` and `f''(x*) < 0` → **local maximum** (concave cap).
- `f'(x*) = 0` and `f''(x*) = 0` → inconclusive (could be a saddle or inflection).

### Convexity - the property that makes optimization *easy*

A function is **convex** if `f''(x) ≥ 0` everywhere (curving up, holding water). Convexity is the holy grail of optimization because **a convex function has exactly one minimum, and any local minimum is the global minimum.** No bad valleys to get stuck in. Gradient descent on a convex function is *guaranteed* to find the best answer.

This is exactly why linear/logistic regression (convex losses) "just work" and train to a unique optimum, while deep neural nets (wildly non-convex losses, full of local minima and saddle points) require all the tricks in 14.2, momentum, careful initialization, learning-rate schedules, to navigate a bumpy landscape. **Convex vs. non-convex is *the* dividing line between "optimization is solved" and "optimization is an art."**

### From "set derivative to zero" to gradient descent

Often you *can't* solve `f'(x) = 0` algebraically (a neural net has millions of weights and a non-linear loss). So instead of solving exactly, you *iterate*: start somewhere, compute the slope, step downhill, repeat. That's **gradient descent**, and the update rule is:

$$x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\, f'(x_t)$$

where `η` (eta) is the **learning rate**, how big a step to take. The minus sign is the key: `f'(x)` points *uphill* (toward increasing `f`), and you want to *decrease* `f`, so you step in the *opposite* direction. If the slope is steeply positive (function rising to the right), you move left; if steeply negative, you move right. Either way you head toward the valley. This is the foggy hillside made into an algorithm.

### Build it: gradient descent in 1D, visualized

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Minimize f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 6.  By hand: f'(x) = 2x - 4 = 0  =>  x* = 2, f(2)=2.
f      = lambda x: x**2 - 4*x + 6
f_grad = lambda x: 2*x - 4

x = 8.0            # start far from the minimum
eta = 0.1          # learning rate
history = [x]
for step in range(40):
    x = x - eta * f_grad(x)        # the update rule, by hand
    history.append(x)

print(f"Converged to x = {x:.5f}  (true minimum at x = 2)")
print(f"f(x) = {f(x):.5f}  (true min value 2)")

# Visualize the descent path rolling down the parabola
xs = np.linspace(-1, 9, 400)
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
plt.plot(xs, f(xs), 'b-', label='f(x) = x² - 4x + 6')
hx = np.array(history)
plt.plot(hx, f(hx), 'ro-', markersize=4, alpha=0.6, label='descent path')
plt.scatter([2], [2], color='green', s=120, zorder=5, label='true minimum')
plt.xlabel('x'); plt.ylabel('f(x)'); plt.legend()
plt.title('Gradient descent rolling downhill to the minimum')
plt.grid(alpha=0.3); plt.show()
```

Watch the red dots: big steps where the slope is steep (far from the bottom), tiny steps as the slope flattens near the minimum. The algorithm *automatically* slows down as it converges, because `f'(x)` shrinks toward zero. That self-braking is a gift of using the gradient.

> **Learning rate is everything.** Set `eta` too small and convergence crawls (try `0.001` and watch it barely move). Too large and you *overshoot* and oscillate, or diverge entirely (try `eta = 1.1` and watch `x` explode to infinity). The "right" learning rate is the single most important hyperparameter in training, and now you know *why*: it's the step size on the foggy hillside. Too timid and you never arrive; too bold and you leap across the valley and up the other wall.

## Multivariable calculus - the real game

Real models have *many* inputs. A neural net's loss is a function of millions of weights: `f(w₁, w₂, …, w_n)`. The foggy hillside is no longer a 2D curve; it's a high-dimensional landscape. The math still generalizes cleanly.

### Partial derivatives

A **partial derivative** asks: how does `f` change if you wiggle *one* input while holding all the others fixed? Notation `∂f/∂x` (curly `∂` instead of `d` to signal "partial"). Mechanically, **treat every other variable as a constant and differentiate normally.**

Example: `f(x, y) = x²y + 3y`. Then:
- `∂f/∂x = 2xy` (treat `y` as a constant; the `3y` term has no `x`, so it dies).
- `∂f/∂y = x² + 3` (treat `x` as a constant; `x²y` differentiates to `x²`, and `3y` to `3`).

Each partial is the slope of the landscape *along one axis*, how steep is the hill if you only walk north-south, vs. only east-west.

### The gradient - direction of steepest ascent (derived)

Stack all the partial derivatives into a vector and you get the **gradient**:

$$\nabla f = \left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}, \ldots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right].$$

The single most important fact in machine-learning optimization: **the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent**, and its magnitude is the steepness in that direction. So `−∇f` points in the direction of steepest *descent*, the fastest way downhill.

**Let's derive *why* the gradient is steepest ascent**, because this is too important to assert. Take a tiny step `v` (a unit vector, a direction) from point `x`. The change in `f` is, to first order (first-order Taylor in multiple dimensions):

$$\Delta f \approx \nabla f \cdot v = \|\nabla f\|\,\|v\|\cos\theta = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta$$

where `θ` is the angle between your step direction `v` and the gradient, and `‖v‖ = 1`. The change `Δf` is biggest when `cos θ = 1`, i.e. `θ = 0`, i.e. **`v` points exactly along `∇f`.** So among all directions you could step, the gradient is the one that increases `f` the most. The dot product *is* the proof. Step the opposite way (`θ = 180°`, `cos θ = −1`) and you decrease `f` the most, that's gradient *descent*. (The dot-product-as-projection geometry that makes this click is in 18.2.)

This is the entire foundation of training. Multivariable gradient descent is identical to the 1D version, just with vectors:

$$\mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_t).$$

Every `.step()` call in PyTorch is this line, executed over a million-dimensional weight vector.

### Build it: visualize the gradient field and steepest descent

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# A 2D bowl: f(x,y) = x^2 + 3y^2  (minimum at the origin)
def f(x, y):       return x**2 + 3*y**2
def grad_f(x, y):  return np.array([2*x, 6*y])     # [∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y], by hand

# Contour plot of the landscape (like a topographic map)
xs = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200); ys = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, ys)
Z = f(X, Y)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 7))
cs = plt.contour(X, Y, Z, levels=20, cmap='viridis', alpha=0.6)

# Draw the NEGATIVE gradient field - the arrows everyone descends along
gx, gy = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-4, 4, 18), np.linspace(-4, 4, 18))
g = grad_f(gx, gy)
plt.quiver(gx, gy, -g[0], -g[1], color='gray', alpha=0.5)  # arrows point downhill

# Run gradient descent and overlay the path
w = np.array([3.5, 3.0]); eta = 0.1; path = [w.copy()]
for _ in range(50):
    w = w - eta * grad_f(*w)
    path.append(w.copy())
path = np.array(path)
plt.plot(path[:,0], path[:,1], 'r.-', markersize=6, label='descent path')
plt.scatter([0],[0], color='red', s=150, marker='*', zorder=5, label='minimum')
plt.xlabel('x'); plt.ylabel('y'); plt.legend()
plt.title('Steepest descent always perpendicular to the contour lines')
plt.axis('equal'); plt.show()
```

Two things to notice and *understand*:
1. **The path always crosses contour lines at right angles.** That's not a coincidence, the gradient is always perpendicular to the level curves (contours of constant `f`), because moving *along* a contour doesn't change `f` at all (zero directional derivative), so the steepest direction must be perpendicular to it. Deep insight, free from the picture.
2. **The path bends.** Because `f` is steeper in the `y` direction (coefficient 3) than the `x` direction (coefficient 1), the descent zig-zags. This *anisotropy*, different steepness in different directions, is exactly why optimizers use per-parameter learning rates (Adam, RMSProp in 14.2) and why feature scaling matters: a stretched bowl makes plain gradient descent crawl.

### The Jacobian and the Hessian (briefly)

Two generalizations worth naming, because they show up in interviews and docs:

- **Jacobian**, when `f` outputs a *vector* (many outputs) and takes a vector input, the Jacobian is the matrix of *all* partial derivatives: `J_{ij} = ∂f_i/∂x_j`. Each row is the gradient of one output. Autograd is fundamentally a Jacobian-vector-product machine. (Matrices and their meaning: 18.2.)

- **Hessian**, the matrix of *second* partial derivatives, `H_{ij} = ∂²f/∂x_i∂x_j`. It's the multivariable curvature, the second-derivative test generalized. The Hessian being *positive definite* (all eigenvalues positive, see 18.2) means a local minimum; mixed signs mean a *saddle point*. Saddle points (flat in the gradient but curving up in some directions and down in others) are the bane of high-dimensional optimization, far more common than local minima in deep nets, which is why momentum exists: to coast through the flat saddle regions.

### The multivariable chain rule → computational graphs → backprop

In multiple variables, when `z` depends on several intermediates that each depend on `t`, the chain rule *sums* over all paths:

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \sum_i \frac{\partial z}{\partial u_i}\cdot\frac{du_i}{dt}.$$

**This summation-over-paths is backpropagation through a computational graph.** A neural net is a graph: inputs flow forward through weighted sums and nonlinearities to a loss. To get `∂loss/∂w` for a weight `w`, you trace *every path* from `w` to the loss, multiply the local derivatives along each path (chain rule), and sum over paths (multivariable chain rule). Autograd builds the graph during the forward pass, then walks it backward applying exactly this rule. **Backprop = the multivariable chain rule + the reuse of shared subexpressions (dynamic programming).** That's the mechanism, and `.backward()` is no longer a mystery. (Full backprop derivation and from-scratch NumPy implementation: 14.2.)

### SymPy: gradients and Hessians symbolically

```python
import sympy as sp

x, y = sp.symbols('x y')
f = x**2 * y + 3*y + sp.sin(x*y)

grad = [sp.diff(f, v) for v in (x, y)]
print("Gradient:", grad)

hessian = sp.hessian(f, (x, y))
print("Hessian:")
sp.pprint(hessian)

# Find critical points of a simple convex function by solving grad = 0
g = x**2 + 3*y**2 - 2*x - 12*y
crit = sp.solve([sp.diff(g, x), sp.diff(g, y)], [x, y])
print("Critical point:", crit)        # {x: 1, y: 2} - set gradient to zero, solve
```

SymPy computes the gradient *and* the Hessian symbolically, and solves `∇f = 0` to find the optimum exactly, the "set the gradient to zero" recipe, automated. For convex problems this gives the closed-form answer; for non-convex ones we fall back to iterative gradient descent.

## Integration - accumulation and area

Now the other half of calculus, the inverse operation. If the derivative is "rate of change," the integral is "total accumulated."

### Antiderivatives - running differentiation backward

An **antiderivative** of `f` is a function `F` whose derivative is `f`: `F'(x) = f(x)`. Since the derivative of a constant is zero, antiderivatives come in families differing by a constant `C`. We write the **indefinite integral**:

$$\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C.$$

Reverse the power rule: `∫xⁿ dx = x^{n+1}/(n+1) + C`. (Check: differentiate the right side, get `xⁿ` back. ✓) So `∫2x dx = x² + C`, undoing the derivative of `x²` we computed by hand at the start.

### Definite integrals - area and accumulation

The **definite integral** `∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx` is a single number: the **signed area** between the curve `f` and the x-axis from `a` to `b` (area above the axis counts positive, below counts negative).

**Why area = accumulation.** Slice `[a, b]` into thin rectangles of width `Δx` and height `f(xᵢ)`. Each rectangle's area `f(xᵢ)·Δx` is "rate × interval = amount accumulated in that slice." Add them up and shrink the width to zero (a limit again!) and you get the exact total, that's the integral, and the elongated `∫` is literally a stretched "S" for "sum":

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\sum_i f(x_i)\,\Delta x.$$

This is why integration is *everywhere* in CS and stats: total bytes downloaded = integral of bandwidth over time; the probability that a continuous random variable lands in `[a,b]` = integral of its density (16.1, a density integrates to a probability); expected value = integral of `x·f(x)`; the area under the ROC curve (AUC) you report for classifiers is literally an integral.

### The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus - why the two halves connect

Here is the theorem that ties the entire subject together, with the intuition promised earlier:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a), \quad\text{where } F' = f.$$

**To find the accumulated area under a rate `f`, find an antiderivative `F` and subtract its endpoints.** Differentiation (slopes) and integration (areas) are *inverse operations*.

**Intuition for why it's true.** Let `F(x)` be the accumulated area from `a` up to `x`. How fast does that accumulated area grow as you push the right edge `x` rightward by a hair `dx`? You add a sliver of area, height `f(x)` and width `dx`, so `dF ≈ f(x)·dx`, meaning `dF/dx = f(x)`. So the *area-so-far function* has the original `f` as its derivative, the rate at which area accumulates *is* the height of the curve. That's the whole theorem: accumulating and rate-of-change are two sides of one coin. The same insight as "your odometer's rate of change is your speedometer's reading."

### Build it: numerical integration from scratch (trapezoid & Simpson)

Most integrals can't be solved in closed form (the Gaussian `e^{−x²}`, central to all of stats, has *no* elementary antiderivative). So you approximate the area numerically, slice into shapes and add. From scratch:

```python
import numpy as np

def trapezoid_rule(f, a, b, n=1000):
    """Approximate area with n trapezoids (better than rectangles:
       a trapezoid hugs the curve along its top edge)."""
    x = np.linspace(a, b, n + 1)
    y = f(x)
    h = (b - a) / n
    # Each trapezoid area = h * (left_height + right_height)/2.
    # Sum telescopes to: endpoints counted once, interior points twice.
    return h * (0.5*y[0] + 0.5*y[-1] + y[1:-1].sum())

def simpson_rule(f, a, b, n=1000):
    """Simpson's rule: fit PARABOLAS through triples of points instead of
       straight lines. Far more accurate (exact for cubics!). n must be even."""
    if n % 2: n += 1
    x = np.linspace(a, b, n + 1)
    y = f(x)
    h = (b - a) / n
    # Weights pattern: 1, 4, 2, 4, 2, ..., 4, 1   (the parabola-fit coefficients)
    return h/3 * (y[0] + y[-1] + 4*y[1:-1:2].sum() + 2*y[2:-1:2].sum())

# Test on ∫₀^π sin(x) dx, whose EXACT answer is [-cos x]₀^π = -(-1) - (-1) = 2
f = np.sin
print(f"Trapezoid: {trapezoid_rule(f, 0, np.pi):.10f}")
print(f"Simpson:   {simpson_rule(f, 0, np.pi):.10f}")
print(f"Exact:     {2.0:.10f}")

# A non-elementary integral: the (unnormalized) Gaussian bell
gauss = lambda x: np.exp(-x**2)
# ∫_{-∞}^{∞} e^{-x²} dx = √π exactly; integrate over [-5,5] to approximate
print(f"\nGaussian ∫ ≈ {simpson_rule(gauss, -5, 5):.8f}   (√π = {np.sqrt(np.pi):.8f})")
```

```
Trapezoid: 1.9999983550
Simpson:   2.0000000000
Exact:     2.0000000000
```

Simpson's rule fits *parabolas* to triples of points and nails `sin` to full precision, while the trapezoid rule (straight-line tops) is slightly off, same lesson as central differences: a higher-order local fit means dramatically better accuracy. And you just computed `√π` by integrating the bell curve, an integral with *no closed-form antiderivative*. This is exactly how `scipy.integrate.quad` works under the hood (adaptive Simpson-like rules), and why you can trust the area-under-a-density numbers in 16.1.

### SymPy integrates symbolically

```python
import sympy as sp
```

## A touch of differential equations - modeling change itself

Sometimes you don't know a quantity directly, but you know *how it changes*, and that relationship is the model. An equation relating a function to its derivatives is a **differential equation (ODE)**, and they describe an enormous fraction of the dynamic systems in the world.

The canonical example, **exponential growth/decay**:

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = k\,N.$$

Read it: "the rate of change of `N` is proportional to `N` itself." More of it → faster growth. The solution is `N(t) = N₀ e^{kt}`. This *one* equation models compound interest, population growth, viral spread (the early phase of an epidemic, see the SIR model), radioactive decay (`k < 0`), and the decay of a learning-rate schedule.

Why a CS person should care:
- **Neural ODEs** treat a network's depth as continuous time and *solve* an ODE for the forward pass, a real, modern architecture.
- **Gradient descent itself** is the discrete approximation of an ODE: `dw/dt = −∇f(w)` (gradient *flow*). Continuous-time analysis of this ODE is how researchers prove convergence rates. The training loop is Euler's method applied to gradient flow.
- **Simulation, physics engines, epidemiology, control systems**, all ODEs under the hood.

### Build it: solve an ODE numerically (Euler's method) and exactly

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# dN/dt = k*N, k = 0.5, N(0) = 10.  Exact solution: N(t) = 10 * e^(0.5 t)
k, N0 = 0.5, 10.0

# Euler's method: step forward using the LOCAL slope, N_{t+1} = N_t + dt * f(N_t).
# (This is the SAME idea as gradient descent - step along the derivative!)
dt, T = 0.1, 6.0
ts = np.arange(0, T + dt, dt)
N = np.zeros_like(ts); N[0] = N0
for i in range(1, len(ts)):
    N[i] = N[i-1] + dt * (k * N[i-1])      # take a small step along the slope

exact = N0 * np.exp(k * ts)
plt.figure(figsize=(8,5))
plt.plot(ts, exact, 'b-', label='exact: N₀eᵏᵗ')
plt.plot(ts, N, 'r--', label="Euler's method (numerical)")
plt.xlabel('t'); plt.ylabel('N(t)'); plt.legend()
plt.title("Solving dN/dt = kN - exact vs. stepping along the derivative")
plt.grid(alpha=0.3); plt.show()
print(f"At t={T}: Euler={N[-1]:.2f}, exact={exact[-1]:.2f}")
```

Euler's method *steps forward along the local slope*, the exact same "follow the derivative" move as gradient descent, just applied to time instead of weight space. Smaller `dt` → closer to exact (try `dt=0.01`). This connects the dots: **gradient descent, Euler's method, and the limit definition of the derivative are all the same idea, locally, a curve looks like its tangent line, so step along the tangent.**

## Mini-project - gradient descent from scratch on a multivariable function, and what training a model really is

Let's build a complete gradient-descent optimizer from scratch, minimize a genuine 2D function with a curved landscape, visualize the path, and then make the connection to model training *explicit* by fitting a line via the exact same loop.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# =========================================================================
# PART 1: Generic gradient descent on a non-trivial 2D surface
# Himmelblau-ish bowl with an off-center minimum and anisotropic curvature.
# =========================================================================
def f(w):
    x, y = w
    return (x - 3)**2 + 5*(y + 1)**2 + 2*x*y      # the cross term tilts the bowl

def grad(w):
    x, y = w
    # ∂f/∂x = 2(x-3) + 2y ;  ∂f/∂y = 10(y+1) + 2x   - derived by hand
    return np.array([2*(x - 3) + 2*y,
                     10*(y + 1) + 2*x])

def gradient_descent(grad_fn, w0, eta=0.02, n_steps=200, tol=1e-8):
    """Vanilla gradient descent - the foundation of all model training."""
    w = np.array(w0, dtype=float)
    path = [w.copy()]
    for step in range(n_steps):
        g = grad_fn(w)
        w_new = w - eta * g                 # THE update rule: step against the gradient
        path.append(w_new.copy())
        if np.linalg.norm(w_new - w) < tol: # converged: barely moving anymore
            print(f"Converged after {step+1} steps")
            break
        w = w_new
    return w, np.array(path)

w_star, path = gradient_descent(grad, w0=[-2.0, 4.0], eta=0.03, n_steps=300)
print(f"Found minimum at w = {w_star},  f = {f(w_star):.6f}")

# Visualize the descent over the contour map
xs = np.linspace(-4, 6, 300); ys = np.linspace(-5, 5, 300)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, ys)
Z = (X - 3)**2 + 5*(Y + 1)**2 + 2*X*Y
plt.figure(figsize=(9, 7))
plt.contour(X, Y, Z, levels=30, cmap='viridis', alpha=0.6)
plt.plot(path[:,0], path[:,1], 'r.-', markersize=4, label='descent path')
plt.scatter(*w_star, color='red', marker='*', s=200, zorder=5, label='minimum')
plt.scatter(*path[0], color='black', s=80, zorder=5, label='start')
plt.xlabel('w₁'); plt.ylabel('w₂'); plt.legend()
plt.title('Gradient descent from scratch on a tilted bowl')
plt.axis('equal'); plt.show()

# =========================================================================
# PART 2: The PUNCHLINE - training a model IS this exact loop.
# Fit a line y = m*x + b to noisy data by minimizing mean squared error.
# The "weights" are now (m, b); the "loss surface" is the MSE.
# =========================================================================
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x_data = np.linspace(0, 10, 50)
y_data = 2.5 * x_data + 1.0 + rng.normal(0, 2, 50)   # true m=2.5, b=1.0, plus noise

def mse_grad(params):
    """Gradient of MSE w.r.t. (m, b) - derived with the chain rule.
       L = (1/N) Σ (m*x + b - y)²
       ∂L/∂m = (2/N) Σ (m*x + b - y)*x
       ∂L/∂b = (2/N) Σ (m*x + b - y)            """
    m, b = params
    err = (m * x_data + b) - y_data              # the prediction error
    dm = (2 / len(x_data)) * np.sum(err * x_data)
    db = (2 / len(x_data)) * np.sum(err)
    return np.array([dm, db])

(m_fit, b_fit), loss_path = gradient_descent(mse_grad, w0=[0.0, 0.0],
                                             eta=0.01, n_steps=2000)
print(f"\nFitted line: y = {m_fit:.3f}x + {b_fit:.3f}  (true: 2.5x + 1.0)")

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
plt.scatter(x_data, y_data, alpha=0.6, label='noisy data')
plt.plot(x_data, m_fit * x_data + b_fit, 'r-', lw=2, label='gradient-descent fit')
plt.xlabel('x'); plt.ylabel('y'); plt.legend()
plt.title('Linear regression trained by hand-rolled gradient descent')
plt.grid(alpha=0.3); plt.show()
```

**The lesson, and it's the whole reason calculus matters in CS.** Parts 1 and 2 use the *identical* `gradient_descent` function. In Part 1 you minimized an abstract math function; in Part 2 you trained a machine-learning model. They are the same operation. A model's parameters are the input `w`; its loss is the function `f`; "training" is gradient descent finding the `w` that minimizes the loss. Swap in a million-parameter neural net and a non-convex loss, add momentum and a learning-rate schedule (14.2), and you have modern deep learning, but the beating heart is the loop you just wrote: **compute the gradient, step against it, repeat.** Every formula derived in this chapter, the limit, the chain rule, the gradient as steepest ascent, is load-bearing in that loop.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing `f'(x) = 0` "necessary" with "sufficient."** Zero slope is *necessary* for a minimum but not *sufficient*, it could be a max or saddle. Always check the second derivative (or just confirm the value dropped).
- **Forgetting the chain rule's inner derivative.** `d/dx sin(x²)` is `cos(x²)·2x`, NOT `cos(x²)`. Dropping the inner `g'(x)` is the #1 calculus error, and in backprop it means a wrong gradient and silently broken training.
- **Sign errors in gradient descent.** It's `w − η∇f` (minus, to go *downhill*). Using `+` makes the loss *climb*, your loss going up instead of down is almost always a sign flip.
- **Trusting too-small a step in finite differences.** `h = 1e-12` gives a *worse* numerical derivative than `h = 1e-6` because of floating-point cancellation. Sweet spot, not "as small as possible."
- **Mixing up `d` and `∂`.** Use `∂` (partial) when other variables are held fixed, `d` (total) when everything that depends on the variable is allowed to change. The multivariable chain rule is precisely about converting partials into a total derivative.
- **Assuming convexity.** Linear/logistic regression is convex (one global min); neural nets are not (many local minima, saddles everywhere). Don't expect a non-convex problem to converge to *the* answer, it converges to *an* answer.

## On the job / interview angle

- **"Explain backpropagation."** Answer in one breath: *it's the chain rule applied to a computational graph, computing `∂loss/∂weight` for every weight by multiplying local derivatives backward through the network and reusing shared subexpressions (dynamic programming).* Then offer to derive a single neuron's gradient. This answer signals you understand the math, not just the API.
- **"Why might training diverge / loss go to NaN?"** Learning rate too high (overshoot, relate to step size on the hillside), exploding gradients (chain-rule products blowing up in deep nets), or a sign error. You can reason about all three from calculus.
- **"What's a vanishing gradient?"** Chain rule multiplies many `<1` derivatives (sigmoid ≤ 0.25) through deep layers, driving early-layer gradients to ~0 so they stop learning. ReLU's derivative is 1 on the active path, which is why it enabled depth.
- **Gradient checking.** Mention that you verify analytic gradients against *central finite differences*, a concrete, senior-sounding debugging practice.
- **Convex vs. non-convex.** Knowing that convex problems have a unique global optimum (and why) signals real optimization literacy.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Estimate `3` vs. exact `2.667`, an overestimate (the parabola is convex, so straight-line tops sit *above* the curve). More subintervals shrink the error; Simpson's rule (parabola fit) would be *exact* here since `x²` is degree 2.
</details>

**7. (Challenge) Connect to backprop.** A tiny "network" computes `L = (σ(wx) − y)²` where `σ` is the sigmoid `σ(z) = 1/(1+e^{−z})` (note `σ'(z) = σ(z)(1−σ(z))`). Derive `∂L/∂w` using the chain rule. Identify each chain-rule factor.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Let `z = wx`, `a = σ(z)`, `L = (a − y)²`. Chain the derivatives backward:
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}}_{2(a-y)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}}_{\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}}_{x}.$$
So:
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 2(a - y)\cdot a(1-a)\cdot x,\quad\text{with } a = \sigma(wx).$$
Three factors, one per layer of the computation: loss-vs-activation, activation-vs-pre-activation (the sigmoid derivative), pre-activation-vs-weight. **This is one backprop step.** Stack many of these and you have a deep net, and you can see the sigmoid's `a(1−a) ≤ 0.25` factor that causes vanishing gradients (14.2).
</details>
