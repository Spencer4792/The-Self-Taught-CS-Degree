# Chapter 15.3 - Behavioral Interviews, Resume & the Job Search

> Goal: Land the offer, the half of the process that isn't writing code. By the end I can answer behavioral questions with crisp STAR stories, write a resume whose bullets actually get callbacks, run the job search like an operations problem (pipeline, referrals, tracking, follow-up), and negotiate a first offer without leaving money on the table. I'll use my *real* background, BYU CS grad, data/ML, telemetry & observability, NYX Finance, Anneava, internships, to build example bullets and stories I can actually use.

You can ace every coding round and still not get hired if the behavioral falls flat, the resume never gets read, or the search has no system behind it. This chapter is the unglamorous machinery that turns technical ability into a paycheck. I'm treating it the same way I treat code: with templates, examples, and a repeatable process.

## 📐 The behavioral interview (STAR)

The behavioral round answers one question for the interviewer: **"Will I want to work with this person, and can they tell a coherent story about their own work?"** They're probing for ownership, collaboration, communication, learning from failure, and self-awareness.

The tool is **STAR**:

```
S - SITUATION   set the scene briefly. Where, when, what was at stake. (1-2 sentences)
T - TASK        what was YOUR specific responsibility / the goal. (1 sentence)
A - ACTION      what YOU did, step by step. The bulk of the answer. Say "I", not "we".
R - RESULT      the outcome, quantified if at all possible. What you learned.
```

The most common failure is living in **S** and **T** forever and never getting to a concrete **A** and **R**. Interviewers want **80% Action and Result.** And say **"I"**, "we shipped it" hides your contribution; "I built the X that let us ship" shows it.

> 💼 Build a **story bank** of 6-8 stories from your real experience, each tagged with the themes it covers (conflict, failure, leadership, ambiguity, a hard technical problem, impact). Most behavioral questions are just different doors into the *same* stories. I draw mine from NYX Finance, Anneava, internships, and class projects.

### Worked STAR answers (using my real background)

**"Tell me about yourself."** (Not really STAR, it's a 60-90 second narrative arc: past → present → why this role.)

> "I'm a recent computer science grad from BYU. My strengths are in data and machine learning, I love taking a messy real-world problem, building the pipeline, and turning it into something people can actually use. Lately I've been building **NYX Finance**, a financial analytics product where I built the data pipeline and a real-time dashboard, and **Anneava** on the side. I've also worked on **telemetry and observability**, instrumenting systems with logging, metrics, and tracing so teams can actually see what's happening in production. What draws me to this role is that it combines the data/ML work I'm strongest at with the chance to ship in a real production environment at scale, which is exactly the direction I want to grow."

Note the arc: *who I am → proof (concrete projects) → why THIS company.* End every "tell me about yourself" by connecting to the specific role.

**"Tell me about a conflict with a teammate."**
> - **S:** "On a class team project, a teammate and I disagreed hard on architecture, he wanted to cram everything into one service, I wanted to separate the data pipeline from the API."
> - **T:** "We were blocked and the deadline was a week out; I needed us unblocked without bulldozing him."
> - **A:** "Instead of arguing in the abstract, I suggested we each write a one-page tradeoff sketch and walk through them. I made sure to genuinely steelman his version. Doing that, we realized his concern was just deployment complexity, which I addressed by showing how Docker Compose kept it simple. We agreed on the split design with his deployment approach."
> - **R:** "We shipped on time, and honestly the conversation made us work better together for the rest of the project. I learned that most 'conflicts' are really an unspoken concern, and surfacing it beats arguing positions."

**"Tell me about a failure."** (They want ownership + learning, *not* a humblebrag.)
> - **S:** "Early on NYX Finance, I pushed a change to the data pipeline that silently dropped a chunk of records because I'd assumed an upstream field was always present."
> - **T:** "I owned the pipeline, so the bad data on the dashboard was on me."
> - **A:** "I owned it immediately rather than hiding it, traced the root cause, backfilled the missing data, and, the real fix, added schema validation at ingestion plus a data-quality metric and alert so a drop like that would page me instead of sitting silent."
> - **R:** "Data integrity issues from that class dropped to basically zero, and it's why I'm now almost religious about validation at boundaries and observability. The failure is what made me good at the telemetry work."

**"Tell me about a hard bug."**
> - **S:** "On a service I'd instrumented, p99 latency would spike randomly with no obvious cause, CPU and memory looked fine."
> - **T:** "I needed to find the cause from production signals without being able to reproduce it locally."
> - **A:** "I leaned on the observability I'd built, correlated the latency metric with traces and found the spikes lined up with a specific downstream call. The traces showed it was retrying on timeout, multiplying the latency. I'd been looking at averages, which hid it; the *traces* made it obvious."
> - **R:** "I tuned the timeout and added a circuit breaker, and the p99 spikes disappeared. The lesson that stuck: averages lie, and good tracing turns an invisible bug into an obvious one."

**"Tell me about a time you showed leadership."** (Leadership ≠ a title, it's initiative.)
> - **S:** "On a project, no one had set up any kind of testing or CI, and we kept breaking each other's code."
> - **T:** "Nobody owned it, so I decided to."
> - **A:** "I set up GitHub Actions to run our tests on every PR, wrote the first batch of tests as examples, and walked the team through writing their own. I kept it lightweight so it didn't feel like bureaucracy."
> - **R:** "Broken-main incidents basically stopped, and the team started writing tests by default. I learned that leadership at my level is mostly *seeing a gap and quietly filling it* before anyone asks."

**"Why this company?"** (Requires homework, generic answers kill you.)
> "Two reasons. First, the work, [specific team/product] is doing [specific thing], and that's exactly the intersection of data/ML and production systems I've been building toward with NYX Finance. Second, [something real about their engineering culture, a blog post, their approach to mentorship for new grads, an open-source project]. I want my first role to be somewhere I'll get strong mentorship while doing work I actually care about, and that's what I see here."

> ⚠️ Behavioral red flags: badmouthing past teammates/employers; never saying "I" (hiding your contribution); a "failure" that's secretly a brag ("I work too hard"); no metrics in any result; rambling with no structure; a "why this company" that could be copy-pasted to any company.

### How to talk about your projects

When they say "walk me through a project," use a STAR-flavored arc: **what problem it solved → your role and the key technical decisions → a hard part and how you handled it → the result/what you learned.** Lead with the *why* (the problem), not a tech stack list. For NYX Finance I'd open with "it solves [problem] for [user]," then the pipeline-and-dashboard architecture, then the hardest decision and its tradeoff (this is your chance to show the 15.2 design thinking). Always be ready for "what would you do differently?", having a thoughtful answer signals growth.

## 📐 Resume writing for entry-level SWE / data roles

A recruiter spends **~6-10 seconds** on the first pass. The resume's only job is to earn the next conversation. For entry-level: **one page, no exceptions.**

### The bullet formula

Every bullet: **Action verb + what you built/did (with the tech) + impact/result (quantified).**

```
[Strong verb] [what + the technology] [measurable impact]
```

Compare:

> ❌ "Worked on a data pipeline for a finance app."
> ✅ "Built a Python data pipeline (Pandas, PostgreSQL) ingesting ~1M financial records/day, cutting dashboard load time from 8s to under 1s by adding caching and indexing."

The second names the **action** (built), the **tech** (Python, Pandas, Postgres), and the **impact with numbers** (1M/day, 8s→1s). Numbers don't have to be huge, they have to be *concrete*. Even "reduced manual work from ~2 hours/week to near zero" beats a vague claim.

### Strong example bullets from my real background

**Projects:**
- "Built **NYX Finance**, a financial analytics platform (Python, FastAPI, PostgreSQL, React): designed the ingestion pipeline and a real-time dashboard serving sub-second queries over ~1M+ records via Redis caching and indexed queries."
- "Engineered **Anneava** end-to-end (Python/JS, Docker, CI/CD): containerized the stack and set up GitHub Actions for automated testing and deploys, taking releases from manual to push-button."
- "Designed and deployed an **observability layer** (metrics, structured logging, distributed tracing) that cut mean time-to-diagnose of production latency issues by surfacing a downstream-retry bottleneck previously invisible in averages."

**Internship / experience:**
- "Instrumented production services with **telemetry** (Grafana/Prometheus-style metrics + tracing), defining SLIs/SLOs and alerts that reduced undetected incidents."
- "Built a **machine learning model** (scikit-learn) for [task], improving [metric] from X to Y over the baseline, and shipped it behind a FastAPI endpoint."

**Education:**
- "B.S. Computer Science, Brigham Young University, relevant coursework: algorithms, databases, machine learning, operating systems."

### What to cut (especially as a new grad)

> ⚠️ Cut these aggressively:
> - **An "Objective" statement** ("Seeking a challenging role..."), dead weight; replace with a tight 2-line summary or nothing.
> - **High school** anything (once you have a degree).
> - **"Proficient in Microsoft Word"** and other non-skills.
> - **A skills bar/rating** ("Python ████░ 80%"), meaningless and amateurish.
> - **Soft-skill claims with no evidence** ("team player," "hard worker"), show, don't tell.
> - **Unrelated jobs** unless you can frame transferable skill, or you need to show work ethic with limited experience. If you keep one, give it a quantified bullet.
> - **A photo, age, full address**, city/state is plenty.

### ATS basics

Most applications go through an **Applicant Tracking System** that parses your resume before a human sees it. To survive it:
- **Use a clean single-column layout.** Fancy multi-column/graphic templates confuse parsers, your bullets can get scrambled or dropped.
- **Standard section headers**: Experience, Projects, Skills, Education.
- **Mirror keywords from the job description** *honestly*, if it says "REST APIs" and you built them, say "REST APIs," not "web endpoints." This is tailoring, not lying.
- **Submit a PDF** (unless they demand .docx), with selectable text (not an image/scan).
- **Spell out and abbreviate** key terms once ("CI/CD (continuous integration/deployment)") so either keyword matches.

### Tailoring (the highest-ROI 10 minutes)

Don't send the identical resume everywhere. For each role: read the job description, identify its top 3-5 required skills, and make sure those are visible and near the top of your resume, reorder bullets, swap which projects you emphasize, mirror their vocabulary. A data role gets your ML/pipeline bullets up top; a backend role gets FastAPI/Postgres/Docker up top. Same content, re-prioritized.

## 📐 Online presence: LinkedIn, GitHub, portfolio

Recruiters *will* look you up. Make them find proof.

- **LinkedIn:** clear headline ("CS Grad | Software Engineer | Data & ML"), a summary that's basically your "tell me about yourself," your projects and experience mirroring the resume, and a real photo. Turn on **"Open to Work."** Connect with recruiters and engineers at target companies.
- **GitHub:** this is your *evidence locker*. Pin your 3-4 best repos (NYX Finance, Anneava, a capstone). Each needs a real **README** (next chapter goes deep on this). Clean up: no half-finished tutorial clones pinned, no committed secrets, sensible commit messages (Part 11). A green-ish contribution graph signals you actually code.
- **Portfolio site (optional but strong for full-stack/data):** a simple one-pager linking your projects, with a short writeup + live demo + repo for each. It doubles as a project itself.

> 💼 The combo that converts: a resume bullet says "Built NYX Finance," your GitHub has the repo with a great README and a live demo link, and your LinkedIn tells the same story. Consistency across all three reads as *credible*.

## 📐 The job search strategy (run it like operations)

The job search is a **pipeline with conversion rates**, not a single lottery ticket. Treat it like one.

### Where to apply (in order of payoff)

1. **Referrals**, by far the highest conversion. A referral can be 5-10x more likely to get an interview than a cold application. Spend disproportionate effort here.
2. **New-grad / early-career pipelines**, most big companies have dedicated "University" or "New Grad" portals that open in cycles (often Aug-Nov for the next year). Apply the *day* they open; new-grad reqs fill fast.
3. **Company career pages directly**, apply on the company's own site, not just aggregators.
4. **Job boards**, LinkedIn, and curated lists (e.g., the community-maintained new-grad GitHub repos). Use them to *find* roles, then apply direct + seek a referral.

### Referrals - and how to actually ask

You don't need to be close friends. Most engineers are happy to refer a candidate who looks real. The ask:

> **Cold referral request (to a 2nd-degree connection or alum):**
> "Hi [Name], I'm a recent BYU CS grad and I noticed you're an engineer at [Company]. I'm really interested in the [specific role/team] there, it lines up with the data/ML and full-stack work I've been doing (I built a finance analytics platform called NYX Finance). Would you be open to referring me, or pointing me to the right person? Happy to send my resume and a short blurb to make it easy. Either way, thanks for reading!"

Make it **easy to say yes**: attach the resume, the exact job link, and a 2-3 sentence blurb they can paste. Always offer an out ("no worries if not").

### Recruiter outreach template

> "Hi [Name], I saw you recruit for early-career engineering roles at [Company]. I'm a recent CS grad focused on data/ML and full-stack development (recently built NYX Finance, a finance analytics platform). I'd love to be considered for your new-grad SWE/data openings, resume attached. Is there a specific req you'd recommend I apply to? Thanks so much!"

### Application tracking

Track every application or it becomes chaos by week 3. (Full schema in the mini-project.) Knowing your *conversion rates* tells you what to fix: lots of applications, no callbacks → fix the resume / get more referrals. Lots of phone screens, no onsites → practice the patterns (15.1). Onsites, no offers → mock more / work on behavioral.

### Timelines & volume (set expectations)

- New-grad hiring is **seasonal**, heaviest in the fall for the following summer/year. Apply early in the cycle.
- It's a **numbers game with a system**: expect to send many applications. A rough healthy funnel might be ~5-10% of applications → screen, ~30-50% of screens → onsite, ~20-40% of onsites → offer. Referrals dramatically improve the top of the funnel.
- The whole process per company often runs **3-6 weeks** screen-to-offer. Keep many in flight in parallel so a slow process or rejection doesn't stall you.

### Negotiation basics for a first offer

Yes, you can and should negotiate, *politely*, even as a new grad. The principles:

- **Never give a number first if you can avoid it.** "I'd love to learn more about the role; I'm sure we can find a number that works, what range did you have in mind?" If pressed, give a researched range (use levels.fyi / Glassdoor for that company + level + location).
- **Get the offer in writing**, then ask for time to consider ("Could I have a few days to think it over?"). Don't accept on the spot.
- **Have use = a competing offer or a strong process elsewhere.** Even "I'm in final rounds with another company" is real use.
- **The ask, scripted:** "I'm really excited about this offer and the team. Based on my research and other conversations I'm having, would you be able to move the base to $X?" Be specific, warm, and brief.
- **Negotiate the whole package**, not just base: sign-on bonus, equity, start date, relocation. Sign-on is often the easiest lever for new grads.
- **Stay gracious.** The worst case of a polite ask is "no," and the relationship is intact. Companies expect it; not asking often just leaves money on the table.

### Handling rejection & keeping momentum

Rejection is the median outcome of any single application, it is *not* a verdict on you. How I keep momentum:
- **Volume insulates you.** With 15 processes in flight, one rejection is noise. With one, it's devastating. Keep the pipeline full.
- **Separate "no" from "not yet."** Ask for feedback (sometimes you get it). Use it.
- **Keep practicing during the search**, every week do problems (15.1) and a mock. Skills decay between interviews otherwise.
- **Track your own progress**, not just outcomes. "I did 12 mocks and improved my behavioral answers" is real progress even before an offer lands.
- **Protect your morale**, it's a months-long process; consistency beats intensity. A sustainable weekly routine (below) wins.

## 🧪 Mini-project - A job-search operating system

Two parts: a tracker schema and a weekly routine. Run it like a system, not vibes.

### Part A - The application tracker (spreadsheet schema)

Create a spreadsheet (or Notion DB) with these columns:

```
Company | Role | Source(referral/portal/board) | Referrer | Date applied |
Stage(applied/screen/OA/onsite/offer/rejected) | Next action | Next-action date |
Contacts(recruiter/referrer) | Salary range | Notes(prep, what went well) | Outcome
```

Add a small **funnel dashboard** that counts rows by Stage so you can see your conversion rates at a glance:

```
Applied: 42   Screens: 6   OAs: 4   Onsites: 2   Offers: 1   Rejected: 9   Active: 23
=> screen rate 14%  |  onsite rate from screen 33%  |  ...
```

The numbers tell you exactly which stage to fix.

### Part B - The weekly routine

A sustainable cadence (~10-12 hrs/week) that keeps every part of the pipeline alive:

```
MON  Find & apply: 5-8 tailored applications + 2 referral asks. (~2 hrs)
TUE  Coding practice: 3-4 problems, by pattern (Ch 15.1). (~1.5 hrs)
WED  Coding practice + 1 mock interview. (~2 hrs)
THU  System design (Ch 15.2) + behavioral story polishing. (~1.5 hrs)
FRI  Follow-ups: thank-you notes, nudge stale referrals, update tracker. (~1 hr)
SAT  Project work / GitHub polish (Ch 15.4) - keeps your evidence fresh. (~2 hrs)
SUN  Rest + review the week's funnel numbers, plan next week. (~30 min)
```

### Part C - The thank-you note (send within 24h of every interview)

> "Hi [Name], thank you for taking the time to talk with me today. I really enjoyed digging into [specific thing you discussed, e.g., how your team handles the data pipeline], it's exactly the kind of work I'm excited about. It reinforced my interest in the [role]. Please let me know if there's anything else I can provide. Thanks again!"

Reference something *specific* from the conversation, it proves you were present and it's memorable.

### Part D - Resume-bullet rewrite exercise (before/after)

Take five weak bullets from your current resume and rewrite each with the **action + tech + impact** formula. Worked examples:

```
BEFORE: "Helped build a dashboard for a finance app."
AFTER:  "Built a real-time analytics dashboard (React, FastAPI) for NYX Finance,
         serving sub-second queries over 1M+ records via Redis caching + indexing."

BEFORE: "Did some machine learning for a project."
AFTER:  "Trained and deployed a scikit-learn model for [task], improving [metric]
         15% over baseline, served behind a FastAPI endpoint."

BEFORE: "Used Docker and set up deployment."
AFTER:  "Containerized the Anneava stack with Docker and built a GitHub Actions
         CI/CD pipeline, turning a manual multi-step release into a one-click deploy."

BEFORE: "Worked on monitoring."
AFTER:  "Instrumented services with metrics, logs, and tracing; defined SLIs/SLOs and
         alerts that caught a downstream-retry bottleneck invisible in averages."

BEFORE: "Group project using a database."
AFTER:  "Designed a normalized PostgreSQL schema with indexed queries for a [X]
         app, cutting average query latency from ~600ms to under 50ms."
```

Do all five for *your* resume. That single exercise often does more for callback rate than another week of LeetCode.
